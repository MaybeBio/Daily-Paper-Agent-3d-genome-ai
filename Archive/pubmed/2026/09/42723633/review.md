## Review setup
- **Input scope** Abstract only
- **Assessment boundary** Claims and evidence as presented in the abstract; no full text, figures, tables, or supplementary materials were provided
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present DNABERT-Enhancer, a fine-tuned DNABERT model for predicting enhancers in the human genome. They report benchmark performance (88.05% accuracy, MCC 76%), genome-wide predictions (1,684,595 enhancer regions covering 26.65% of the genome), and integrative variant effect analyses identifying 2,681 loss-of-function and 1,917 gain-of-function enhancer variants, plus 4,057 candidate de novo enhancers. Resources are made available via GitHub, a web application, and Zenodo.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract text only; no methods, figures, tables, or supplementary data were provided
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Full methods, benchmark dataset construction details, model architecture and hyperparameters, independent dataset composition, variant effect analysis methodology, statistical thresholds, validation procedures, and all quantitative results beyond those stated in the abstract

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract describes a potentially useful application of a pre-trained genomic language model to enhancer prediction, with substantial genome-wide annotations and variant effect predictions. However, the abstract alone provides insufficient methodological detail to evaluate the validity of the performance claims, the appropriateness of the benchmark design, or the biological meaning of the variant effect predictions. The genome-wide coverage figure of 26.65% raises questions about the specificity of the model that cannot be resolved from the supplied material. The work may be of interest to computational genomics and regulatory biology communities, but the case is not established from the abstract alone.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on regulatory element prediction and genome annotation; researchers studying enhancer biology and cis-regulatory variation; clinical genomics groups interested in interpreting non-coding variants; developers of genomic language models seeking benchmarks and applications.
- **Major strengths** The use of a pre-trained genomic language model (DNABERT) is timely and aligns with current trends in regulatory genomics. The authors provide a public benchmark dataset and make models and predictions freely available, which supports reproducibility and community use. The integration of enhancer prediction with variant effect analysis addresses a practical need in genome interpretation.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The model achieved 88.05% accuracy and a Matthews correlation coefficient of 76% on an independent dataset.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Results section; location not provided
  - **Concern** The abstract does not describe the composition of the independent dataset, how it was selected, whether it is balanced, or how it relates to the training data. Without this information, the reported accuracy and MCC cannot be interpreted. In particular, if the independent dataset has a different class balance or sequence length distribution than the training data, the performance metrics may not reflect generalizable performance.
  - **Why it matters** Performance claims on an independent dataset are central to the paper's contribution. If the dataset is not truly independent or is not representative of the target application, the reported metrics may overstate the model's utility. The MCC of 76% is high for enhancer prediction and warrants scrutiny of the evaluation protocol.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a detailed description of the independent dataset, including source, size, class balance, sequence length, and how it was held out from training and validation. Report performance stratified by sequence length and genomic context. If possible, compare against a baseline model on the same independent dataset.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** Genome-wide application identified 1,684,595 enhancer regions covering 26.65% of the human genome.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Results section; location not provided
  - **Concern** The abstract reports that over a quarter of the human genome is predicted as enhancer. This is a very high fraction compared to typical estimates of enhancer content in the human genome, which are usually in the low single digits. The abstract does not explain the threshold used for genome-wide prediction, the length of predicted regions, or how overlapping predictions were merged. The high coverage suggests the model may have low specificity when applied genome-wide.
  - **Why it matters** If the genome-wide predictions are used as a resource for functional and clinical genomics, over-prediction could lead to a large number of false positives, undermining the utility of the resource. The coverage figure also raises questions about the calibration of the model's probability threshold.
  - **Resolution test** Report the precision-recall characteristics of the model on a genome-wide basis, describe the threshold selection procedure, and provide a comparison with existing enhancer annotations (e.g., ENCODE cCREs, FANTOM5) to assess overlap and specificity. Justify the biological plausibility of the 26.65% coverage figure.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** Integrative analyses with DNABERT-based transcription factor models identified 2,681 loss-of-function and 1,917 gain-of-function enhancer variants, affecting 1,623 and 1,247 ENCODE-cCRE enhancers, respectively, and 4,057 candidate de novo enhancers created by 5,464 gain-of-function variants.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Results section; location not provided
  - **Concern** The abstract does not describe the methodology for variant effect prediction. It is unclear how the DNABERT-based transcription factor models were used, what variants were considered, how statistical significance was defined, and what thresholds were applied. The distinction between loss-of-function and gain-of-function effects is not explained, and the relationship between variant effects and enhancer function is not defined.
  - **Why it matters** Variant effect predictions are a major claimed contribution. Without methodological detail, these numbers cannot be evaluated or reproduced. The biological interpretation of these variants depends on the validity of the underlying models and the statistical framework.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a detailed description of the variant effect prediction pipeline, including input variant sets, model architecture, scoring function, statistical testing procedure, multiple testing correction, and thresholds. Report validation of predicted variant effects against experimental data (e.g., MPRA, STARR-seq) if available.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (independent dataset description), R1-M2 (genome-wide coverage justification), R1-M3 (variant effect methodology)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The application of DNABERT to enhancer prediction is not entirely novel, as similar language model approaches have been applied to regulatory genomics, but the specific focus on enhancer prediction and variant effects may offer incremental novelty. Scientific importance: The potential resource value is significant, but the high genome-wide coverage and lack of methodological detail undermine confidence in the importance of the claims. Interdisciplinary readership: The work is primarily of interest to computational biologists and genomics researchers; the abstract does not clearly communicate relevance to clinical or experimental audiences. Technical soundness: Cannot be assessed from the abstract alone; the reported metrics and genome-wide coverage raise concerns that require full methods to resolve. Readability for nonspecialists: The abstract is dense with field-specific terms and lacks context for a broader audience.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The abstract presents potentially useful resources, but the technical claims cannot be evaluated without full methods and validation. A revised manuscript with detailed methodology, dataset descriptions, and validation against external resources would be needed to assess whether the case can be made.
