# PSHIC reveals allele-specific chromatin organization by phasing-free structural modeling


## Abstract
  Resolving the distinct 3D chromatin architectures of parental chromosomes is critical for understanding diploid genome function, but many haplotype-resolved Hi-C contact-map methods remain constrained by their reliance on phased single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). This dependency treats reconstruction as a simple tag-and-sort problem, leading to massive data loss, extreme fragility to phasing errors, and inapplicability in SNP-sparse contexts. We introduce PSHIC, a computational framework that reveals this hidden chromatin organization by employing phasing-free structural modeling, directly from aggregated Hi-C data. PSHIC models chromosomes as continuous manifolds and solves the inverse problem of finding two homolog-specific structures whose combined contacts best explain the observed data. Comprehensive simulations across varying sequencing depths, SNP densities, and phasing error rates demonstrate that PSHIC consistently outperforms established methods, achieving superior accuracy (SCC ≥ 0.987), loop detection (F1≥ 0.952), and structural fidelity (DER ≤ 0.04). Applications to real human and mouse Hi-C datasets show that PSHIC recovers known allele-associated features in X-chromosome inactivation, genomic imprinting, and regulatory networks. Results align with orthogonal experimental approaches including 3D DNA-FISH, 4C-seq, and ChIA-PET. PSHIC democratizes diploid chromatin analysis by enabling robust reconstruction in non-model organisms and other SNP-limited or poorly phased contexts.


## Introduction
  High-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) has fundamentally transformed our understanding of three-dimensional genome organization by revealing the hierarchical architecture of chromatin, including topologically associating domains (TADs), CTCF-mediated loop structures, compartmentalization patterns, and nuclear territorial organization1–8. These discoveries have established the critical link between spatial genome organization and cellular processes, from transcriptional regulation to developmental programming.

  However, conventional Hi-C analyses aggregate chromatin interactions from both parental chromosomes, masking allele-specific organizational features that are fundamental to diploid genome function. This analytical limitation obscures critical biological phenomena, including imprinted chromatin loops, allele-specific topological domains and regulatory networks9,10. Such allele-specific structures arise through multiple mechanisms: epigenetic modifications that affect only one homolog, sequence variants that disrupt architectural protein binding sites, and genomic imprinting that silences entire chromosomal regions on a parent-specific basis. The functional consequences of these asymmetric organizations are profound, as aberrant allele-specific chromatin architectures contribute to developmental disorders, cancer progression, and imprinting-related pathologies11,12.

  Recent advances in 3D genome analysis have produced a diverse toolkit for reconstructing chromatin organization from bulk Hi-C data. Population-level 3D genome modeling methods infer consensus structures or structural ensembles that summarize bulk interaction patterns and enable characterization of nuclear microenvironments, chromatin compaction, and compartmentalization13,14. However, most such approaches either model a single structure per chromosome or implicitly assume homologs share similar conformations, and therefore do not explicitly resolve homolog-specific organization from diploid, allele-ambiguous Hi-C. When the goal is to recover homolog-specific (allele-specific) organization from bulk Hi-C, prior work has pursued two complementary strategies. One strategy performs phasing-free diploid structural inference, aiming to reconstruct two homolog-specific 3D conformations directly from allele-ambiguous contact counts by explicitly modeling allelic uncertainty in a generative count model. Diploid-PASTIS extends the PASTIS Poisson count-to-distance framework to diploid genomes by treating each observed count as a sum over the four possible homolog-to-homolog interactions, together with polymer connectivity and homolog-separation constraints15. While such methods yield homolog-resolved 3D structures, many downstream allele-specific analyses require allele-specific contact maps, motivating approaches that reconstruct maternal and paternal contact matrices directly from bulk Hi-C. A second strategy reconstructs allele-specific Hi-C contact matrices by leveraging phased single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to partition reads by parental origin, producing separate maternal and paternal contact maps for downstream allele-specific analysis. These methods classify Hi-C read pairs based on their overlap with phased SNPs: both-end allele-certain reads, where both ends can be unambiguously assigned to specific homologs; one-end allele-ambiguous reads, where only one end overlaps informative SNPs; and both-end allele-ambiguous reads, where neither end provides haplotype information16. Existing approaches fall into three categories depending on how these read types are used. Allele-certain methods utilize only reads with unambiguous haplotype assignments, providing high confidence but severely limited data utilization5,17. Mate-rescue strategies expand the usable data by inferring haplotype assignments for ambiguous reads based on their confidently assigned partners, though substantial data loss persists18–20. Probabilistic frameworks attempt to incorporate all available data through statistical inference, with methods like ASHIC demonstrating enhanced Hi-C signal strength by modeling contact probabilities using all read categories in a hierarchical Bayesian framework21. Nevertheless, these approaches face three fundamental constraints that severely limit their broader applicability.

  Most SNP-driven haplotype-resolved contact-map reconstruction methods treat diploid Hi-C map reconstruction as a simple tag-and-sort task rather than a structural modeling problem, using SNPs as arbitrary tags to partition reads into maternal and paternal groups while operating blind to chromosomal physics and geometry. This paradigm lacks understanding of manifold structures, genomic continuity, or polymer behavior. Second, SNP-dependent methods suffer from massive data loss: in datasets with an average SNP density of one variant per kilobase, up to 94.1% of Hi-C read pairs are both-end allele-ambiguous and either discarded or used with high uncertainty, resulting in sparse contact matrices with severely reduced signal-to-noise ratios. Even probabilistic models that attempt to rescue ambiguous reads remain fundamentally anchored by the small fraction of allele-certain reads, where the entire statistical inference framework degrades significantly if this anchor information is sparse or contains errors. Finally, these methods create substantial infrastructure barriers by requiring high-quality phased SNP data that remains scarce outside well-studied model organisms, exhibit extreme sensitivity to unavoidable phasing errors (0.05–2%22,23), and exclude many important biological contexts, including non-model organisms and low-heterozygosity samples.

  Here, we introduce PSHIC, a computational framework that reveals allele-specific chromatin organization by phasing-free structural modeling. Rather than attempting to tag and sort individual reads based on genetic markers–a local decision-making process blind to global chromosome structure–PSHIC reveals the underlying allele-specific organization by adopting a fundamentally different approach grounded in the physical properties of chromatin. Our method models chromosomes as continuous manifolds in low-dimensional space and formulates diploid reconstruction as an inverse problem: finding the two spatial structures whose combined predicted contacts optimally explain the observed aggregate data. This modeling framework enables PSHIC to leverage global information by using the entire contact matrix as a single constraint, where every genomic bin’s position is influenced by its relationship to all other bins rather than local SNP-based decisions. The method incorporates inherent denoising through smooth manifold assumptions and structural priors encoded in regularization terms for genomic continuity and spatial occupancy. Through comprehensive simulation studies across varying sequencing depths, SNP densities, and phasing error rates, we demonstrate that PSHIC consistently outperforms existing methods while maintaining stability under challenging experimental conditions. We further validate PSHIC’s accuracy using real Hi-C datasets from mouse and human, successfully reconstructing allele-specific chromatin organization in diverse biological contexts, including X-chromosome inactivation, genomic imprinting at the Igf2/H19 locus, and RNA polymerase II-mediated regulatory networks, with findings consistent with orthogonal experimental approaches, including 3D DNA-FISH, 4C-seq, and phased ChIA-PET data. In addition, PSHIC reconstructs single-cell diploid contact maps without phased SNPs that exhibit stronger signal and more delineated TAD boundaries than SNP-based approaches.


## Results

### Diploid Hi-C map Reconstruction for in silico datasets
  PSHIC was benchmarked against five existing methods, including ASHIC-PM and ASHIC-ZIPM (implemented in ASHIC v0.1.0), Diploid-PASTIS (implemented in PASTIS v0.3.3), and allele-certain and mate-rescue (implemented using HiC-Pro v3.1.0), using simulated diploid Hi-C datasets with known ground truth. Because all methods share the same power-law distance-decay framework (see “Methods”), these experiments isolate differences in each method’s capacity for diploid signal decomposition and noise robustness. We evaluated performance across sequencing depth, SNP density, and SNP phasing error rate using SCC, F1 score, and distance error rate (DER), with 10 replicate datasets generated for each setting (Fig. 2C). We also assessed the effect of embedding dimension k, observing that performance stabilized at k ≥ 4, and therefore used k = 4 in all simulations (Fig. 2A). The NSR analysis confirmed that simulated data became cleaner with increasing sequencing depth (Fig. 2B).


#### PSHIC exhibits robustness at low sequencing depth
  Across varying sequencing depths, PSHIC consistently achieved the best overall performance among all evaluated methods (Fig. 2C). For contact-map reconstruction, PSHIC maintained high SCC scores of 0.989 − 0.994 across all sequencing depths, indicating strong agreement between the reconstructed and ground-truth diploid contact maps. Among competing methods, ASHIC-ZIPM achieved the closest performance with SCC scores of 0.906 − 0.936, followed by ASHIC-PM (0.845 − 0.879), while mate-rescue and allele-certain showed stronger sequencing-depth dependence, increasing from 0.851 to 0.930 and from 0.634 to 0.752, respectively, as coverage increased. Similar trends were observed for chromatin loop recovery: PSHIC maintained consistently high F1 scores of 0.959 − 0.967, whereas ASHIC-ZIPM, ASHIC-PM, mate-rescue, and allele-certain achieved F1 ranges of 0.904 − 0.917, 0.828 − 0.838, 0.688 − 0.774, and 0.163 − 0.275, respectively. For spatial genome structure reconstruction, PSHIC produced the lowest distance error rates (DER = 0.010 − 0.023), compared with 0.064 − 0.084 for ASHIC-ZIPM and 0.163 − 0.184 for ASHIC-PM. Diploid-PASTIS served as an additional structure-inference baseline, showing relatively stable SCC, F1, and DER values across sequencing-depth settings. Together, these results demonstrate that PSHIC preserves contact-map fidelity, loop-detection accuracy, and structural accuracy under reduced sequencing depth. #### PSHIC is insensitive to SNP phasing errors
  Many species lack phased SNP reference genomes, necessitating the application of haplotype assembly tools to phase polymorphic variants required by current diploid Hi-C map reconstruction methods. These methods rely extensively on accurate allele-specific read assignment. Even tiny phasing error rates (0.05 − 2%) can significantly compromise performance by misallocating reads between parental chromosomes, distorting allele-specific topological features, and creating spurious interactions that confound biological interpretation. It is therefore critical to develop reconstruction algorithms that maintain fidelity despite inevitable phasing imperfections, especially for applications in non-model organisms and individual-specific analysis. Therefore, we systematically introduced controlled error rates and quantified how reconstruction accuracy degrades as a function of phasing error rate across different methods.

  PSHIC consistently maintained the highest diploid map reconstruction accuracy with SCC = 0.989 ± 0.004 across all phasing error rates, while SNP-dependent methods showed substantial performance degradation with even minimal errors. At just 0.05% phasing error rate, ASHIC-PM declined from 0.877 to 0.543, ASHIC-ZIPM from 0.936 to 0.514, allele-certain from 0.752 to 0.311, and mate-rescue from 0.930 to 0.573. PASTIS remained unchanged across phasing-error settings, with SCC scores around 0.225, as expected for a method that does not rely on phased SNP assignment. Interestingly, at higher error rates (1% − 2%), these SNP-dependent methods showed slight performance stabilization or even modest improvements with reduced variability across replicates. This counterintuitive phenomenon likely stems from statistical properties of error distribution; at low error rates, sparse and potentially clustered phasing errors create localized distortions with asymmetric impacts on chromosome reconstruction, while at higher rates, the more uniform distribution of errors creates a more predictable noise pattern that allows methods to achieve more consistent results with smaller performance variations. Similar patterns emerged in F1 scores for loop detection, with PSHIC maintaining the highest and most stable performance F1 = 0.959 ± 0.004 across all phasing error rates. However, with increasing errors, all SNP-dependent methods exhibited drastic decreases in F1 scores, with large variability at low phasing error rates (ξ = 0.05%). ASHIC-ZIPM’s F1 score dropped from 0.917 to 0.529, and fluctuated around 0.435 − 0.475 when ξ exceeded 0.5%. Similarly, ASHIC-PM declined from 0.829 to 0.533 at 0.05% phasing error and remained around 0.439 − 0.475 at higher error rates. Mate-rescue exhibited a comparable decline from 0.774 to 0.468, while allele-certain consistently demonstrated low F1 scores, declining from 0.275 to below 0.16 under phasing errors. The F1 score of Diploid-PASTIS changed minimally across phasing-error settings, remaining around 0.255. To further evaluate reconstruction quality, we also examined distance error rates across methods. PSHIC demonstrated exceptional precision with a consistently low error of 0.0104 ± 0.0006 regardless of phasing error rate. In contrast, at ξ = 0.0005, ASHIC-ZIPM’s distance error increased from 0.084 to 0.287, while ASHIC-PM’s rose from 0.184 to 0.290. As phasing errors increased to 0.5 − 2%, both methods showed similar stabilization patterns observed in other metrics, with errors fluctuating between 0.311 − 0.324 for ASHIC-PM and 0.305 − 0.309 for ASHIC-ZIPM, accompanied by decreasing variability at higher error rates. Diploid-PASTIS exhibited little variation in DER across phasing-error settings, with values remaining close to 0.443, consistent with its phasing-independent design (Fig. 2C).

  PSHIC was distinguished by its exceptional robustness to phasing errors. While SNP-dependent approaches exhibit performance degradation at even 0.05% error rates, PSHIC maintains consistent accuracy metrics across all simulated conditions. Notably, although Diploid-PASTIS is also phasing-free and therefore insensitive to SNP density and phasing errors, its absolute reconstruction accuracy was substantially lower than that of PSHIC, with much lower SCC and F1 scores and markedly higher DER. This large performance gap indicates that PSHIC’s advantage over Diploid-PASTIS is not merely direct contact-map generation, but accurate homolog-specific reconstruction from aggregate Hi-C data. This unique resilience stems from PSHIC’s fundamental algorithmic design that eliminates dependence on error-prone allele-specific information, thereby expanding diploid Hi-C analysis capabilities to broader biological contexts where high-quality phased genomes remain unavailable. ### PSHIC recognizes the bipartite structures of the mouse inactive X chromosome and captures the redistribution of long-range contacts in Dxz4-edited cells
  X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) in female mammals achieves dosage compensation by silencing one of the two X chromosomes. The inactive X chromosome (Xi) features a bipartite structure with two superdomains characterized by frequent long-range contacts18. In the mouse species, Dxz4 in the hinge regions serves as a structural platform to establish the bipartite architectures necessary for effective allelic silencing. The orientation of Dxz4 influences the distribution of long-range contacts on the Xi, as demonstrated by Dxz4-edited cell lines20. To investigate the bipartite structure of the inactive X chromosome and the role of orientation-dependent Dxz4, we applied PSHIC to three published in situ DNase Hi-C datasets of mouse Patski cells. Specifically, we studied a clone from wild-type (WT) cells, a clone with a 44 kb inversion of Dxz4 (Inv-Dxz4), and a clone with a 44 kb deletion of Dxz4 (Del-Dxz4) at 1 Mb and 500 Kb resolution. To enable accurate reconstruction of the entire chromosome, we selected a higher embedding dimension of k = 32, which allows the model to capture a wider range of structural features and complex relationships such as cell-to-cell heterogeneity, epigenetic states, and chromatin accessibility.

  Figure 3A displays the reconstructed diploid Hi-C contact maps for WT, Inv-Dxz4, and Del-Dxz4 cell lines. The active X chromosome (Xa) maintains a consistent plaid pattern across the three datasets, reflecting alternating A/B compartment structures and confirming that Dxz4 modifications on the Xi do not affect the homologous Xa. Conversely, the Xi shows distinct structural responses to Dxz4 modifications. Both WT and Inv-Dxz4 cells preserve the characteristic bipartite architecture with enriched intra-domain and depleted inter-domain interactions, while Del-Dxz4 cells completely lose this organization, consistent with Dxz4’s established role in maintaining the Xi bipartite structure. Notably, Inv-Dxz4 cells exhibit altered long-range contact patterns despite retaining the bipartite structure. The inversion creates new domain-like interactions between Dxz4 and Firre, forming a contact hub absent in WT cells and shifting interaction preferences from telomeric to centromeric regions (Fig. 3G). This orientation-dependent rewiring reflects the directional nature of CTCF-mediated chromatin looping and demonstrates Dxz4’s dual function as both a structural anchor for Xi silencing and a directional mediator of long-range chromatin interactions. Consistent with this, CTCF ChIP-seq data (GSM2863715, GSM2863716, GSM2863717) from Bonora et al.20 confirm dense CTCF occupancy at the Dxz4 locus in WT cells, loss of binding upon Dxz4 deletion, and retained but slightly changed binding upon inversion (Fig. 3F). To highlight these structural changes, we generated differential Pearson-transformed contact maps comparing WT to Del-Dxz4 and Inv-Dxz4 (Fig. 3H). The Inv-Dxz4 map displays a blue band toward the centromere and a red band toward the telomere–hallmarks of directionally reversed long-range interactions caused by Dxz4 inversion, along with a slight telomeric shift of the bipartition boundary.

  To further dissect the structural changes of Dxz4 modification on Xi architecture, we analyzed principal component (PC) scores derived from the diploid Hi-C contact maps and computed pairwise Spearman correlations of PC1 across three conditions (Fig. 3B, C). The Xa maintains consistent PC1 profiles across all datasets, capturing characteristic A/B compartment patterning with high inter-sample correlations (WT Xa vs Del-Dxz4 Xa: ρ = 0.90; WT Xa vs Inv-Dxz4 Xa: ρ = 0.91). In contrast, the Xi exhibits substantial PC1 variation. The WT Xi displays a sharp PC1 sign switch at the hinge region, marking the boundary between the two superdomains. This switch is completely abolished in Del-Dxz4 Xi, consistent with bipartite architecture breakdown and resulting in low correlation with WT Xi (ρ = 0.30). The Inv-Dxz4 Xi retains a PC1 sign switch near the hinge, indicating preserved bipartition, yet shows reduced correlation with WT Xi (ρ = 0.84) compared to higher Xa correlations across datasets. This suggests that while the superdomain boundary persists, inversion fundamentally restructures the underlying contact distribution within the bipartite framework.

  To quantitatively evaluate Xi bipartite architecture, we computed the Bipartite Index (BI)24 across the X chromosome using reconstructed diploid contact maps (Fig. 3D). The BI measures superdomain separation by comparing intra-domain versus inter-domain contact strengths around the hinge region. Only the WT paternal and Inv-Dxz4 paternal chromosomes display strong BI peaks flanking the hinge, while the Del-Dxz4 paternal chromosome lacks these signals. All three Xa chromosomes show no pronounced BI peaks, confirming that bipartition is Xi-specific. The Inv-Dxz4 Xi exhibits a telomeric BI peak shift, further suggesting that Dxz4 inversion physically repositions the superdomain boundary in an allele-specific manner. Allele-resolved contact maps derived from allele-certain and mate-rescue strategies with phased SNP references also validated this observation.

  Taken together, our method successfully identified the formation and disruption of Xi bipartite structure in WT and Del-Dxz4 cells, respectively, while detecting subtle changes in long-range contact patterns in Inv-Dxz4 cells–all without requiring phased SNP references. These results demonstrate Dxz4’s dual function as both a structural anchor for Xi compartmentalization and a directional mediator of long-range chromatin interactions. Beyond the allele-resolved Patski system, we further applied PSHIC to an unphased human IMR90 Hi-C dataset (GSE63525)5 and observed haplotype-specific chromatin interaction differences around the DXZ4 locus (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Note 5), indicating that PSHIC can recover biologically meaningful homolog-specific structural variation from standard diploid Hi-C data without allele-resolved references. Collectively, these analyses across species, perturbation conditions, and data types highlight the generality and robustness of PSHIC for resolving homolog-specific chromatin organization.


### PSHIC decodes allele-specific chromatin organization of the mouse H19-Igf2 imprinted domain
  Genomic imprinting represents a critical epigenetic mechanism in which approximately 200 mammalian genes are expressed in a parent-of-origin-specific manner despite the diploid nature of mammalian genomes25. This mono-allelic expression is essential for normal embryonic development, with various human disorders such as Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, Silver-Russell syndrome, and certain types of cancer attributed to disruptions in parental specificity. Most imprinted genes are organized into approximately 20 chromosomal domains regulated by Imprinting Control Regions (ICRs) that function as differentially methylated regions (DMRs) with germline-acquired allelic DNA methylation imprints26. The H19/Igf2 locus is distinctive as one of only two evolutionarily conserved domains where the ICR is methylated on the paternal rather than the maternal allele, with this unique paternal ICR linked to allele-specific binding of the CTCF on the maternal chromosome27. The H19 DMR, located 2–4 kb towards the telomeric side from the H19 promoter, contains multiple binding sites, with CTCF binding to the unmethylated maternal H19 DMR creating an insulator that prevents interactions between the Igf2 promoter and shared enhancers while facilitating H19 expression; conversely, DNA methylation on the paternal chromosome inhibits CTCF binding, allowing Igf2 activation and H19 silencing25. This reciprocal regulation results in the paternally expressed insulin-like growth factor 2 (Igf2) and the maternally expressed non-coding RNA H19, underscoring the essential role of allelic CTCF binding in maintaining imprinting.

  In the H19/Igf2 imprinted region, chromatin architecture displays distinct configurations between parental alleles. Previous 4C-seq analysis revealed that the maternal H19 DMR preferentially interacts with four centromeric bi-allelic CTCF binding clusters oriented toward the H19 DMR, while the paternal chromosome exhibits increased interactions around and toward the 3’ of the H19 DMR27. Using diploid Hi-C contact maps of human GM12878 cells at 25 kb resolution, Rao et al. identified parent-specific chromatin loops between the H19/IGF2 cluster and a distal regulatory element termed the H19/IGF2 Distal Anchor Domain (HIDAD)5. The mouse HIDAD (mHIDAD) also demonstrates significant allele-specific interactions, with 3D DNA-FISH measurements showing shorter distances between H19 and mHIDAD specifically on the maternal chromosome. Further 4C-seq experiments confirmed that centromeric CTCF clusters (Syt8 US and Lsp1) form loops with the H19 DMR on the maternal chromosome, while on the paternal chromosome, these same elements show more interactions with distal CTCF sites telomeric to Igf227.

  Diverse methods have been employed to investigate chromatin organization at the H19/Igf2 locus, including 3D DNA-FISH for measuring physical distances between genomic elements, ChIP for identifying CTCF binding sites, and 4C-seq for verifying allele-specific interactions and chromatin loops. While valuable, these approaches provided only partial views of the intricate parent-specific organizational landscape, with existing reconstruction methods for diploid maps limited by either coarse resolution or dependency on phased SNP information. Here, we applied our novel PSHIC algorithm to reconstruct chromatin structures and diploid Hi-C contact maps in a SNP-phasing-free manner, facilitating comprehensive analysis of allele-specific architecture. We conducted 128 independent trials on the H19/Igf2 imprinting region spanning 1 Mb (142-143 Mb) at 10 kb resolution with embedding dimension k = 8, utilizing published Hi-C data from mouse Patski cells on chromosome 7. Following filtration of invalid candidate solutions and matrix averaging, we enhanced Hi-C signals and generated differential maps to identify parent-specific chromatin interactions within this imprinting control region.

  Our results are consistent with previous studies and provide a comprehensive view of the regulatory architecture. We identified several key allele-specific interaction patterns (Fig. 4A): (1) mHIDAD exhibits preferential interactions with the H19 DMR on the maternal chromosome (box 1) while displaying enhanced contact frequencies with the Igf2 promoter region on the paternal chromosome (box 2); (2) A distinctive paternal interaction pattern is characterized by higher contact frequencies around the H19 DMR viewpoint extending toward the 3’ (telomeric) regions (box 3); (3) two centromeric CTCF binding clusters (Syt8 US and Lsp1) demonstrate parent-of-origin-specific interactions, forming more contacts with the H19 DMR on the maternal chromosome, consistent with CTCF’s insulator function when bound to the unmethylated maternal ICR (box 4); and (4) on the paternal chromosome, these same CTCF clusters selectively engage with regions surrounding the Igf2 promoter (box 5). In addition, we used Fit-Hi-C v2.0.8 to identify chromatin loops of 30-500 kb with FDR threshold q-value  < 10−7 on diploid Hi-C maps. Analysis of four bi-allelic CTCF binding clusters revealed parent-of-origin-specific looping patterns: maternal loops predominantly connected the H19 DMR with distal CTCF sites, while paternal loops preferentially linked CTCF clusters near Igf2.

  We examined reconstructed chromatin structures to quantify physical distances between regulatory elements on homologous chromosomes. High-dimensional structural coordinates were projected into 3D space using the PCA implementation in scikit-learn v1.2.2 for visualization (Fig. 4C), and pairwise distances were calculated from 128 independent structural predictions (Fig. 4B). Statistical analysis revealed that mHIDAD-H19 distances are significantly shorter on the maternal chromosome (one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 1.37 × 10−19), while H19-Igf2 distances show no parental bias. These results corroborate previous 3D DNA-FISH measurements using fosmid probes in the Igf2-H19 TAD. Notably, PSHIC detected significant differences in mHIDAD-Igf2 distances between homologs, with mHIDAD positioned substantially closer to Igf2 on paternal versus maternal chromosomes (one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 1.37 × 10−19). This observation agrees with ASHIC algorithm findings21 but differs from previous 3D DNA-FISH experiments showing no significant Igf2-mHIDAD distance differences between homologous chromosomes. This discrepancy reflects fundamental methodological differences: FISH captures spatial arrangements in individual cells, whereas our algorithm and ASHIC analyze population-aggregated signals from millions of cells in Hi-C datasets. While substantial cell-to-cell variability may obscure population-level patterns in single-cell FISH experiments, our population-based analysis reveals consistent structural features from ensemble averaging across millions of cells.

  Finally, we investigated the maternal-specific sub-TAD organization of the Igf2-H19 imprinted domain identified in previous studies21,27. Hi-C technology has revealed topologically associating domains (TADs) as fundamental mammalian genome structures–localized megabase-sized regions with preferential internal chromatin interactions. These domains remain stable across cell types and species, with boundaries enriched for CTCF binding sites that establish genome architecture7. Within larger TADs, sub-TADs form more localized interaction domains corresponding to regulatory neighborhoods that facilitate or restrict enhancer-promoter communications in a cell-type- and developmental stage-specific manner, though precise submegabase chromatin folding features remain poorly understood28,29.

  To quantitatively evaluate the domain-level chromatin organization, we computed the Directionality Index (DI)7 using inferred diploid Hi-C maps. Consistent positive DI peaks around mHIDAD and negative DI peaks near Igf2 were observed on both parental alleles, indicating the presence of a shared TAD covering these regulatory elements. Notably, both DI peaks align with bi-allelic CTCF binding clusters, supporting the role of CTCF in anchoring domain boundaries and mediating long-range loops between mHIDAD and Igf2. In contrast, an obvious negative DI peak is uniquely detected near the H19 DMR on the maternal chromosome, suggesting the presence of a subdomain boundary that does not occur on the paternal allele. This region coincides with maternal-specific CTCF binding, which is enabled by the absence of DNA methylation at the maternal H19 ICR, thereby facilitating insulator activity and establishing a distinct chromatin domain. To further validate this observation, we computed insulation scores30 for both alleles and derived a differential insulation profile. A local minimum was found at the H19 DMR, again exclusively on the maternal chromosome. This supports the existence of an allele-specific insulator element at the H19 DMR that establishes a maternal sub-TAD, functionally isolating the H19 promoter from shared downstream enhancers that would otherwise activate Igf2 (Fig. 4D).


### PSHIC recovered allelic chromatin features consistent with the phased ChIA-PET loops in human GM12878 cells
  We applied PSHIC to reconstruct diploid contact maps from mouse Hi-C datasets to investigate XCI and H19/Igf2 imprinting, validating PSHIC’s accuracy and robustness through consistency with previous experiments, including 3D-FISH measurements of parent-of-origin-specific spatial distances between regulatory loci, 4C-seq profiles of allele-specific interactions, and diploid Hi-C maps reconstructed using allele-certain and mate-rescue strategies based on phased haplotypes. To examine the cross-species robustness of our algorithm, we extended the analysis to human GM12878 lymphoblastoid cells by applying PSHIC to three regions with haplotype specificity from in situ Hi-C datasets5. We compared our reconstructed diploid maps with allele-specific features and chromatin loops previously identified using Chromatin Interaction Analysis by Paired-End Tag sequencing (ChIA-PET)31, a technique that captures high-resolution chromatin loops anchored by proteins such as CTCF through chromatin immunoprecipitation and proximity ligation. Unlike Hi-C or 4C-seq, ChIA-PET directly identifies protein-mediated chromatin loops and, when combined with phased genotypes, enables allele-specific loop assignment.

  First, we examined RNAPII-mediated allelic chromatin interactions involving the promoters of LOC374443, CLEC2D, and CLECL1 genes. RNA Polymerase II functions beyond transcription as a chromatin organizing factor, establishing functional loops that connect distant enhancers to target promoters32. RNAPII-mediated interactions frequently exhibit allele-specificity due to sequence variations and epigenetic differences between homologs, creating coordinated regulatory networks where enhancer activity and gene expression are biased toward the same parental chromosome. To study haplotype specificity in these enhancer-promoter interactions, we applied PSHIC to the 9.2–10.2 Mb region on chromosome 12 at 10 kb resolution. The reconstructed contact maps revealed exclusive paternal interactions between these gene promoters and their upstream regulatory elements (Fig. 5A), consistent with Tang et al.31, who demonstrated using phased RNAPII ChIA-PET in GM12878 cells that RNAPII occupancy at the upstream enhancer and promoter sites of LOC374443, CLEC2D, and CLECL1 was approximately 3-fold and 2.5-fold higher on the paternal allele, respectively, and that exclusive RNAPII-anchored loops were detected on the paternal allele at this locus (Fig. 6E therein). This alignment validates PSHIC’s capacity to detect functionally relevant allelic differences in transcriptional regulatory networks.

  We next applied PSHIC to the human IGF2/H19 imprinted region (chr11:1.4–2.4 Mb) at 10 kb resolution. The reconstructed diploid contact maps closely matched phased SNP-based Hi-C data from GM12878 cells, correctly capturing maternal-specific loops between HIDAD and H19, and paternal-specific loops between IGF2 and HIDAD (Fig. 5B). The allele-specific chromatin organization at this locus is consistent with the well-established imprinting mechanism, whereby differential methylation at the H19 differentially methylated region (DMR) determines allele-specific CTCF binding: on the maternal allele, unmethylated CTCF binding sites at the H19 DMR establish an insulator that prevents IGF2 from accessing downstream enhancers, whereas methylation of the paternal DMR abolishes CTCF binding and allows IGF2 activation31,33. Notably, the PSHIC-inferred architecture also mirrored the allele-specific chromatin patterns previously observed in the mouse H19/Igf2 domain, suggesting a conserved imprinting mechanism across species. In line with these findings, we observed a shorter spatial distance between HIDAD and H19 on the maternal homolog, and between HIDAD and IGF2 on the paternal homolog, further supporting the asymmetric, parent-of-origin-specific organization at this locus.

  Finally, we investigated the large-scale domains and loops on the inactive X chromosome by reconstructing diploid maps with PSHIC for the entire X chromosome at 1 Mb resolution. Similar to XCI in mouse Patski cells, GM12878 cells exhibited allele-specific chromatin structures. The paternal X chromosome is partitioned into two superdomains with frequent intra-domain contacts and weakened inter-domain interactions, which is absent in the maternal copy (Fig. 5C). DXZ4 lies at the boundary, maintaining the bipartite structure.

  Across all three genomic regions examined in human GM12878 cells, including the X chromosome, the IGF2/H19 imprinted locus, and the RNAPII-regulated gene cluster on chromosome 12, PSHIC successfully recovered allele-specific chromatin features that closely matched phased ChIA-PET data. For each region, we again performed 128 independent random trials, and the results demonstrated high consistency across all trials. The strong concordance between PSHIC-inferred allelic interactions and experimentally validated ChIA-PET loops demonstrates that our method can accurately identify allele-specific chromatin organization across diverse regulatory contexts in human cells.


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we introduce PSHIC, a novel computational framework for reconstructing diploid Hi-C contact matrices and inferring allele-specific chromatin structures without requiring phased haplotype information. PSHIC employs manifold learning within a customized stress function to disentangle observed Hi-C contact matrices into maternal and paternal components, enabling comprehensive analysis of allele-specific chromatin organization across diverse biological contexts.

  The most significant innovation of PSHIC lies in its complete independence from phased SNP data, addressing a fundamental limitation that has constrained diploid Hi-C analysis. Most existing contact-map reconstruction methods, including ASHIC-PM, ASHIC-ZIPM, allele-certain, and mate-rescue approaches, rely critically on accurately phased SNPs. For these SNP-dependent methods, this requirement creates substantial barriers for the analysis of non-model organisms, endangered species, and other resource-constrained or SNP-limited research contexts. Diploid-PASTIS is an important phasing-free structural inference method and therefore represents the closest conceptual baseline to PSHIC. However, our benchmarks show that the main limitation of Diploid-PASTIS in the tested scenarios is not only that it outputs homolog-specific 3D structures rather than allele-specific contact maps directly, but also that it struggles to accurately recover the underlying homolog-specific organization, producing substantially lower SCC and loop-recovery scores and much higher distance errors than PSHIC. Even minor phasing errors (0.1-2%) can dramatically compromise existing methods’ performance, while PSHIC maintained consistent accuracy across all error rates tested.

  Our evaluation strategy was designed to address complementary aspects of method validation: simulation studies, in which all methods operate under a shared and known generative model, isolate differences in decomposition accuracy and noise tolerance under controlled conditions; real-data analyses, where the underlying contact-distance relationship is not explicitly known, assess whether the reconstructed allele-specific features correspond to independently validated biology. PSHIC consistently outperformed competing approaches in simulation across every metric, and the real-data experiments across multiple species, cell types, resolutions, and biological contexts (Figs. 3–5) confirmed that this advantage translates to empirical settings, with reconstructed features aligning with orthogonal experimental evidence from 3D DNA-FISH, 4C-seq, and phased ChIA-PET.

  While PSHIC represents a significant advance, several limitations remain. First, the current framework primarily focuses on intra-chromosomal diploid reconstruction and does not explicitly model inter-chromosomal long-range interactions, which are an important component of the genome-wide chromatin contact network. Future extensions of PSHIC will incorporate inter-chromosomal contacts and whole-genome spatial constraints to provide a more complete representation of diploid genome organization in the 3D nucleus. Second, the resolution explored in this study is constrained by the availability of ground-truth structural references in our simulation framework. Although PSHIC can in principle be applied to 10 kb contact matrices, our systematic resolution benchmark was limited to 20 kb because the Dip-C-derived ground-truth coordinates used for simulation were available at 20 kb as the finest resolution. Therefore, routine whole-genome reconstruction at 10 kb has not been systematically validated in this study. In our current benchmarks, 250 kb–1 Mb provided the best trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and computational efficiency for chromosome-wide analyses, whereas 10–20 kb reconstruction should be interpreted as more suitable for targeted regions or deeply sequenced datasets. Beyond diploid Hi-C reconstruction, PSHIC may also serve as a general feature-extraction framework for genomic data analysis. Because PSHIC uses Hi-C contact matrices as input and does not require species-specific phased SNP references, it may be adaptable to diverse organisms and experimental settings with reduced preprocessing requirements. This dataset-agnostic design could facilitate its integration into downstream analytical workflows and may enable the extraction of homolog-specific or structure-aware features from complex genomic datasets across emerging model systems and spatial genomics technologies.


## METHODS

### Problem formulation
  The experimental workflow begins with the crosslinking of DNA, which preserves spatially proximal genomic loci through chemical fixation. The subsequent steps of digestion, ligation, and sequencing yield raw Hi-C reads in FASTQ format. Computational pipelines, such as Juicer34, process these reads to construct Hi-C contact matrices. This involves mapping the reads to the reference genome, filtering for valid ligation products, and assigning interactions to fixed-size genomic bins. The resulting contact map matrix provides a quantitative measure of chromatin interactions between pairs of genomic loci, representing the combined contribution of both homologous chromosomes in diploid genomes. This lack of homolog specificity introduces challenges for studying the distinct spatial structures and differential expressions of maternal and paternal chromosomes. To address this, we aim to disentangle the observed contact matrix into allele-specific contact matrices and reconstruct the spatial structures of both homologs.

### Hi-C manifold embedding with adaptive loss

#### Revisit Multidimensional Scaling
  To address the challenge of reconstructing diploid genome structures from noisy Hi-C contact matrices, we used Multidimensional Scaling (MDS)35 as a foundational mathematical framework for embedding high-dimensional data into lower-dimensional space. MDS transforms a matrix of pairwise distances or similarities into spatial embeddings, where each data point is represented as a coordinate in a geometric space. By preserving pairwise relationships, MDS enables the visualization and analysis of complex datasets in a more interpretable form. In the context of this study, this involves translating Hi-C contact frequencies–approximating spatial proximities between genomic loci–into structural representations of the genome.

  MDS offers several variants, each suited to specific data types and analytical needs. Classical MDS assumes Euclidean geometry and computes embeddings through eigenvalue decomposition of a double-centered distance matrix36. While efficient, it is sensitive to noise in the input data. Metric MDS, in contrast, minimizes a stress function to reduce discrepancies between observed and modeled distances, offering greater robustness for noisy or imperfect data. Non-metric MDS only preserves the rank order of pairwise dissimilarities rather than their exact values, making it particularly useful for qualitative or highly variable data.

  Hi-C contact matrices introduce unique challenges, including experimental noise, uneven restriction enzyme cut-site distribution, and stochastic sequencing biases37. To address these issues, we adopt a hybrid approach. First, classical MDS is used to efficiently generate draft spatial structures of diploid genomes as an initial embedding. Then, metric MDS refines these structures by optimizing a customized stress function with regularization designed to account for the specific properties and biases of Hi-C data. Details of the manifold learning procedure are described in the following section.


#### Transfer function for mapping distances to contacts
  To reconstruct spatial genome structures, a critical step involves converting Hi-C contact counts into spatial distances. Hi-C experiments measure the number of ligation events between pairs of genomic fragments, producing a matrix of contact frequencies that represents the likelihood of spatial proximity between loci. However, these contact counts do not directly correspond to physical distances in the embedding space. To infer spatial distances from contact counts, a transfer function is required to formalize the relationship between these two measures, which is governed by polymer physics and depends on the structural state of chromatin.

  Extensive studies of polymer behavior, particularly in the context of DNA, have proposed relationships between genomic distance s, contact counts c, and physical distances d 38. Specifically, in mammalian DNA modeled with the fractal globule model, contact counts and genomic distance are inversely correlated (c ∝ s−1), and polymer volumes expand linearly with subchain length, following the relationship (d3 ∝ s). Similarly, in smaller genomes fitting the equilibrium globule model, contact counts scale as c ∝ s−3/2, while physical distances scale as d ∝ s1/2. Both models yield the same scaling law, where physical distance is proportional to the cube root of the inverse contact count (d ∝ c−1/3). Moreover, Ye et al. identified a similar distance-decay pattern using two human GM12878 datasets21: the X chromosome structure predictions obtained from single-cell Hi-C data by Tan et al.39 and bulk in situ Hi-C data provided by Rao et al.5 at a resolution of 100 kb. By employing genomic distance as an intermediate bridge variable, they estimated the scaling relationships for maternal and paternal chromosomes as c ~ d−3.02 and c ~ d−3.15, respectively.

The structures are then iteratively updated via gradient descent until convergence.   For real bulk Hi-C datasets, a single execution of the optimization process exhibits considerable variability and potential bias. This variability is expected, as bulk Hi-C data represent aggregated signals from heterogeneous cell populations at various stages of the cell cycle, leading to substantial interpretational challenges due to structural heterogeneity among samples. To mitigate these effects, we conduct multiple independent trials (S) with different random initializations for real Hi-C datasets, thereby exploring various optimization pathways within the search space.

  To obtain a robust final reconstruction, we employ a spectral clustering-based selection mechanism to remove outliers from the candidate solutions. Each independent run generates two candidate contact maps, C1 and C2, but it is unknown whether C1 corresponds to Cp and C2 to Cm, or vice versa. To resolve this, we apply spectral clustering to the full set of reconstructed maps across all runs, partitioning them into two distinct clusters based on structural similarity. For a valid reconstruction, it is expected that within each run, the two output maps (C1 and C2) should belong to different clusters, reflecting their allelic distinction. If both maps from the same run are assigned to the same cluster, the run is deemed unreliable and discarded. Given the final clustering assignment, we consistently assign one cluster to Cp and the other to Cm across all valid runs, ensuring that only high-confidence results contribute to the final averaged reconstructed maps. ### Optional inter-homolog contact modeling mode
  In standard diploid Hi-C experiments of mammalian interphase cells, inter-homolog contacts constitute a small fraction of total chromatin interactions (approximately 2%41), and PSHIC’s default mode treats these residual signals as experimental noise absorbed by the intra-homolog reconstruction. However, for biological contexts where inter-homolog contacts are substantially elevated, such as during meiosis, where Muller et al. demonstrated using a designer Hi-C approach that inter-homolog contact fractions rise from 2.7% prior to replication to 7.0% at 4 h into meiotic entry42, PSHIC provides an optional inter-homolog contact modeling mode that explicitly incorporates a third contact component into the reconstruction framework: 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$${{{{\bf{C}}}}}_{{{{\rm{obs}}}}}={{{{\bf{C}}}}}_{m}+{{{{\bf{C}}}}}_{p}+{{{{\bf{C}}}}}_{{{{\rm{inter}}}}},$$\end{document}Cobs=Cm+Cp+Cinter, where Cinter captures contacts arising between loci on opposite homologs, parameterized by an inter-homolog scaling factor sinter that modulates the relative contribution of inter-homolog interactions to the total observed signal. In our simulation study, we set the inter-homolog contact fraction to 8% of total contacts, representing a conservative upper bound of this empirically reported range. This mode employs a two-phase optimization strategy: Phase 1 performs standard PSHIC reconstruction to obtain reliable initial estimates of the homolog-specific structures, which are subsequently refined in Phase 2 through joint optimization of intra- and inter-homolog contact components with iterative closed-form estimation of sinter. Full mathematical details of the inter-homolog contact model, the two-phase optimization procedure, and the closed-form sinter estimation are provided in Supplementary Methods, and the corresponding benchmarking results are presented in Supplementary Note 1.


### Hi-C Datasets and simulation
  To comprehensively and quantitatively evaluate the performance of several reconstruction methods, we designed the simulation study and conducted experiments on publicly available genome-wide Hi-C datasets. ### Compared methods
  We benchmarked PSHIC against five representative baselines for diploid or haplotype-resolved Hi-C reconstruction: Diploid-PASTIS15, ASHIC-PM, ASHIC-ZIPM21, allele-certain, and mate-rescue. Diploid-PASTIS was run with default parameters, with the power-law exponent set to α = 3 to match the initialization used in datasets. For the resolution analysis, we used the default parameters at resolutions with fewer than 1000 bins. At the 20 kb resolution, where the matrix contained more than 1000 bins, Diploid-PASTIS did not converge within 48 h under its default setting in our runtime tests. To obtain a practical runtime reference at this resolution, we therefore capped the maximum number of optimization iterations at 5000. ASHIC-PM and ASHIC-ZIPM were included as SNP-dependent probabilistic baselines that jointly infer allele-specific contact maps and structures. Both methods were run with default parameters using the same phased SNP references, except that structure initialization was set to random instead of the default MDS to avoid numerical errors during MDS initialization. Allele-certain and mate-rescue5,17–20 were evaluated as SNP-based read-assignment baselines implemented in HiC-Pro. Allele-certain retains only read pairs with both ends confidently assigned to parental alleles, whereas mate-rescue additionally assigns pairs with one SNP-overlapping end based on the confidently assigned mate. Both strategies used the same phased SNP references as ASHIC-PM and ASHIC-ZIPM.


### Evaluation metrics
  We adopted the following criteria to evaluate the performance of PSHIC in simulation studies. (1) We calculate the proportion of observed Hi-C reads that are assigned to either paternal or maternal Hi-C contact maps, which can reflect the strength of the recovered Hi-C signals. The same orientation is not required, as we are primarily concerned with relative distances rather than absolute positions. The predicted structures can be subsequently rotated and translated to the most meaningful orientations that align with biological knowledge of the studied organism. (3): We evaluated the similarity between reconstructed diploid matrices and the ground truth using two Hi-C analysis software packages. First, we calculated the Stratum Adjusted Correlation Coefficient (SCC) score using HiCRep v0.2.647 to compare contact maps by accounting for the dependency of contact frequencies on genomic distance. Specifically, Hi-C matrices are stratified based on genomic distance, and for each stratum, the Pearson correlation coefficient is calculated between the predicted and true matrices. The SCC score is then obtained by combining the weighted correlation coefficients across all strata. This measurement ensures that comparisons are not biased by global variations, thereby providing a balanced measure of similarity that reflects both local and global chromatin structures. (4): Furthermore, we used Fit-Hi-C48 to estimate the confidence of interactions in both predicted and ground-truth matrices. Statistically significant interactions were then identified based on a q-value threshold. We calculated recall, precision, and F1 scores to measure the accuracy of the reconstructed loops in diploid matrices.

  For real-world datasets, we employed different evaluation strategies based on the specific biological context. Quantitative metrics included the bipartite index for assessing XCI, the directionality index and insulation score for sub-TAD organization in the H19-Igf2 imprinted region, and Pairwise Spearman correlation of PC1 profiles to compare the similarity of Hi-C maps reconstructed in different gene-edited cells. These metrics together quantitatively validate the ability of PSHIC to accurately and reliably reconstruct and distinguish differential genomic patterns and chromatin structures.


### Convergence and running time
  The convergence time of PSHIC depends mainly on the number of genomic bins, the embedding dimension, and the number of random initializations. Higher resolutions and higher embedding dimensions introduce more model parameters and therefore require longer reconstruction times. PSHIC was implemented with GPU acceleration in this study, and the PSHIC runtimes reported here were measured using this GPU-accelerated implementation. For PSHIC optimization, we used multiple random initializations to explore the non-convex optimization landscape. Convergence was determined when the loss value failed to improve for 10,000 consecutive iterations, with a maximum of 50,000 iterations to avoid excessive computation. In practice, most runs converged within 20,000 iterations. To assess practical feasibility, we evaluated the wall-clock runtime of PSHIC and compared it with ASHIC-ZIPM, ASHIC-PM, Diploid-PASTIS, Mate-rescue, and Allele-certain across six resolutions from 1 Mb to 20 kb, corresponding to 28 to 1334 genomic bins. PSHIC is implemented with GPU acceleration, whereas the other methods are run on CPU, as no GPU-accelerated implementations are publicly available for them. The summarized average runtimes are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Overall, the GPU-accelerated implementation of PSHIC remained computationally feasible at the chromosome scale. PSHIC’s runtime scales with matrix size: at coarse resolutions (1 Mb-250 kb, corresponding to 28-108 bins), a single run typically completes within 10–20 min, while at finer resolutions (50 kb-20 kb, 534–1334 bins), runtime increases to approximately 30–55 min per run. In all tested settings, PSHIC’s runtime remains practical for routine chromosome-scale analyses. We also evaluated preprocessing time from raw FASTQ files using the real Hi-C dataset GSM2863686/SRR6317652. Since PSHIC does not require SNP information, its input can be generated directly using Juicer preprocessing, which took 05:19:17 with 32 threads in our experiment. In contrast, SNP-dependent analyses require an additional SNP-calling step. Using the BWA-MEM/SAMtools/bcftools v1.12 pipeline on the mouse mm10 chromosome X reference, this SNP-calling step took 25:52:43 with 32 threads. Thus, PSHIC avoids ~ 25.9 h of additional preprocessing in this example. This preprocessing time is dataset- and platform-dependent and is provided as an empirical reference.

  Custom analyses, including PSHIC model optimization, allele-specific contact-map reconstruction, structural analysis, statistical testing, and figure generation, were performed using Python v3.8.10 with NumPy v1.22.4, SciPy v1.7.0, pandas v2.0.0, scikit-learn v1.2.2, Matplotlib v3.7.1, and PyTorch v2.0.0+cpu.
