# MutFormer: a multimodal deep learning framework for predicting somatic mutation hotspots from sequence and chromatin features


## Background
  Somatic mutations are unevenly distributed across the genome, with certain loci recurrently mutated across independent tumors. Both local DNA sequence context and chromatin organization have been associated with mutation rate variation, yet their combined effects at single-nucleotide resolution remain incompletely characterized.


## Results
  We developed MutFormer, a multimodal framework that integrates sequence context with chromatin-associated features to estimate mutation hotspot susceptibility at single-base resolution. Model performance was evaluated using a chromosome-partitioned strategy to reduce potential bias from local sequence similarity. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.979 and an AUPRC of 0.955 on TCGA data, and showed consistent performance on an independent METABRIC cohort.

  In addition to overall predictive accuracy, the integration of chromatin features altered the relative ranking of genomic loci. A subset of hotspot positions showed increased predicted susceptibility compared with sequence-only models, suggesting that chromatin context contributes information not captured by sequence composition alone. Sequence-derived features, particularly CpG-related metrics, accounted for the majority of predictive signal, whereas chromatin features provided a secondary, context-dependent contribution. The absolute AUROC gain from chromatin integration over the sequence-only model was modest (ΔAUROC = 0.004). Chromatin features instead reordered the prediction ranks of a specific subset (∼14%) of hotspot loci, with most loci unaffected.


## Conclusions
  These results support a model in which intrinsic sequence composition defines baseline mutation susceptibility, while chromatin context modulates the relative prioritization of specific loci. This framework provides a complementary approach for identifying mutation-prone regions beyond sequence-based models. MutFormer runs in a sequence-only mode by default and incorporates chromatin features as an optional enhancement when matched data are available.

## Background
  Somatic mutations are unevenly distributed across the human genome, with certain loci recurrently mutated across independent tumors [1–4]. These mutation hotspots are commonly used to study mutational processes and their potential functional relevance in cancer genomics. At the sequence level, local nucleotide composition, particularly CpG dinucleotides, has been identified as a major determinant of mutability, reflecting well-characterized biochemical mechanisms such as spontaneous deamination [5–7]. In parallel, large-scale studies have shown that chromatin organization, including accessibility, histone modifications, and replication timing, contributes to regional variation in mutation rates. These observations are further supported by work on three-dimensional chromatin organization and the joint contribution of epigenomic features to local sequence-context biology [8–14].

  Despite these advances, predicting mutation susceptibility at single-nucleotide resolution remains challenging. Existing statistical frameworks, such as MutSigCV and related methods, model background mutation rates at the gene or regional level and are primarily designed for identifying positively selected genes [15–20]. While effective for driver discovery, these approaches are not suited for estimating locus-specific susceptibility or capturing nonlinear interactions between sequence context and epigenomic features [3, 18, 19]. As a result, they provide limited resolution for prioritizing individual genomic positions.

  Recent deep learning approaches have demonstrated strong performance in modeling genomic sequence and regulatory features. Convolutional and Transformer-based architectures can learn complex representations of DNA sequence and have been successfully applied to tasks such as chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity, and regulatory element prediction [21–24]. More recently, large-scale pretrained models such as Nucleotide Transformer, DNABERT-2, and HyenaDNA further highlight the potential of sequence-based representation learning in genomics [25–27]. In addition, multimodal frameworks integrating sequence and epigenomic data have improved predictive performance in several cancer genomics applications [28–30]. However, most existing models are optimized for regulatory prediction or variant effect estimation, rather than explicitly modeling recurrence of somatic mutations at base resolution. We include a reimplemented DeepSEA [31] as a comparison baseline to benchmark sequence-based deep learning performance on our task.

  Here, we present MutFormer, a multimodal framework for predicting mutation hotspot susceptibility at single-nucleotide resolution. MutFormer integrates local DNA sequence context with chromatin-associated features using a hybrid CNN–Transformer encoder and cross-attention fusion. To reduce information leakage arising from genomic proximity, we adopt a chromosome-partitioned evaluation strategy that provides a stringent assessment of model generalization. The framework outputs a per-position probability score, enabling systematic prioritization of mutation-prone genomic loci across the genome. Architectural details, including the layer dimensions of the sequence and epigenomic branches and the cross-attention fusion module, are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. The pairwise correlation structure of the 28 features is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.


## Results

### Overview of the MutFormer framework and dataset composition
  MutFormer integrates local DNA sequence context with chromatin-associated features to predict susceptibility to somatic mutation hotspots at single-nucleotide resolution. The framework employs a hybrid CNN–Transformer encoder to process 1,001 bp sequence windows and a multilayer perceptron to encode numerical chromatin features, with cross-attention enabling multimodal fusion (Fig. 1A).

  Fig. 1Overview of the MutFormer framework and feature composition. A Architecture of MutFormer. DNA sequence windows (1,001 bp) are encoded by a CNN–Transformer module; epigenomic features are encoded by a multilayer perceptron (MLP). A cross-attention layer fuses the two modalities and outputs a per-locus hotspot susceptibility score. B Composition of the 28 input features, grouped into sequence-derived metrics (CpG observed/expected ratio, GC content, CpG density, k-mer spectra), histone modification signals (H3K4me3, H3K27ac), and genome-annotation variables (ChromHMM state, replication timing, evolutionary conservation). Alt text: Schematic overview of the MutFormer framework integrating sequence and chromatin feature encoders with cross-attention fusion

  A total of 28 features were incorporated, including sequence-derived metrics, histone modification signals, and annotation-based variables (Fig. 1B). This compact feature set was designed to balance interpretability and predictive performance, while expanded epigenomic features were evaluated separately in external validation (Supplementary Fig. S3). The primary dataset comprised 5,000 hotspot positions and 15,000 chromosome-matched control sites. An additional 1,979 hotspots that fell outside the training/validation/test partitions were retained for genome-wide score comparison (Figs. 5 and 6), yielding a total of 6,979 hotspot loci for re-ranking analyses.


### Genomic and feature characteristics of hotspot loci
  Mutation hotspots were distributed across all chromosomes with variable frequencies (Fig. 2A). Correlation analysis revealed strong associations among CpG-related features and GC content (Fig. 2B), consistent with established sequence composition biases at mutagenic sites.

  Feature distributions differed between hotspot and control sites, with hotspots showing elevated CpG observed-to-expected ratios and GC content (Fig. 2C, D). Effect-size analysis identified CpG-related metrics as the most discriminative features (Supplementary Fig. S4). Both sequence composition and chromatin-associated features are therefore relevant to modeling mutation susceptibility.

  At a decision threshold of 0.5, MutFormer achieved an accuracy of 0.950 and an F1 score of 0.900, with sensitivity of 0.908 and specificity of 0.964 (Fig. 3D). These metrics confirm robust classification under chromosome-partitioned evaluation, which minimizes information leakage from regional genomic similarity.

  A modality comparison showed that sequence features account for the majority of predictive signal: the sequence-only model achieved an AUROC of 0.975, whereas the epigenomic-only model achieved an AUROC of 0.926 (Table 1; DeLong test of MutFormer vs. sequence-only, P = 0.043). Although the absolute AUROC gain over the sequence-only model was modest (ΔAUROC = 0.004), chromatin integration produced a consistent and reproducible re-ranking of a defined subset of loci (∼14%), characterized in detail in the following sections. Under stricter trinucleotide + GC+CpG-density+replication-timing+ChromHMM-class control matching (Methods “Stricter control matching”; Supplementary Fig. S7), the pre-trained MutFormer retains AUROC = 0.988 (95% CI 0.987–0.990) on the 4,969 matched pairs, with sequence-only AUROC = 0.980 (ΔAUROC = + 0.0086). A bootstrap evaluation on the METABRIC validation cohort (200 resamples; 550 hotspots + 550 matched controls) gave AUROC = 0.835 ± 0.011, AUPRC = 0.827 ± 0.017, balanced accuracy = 0.745 and MCC = 0.489 (Supplementary Table S6).


### Ablation analyses
  We performed systematic ablation experiments to quantify modality-specific contributions (Fig. 4). Removing sequence features reduced AUROC from 0.979 to 0.926, indicating that sequence context is the primary determinant of predictive performance. In contrast, removing histone modification features resulted in a modest decrease to AUROC = 0.972, suggesting that epigenomic features provide complementary but secondary signal.

  Replacing cross-attention with simple feature concatenation resulted in a small performance decrease (AUROC = 0.975), indicating a limited but measurable benefit of explicit multimodal interaction.

  We further compared peak-based and signal-based epigenomic representations using an expanded feature set (eight chromatin tracks with summary statistics; see Methods). Continuous chromatin signals substantially outperformed binary peak annotations (AUROC 0.992 vs. 0.622; Fig. 4C), highlighting the importance of quantitative epigenomic measurements. The higher AUROC relative to the primary MutFormer model (0.979) reflects the use of additional signal-based features not included in the compact 28-feature set; this setting is therefore not directly comparable to the primary model and was not adopted as the default configuration, because the compact 28-feature set prioritizes interpretability and consistency across datasets. Full statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S2.


### Feature contributions
  Feature-level analyses consistently identified CpG-related metrics and GC content as the most discriminative predictors of hotspot susceptibility (Supplementary Fig. S4). CpG observed-to-expected ratio showed the strongest effect size, followed by GC content and CpG density.

  Epigenomic features, including H3K4me3 and H3K27ac signals, were moderately enriched in hotspot regions, suggesting that chromatin state provides predictive signal beyond intrinsic sequence composition.

  To quantify how chromatin integration shifts model predictions across hotspot categories, we directly compared the sequence-only and full-model outputs at individual loci (Fig. 5).

### Score-based re-ranking by chromatin features
  To characterize how chromatin features alter predictions relative to the sequence-only model, we compared the two sets of outputs across all hotspot loci (Fig. 5A). Based on their score trajectories, hotspots fell into three score-based categories: Minimal-shift loci (13.8%, n = 962), whose scores were largely unchanged by chromatin integration; Stable high-score loci (72.3%, n = 5,045), which received high scores under both models; and Large-shift loci (13.9%, n = 972), which scored substantially higher in the multimodal model than in the sequence-only model (Fig. 5B).

  The score shift distribution (full model minus sequence-only model) differed markedly across the three classes (Fig. 5C). Minimal-shift loci showed a modest negative shift, consistent with chromatin features slightly redistributing probability mass. Stable high-score loci were minimally affected. Large-shift loci displayed the largest positive shifts, confirming that chromatin context captures risk at loci largely missed by sequence features alone. The same re-ranking pattern was observed at the genome-wide level, with Large-shift loci preferentially elevated in the full-model ranking (Fig. 6A, B).

  In sum, chromatin does not replace sequence-based predictions but refines them, boosting scores for a specific subset of loci that the sequence-only model underranks. (Supplementary Fig. S5 shows that the same re-ranking pattern is reproduced when restricted to the 5,000 training-set hotspots).


### Biological context of hotspot-associated features
  Hotspot regions were enriched for CpG-related sequence features and elevated GC content (Supplementary Fig. S4). In addition, modest increases in active histone marks (H3K4me3 and H3K27ac) were observed in hotspot windows compared with controls.

  These patterns are consistent with prior observations linking CpG dinucleotide density and GC-rich context to elevated mutation rates, and with evidence that active regulatory chromatin is associated with increased DNA damage or reduced repair efficiency.

  To assess generalizability, we performed external validation across multiple settings (Fig. 7). In chromosome-partitioned TCGA evaluation, MutFormer achieved AUROC = 0.979, outperforming sequence-only (0.975) and epigenomic-only (0.926) models (Fig. 7A).

  We further evaluated model transferability on the independent METABRIC breast cancer cohort [32]. Using matched hotspot and control positions and epigenomic features derived from MCF-7 cell line data, the combined model achieved AUROC = 0.866, compared to 0.835 for sequence-only and 0.634 for epigenomic-only models (Fig. 7B), demonstrating consistent performance gains from multimodal integration. Per-model performance metrics are tabulated in Supplementary Table S3, and panel-level visualisations (ROC, PR, feature ablation, feature importance) are provided in Supplementary Fig. S6.

  Feature ablation analysis on the METABRIC dataset confirmed the contribution of epigenomic features, yielding a 3.1%-point improvement in AUROC when combined with sequence features (Fig. 7C).

  Feature importance analysis further showed that while sequence-derived features dominate predictive performance, chromatin state annotations (e.g., chromhmm_Txn_Elongation) rank among the top contributors (Fig. 7D), supporting the transferability of epigenomic signals across independent cohorts. (Supplementary Table S4 reports the full cross-cohort feature importance rankings),

  To directly test whether K562 functions as a generalizable reference epigenome rather than a tumor-of-origin proxy, we performed a cross-tissue external validation on the Broad Institute SKCM melanoma cohort, whose mutational landscape is dominated by UV-driven C→T transitions at dipyrimidine sites — a process biologically unrelated to either K562 (chronic myeloid leukemia) or MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma). From 220,430 SNVs across ~ 700 samples we extracted 422 hotspots (≥ 3 distinct tumor samples) and 422 matched controls, and applied the same 26 sequence + 24 K562-epigenomic feature pipeline used for METABRIC. Five-fold cross-validation yielded AUROC = 0.768, AUPRC = 0.730, balanced accuracy = 0.710, and F1 = 0.724. Strikingly, 4 of the top 10 most important features were K562 epigenomic tracks (H3K36me3 max, H3K4me3 mean, K562 Repli-Seq center mean, DNase max), demonstrating that K562 chromatin context carries transferable predictive signal even when applied to a cancer type with completely different tissue of origin and mutational etiology (Supplementary Fig. S10, Supplementary Table S8).

  To address the K562/MCF-7 cell-line mismatch, we trained a parallel MutFormer variant in which the epigenomic encoder used MCF-7 ENCODE tracks (ENCSR849UYL, ENCSR000DOS et al.) instead of K562. The MCF-7-matched variant achieved AUROC = 0.881 on METABRIC, compared to 0.866 for the K562-trained model (+ 0.015), confirming that part of the cross-cohort performance gap is attributable to the cell-line mismatch but is not the dominant factor. In a complementary tumor-matched analysis, MutFormer augmented with TCGA Illumina 450 K methylation features (see Methods “Tumor-matched DNA methylation features”) achieved AUROC = 0.982 on the chromosome-partitioned TCGA test set and AUROC = 0.884 on METABRIC, with the two methylation features ranking 6th and 11th in importance, behind the CpG metrics but ahead of all K562 histone marks.

  MutFormer retains predictive ability across independent cohorts and cancer types, although performance decreases as biological distance from the training data increases (TCGA AUROC 0.979 → METABRIC 0.866 → SKCM 0.768). Chromatin features continue to add a small but consistent ranking-level contribution across these settings.


## Discussion
  In this study, we evaluated the relative contributions of sequence-derived and chromatin-associated features to somatic mutation hotspot susceptibility. Our results show that sequence features account for the majority of predictive signal, while chromatin features provide a consistent but more modest improvement across both chromosome-partitioned TCGA evaluation and independent validation. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that intrinsic sequence composition, particularly CpG-related features, plays a major role in determining mutation rates [7].

  The chromatin contribution acts differently from the sequence-based signal. Most positions are unaffected by chromatin integration; a minority of loci are reordered, and the reordering is reproducible between the TCGA chromosome-partitioned set and METABRIC. Loci that score poorly on sequence composition alone often score higher when chromatin features are added, in line with mutation susceptibility being shaped jointly by intrinsic sequence context and local chromatin state. Functional annotation of the Large-shift subset (promoter / enhancer / transcribed-region enrichment, cancer-gene overlap, replication-timing position) is not presented here and remains a target for follow-up work.

  From a mechanistic perspective, sequence composition reflects intrinsic mutability driven by established biochemical processes such as spontaneous deamination at CpG dinucleotides [7]. In contrast, chromatin-associated features may reflect context-dependent modulation of mutation rates through factors such as chromatin accessibility, replication timing, and DNA repair efficiency [8–11]. These observations are compatible with a model in which chromatin context shifts the local balance between DNA damage and repair at specific loci. That only a subset of loci shows strong re-ranking is consistent with selective rather than uniform chromatin effects.

  Compared with existing approaches, MutFormer answers a different question from existing tools. Statistical frameworks such as MutSigCV and related methods estimate background mutation rates at the gene or regional level and are primarily designed to identify positively selected genes [18–20]. Deep learning models developed for regulatory genomics have demonstrated strong performance in predicting chromatin accessibility and regulatory activity [21–24] but are not specifically designed to model recurrence of somatic mutations at single-nucleotide resolution. By formulating hotspot prediction as a locus-level ranking problem and integrating sequence and chromatin features within a unified framework, MutFormer provides a complementary approach for characterizing mutation susceptibility across the genome.

  Because the multimodal AUROC gain is small (ΔAUROC = 0.004), global discrimination metrics under-state the chromatin contribution to ranking. For variant prioritization or candidate locus selection, the relevant quantity is the relative ranking of high-risk loci rather than the absolute AUROC.

  Statistical comparison using DeLong’s test (Bonferroni-corrected) confirmed that AUROC differences between MutFormer and each baseline (logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and the reimplemented DeepSEA) were statistically significant (P < 0.001 for the four comparisons against the simpler baselines [logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and the reimplemented DeepSEA] after Bonferroni correction; the multimodal-vs-sequence-only comparison gave the uncorrected DeLong P = 0.043 reported in Results), although the absolute gain over the sequence-only variant (ΔAUROC = 0.004) was small. We therefore caution against interpreting chromatin features as a strong predictive driver based on AUROC alone, and instead emphasize their role in re-ranking a defined subset of loci (~ 14%) that are systematically under-prioritized by sequence-only models. Critically, when controls are matched on trinucleotide context, GC content, CpG density, replication timing, and ChromHMM class (Methods “Stricter control matching”), the pre-trained MutFormer retains AUROC = 0.988 versus sequence-only 0.980 (ΔAUROC = + 0.0086), comparable to the + 0.004 gain reported on the original chromosome-matched controls. The original AUROC numbers therefore do not stem from covariate confounding between hotspots and controls.

  Cross-cohort generalization should be interpreted with care. On the independent METABRIC cohort, the full multimodal model achieved AUROC = 0.866, a notable decrease from the chromosome-partitioned TCGA performance (AUROC = 0.979). Several factors likely contribute to this gap: (i) the training epigenome (K562) and the validation epigenome (MCF-7) reflect different cell-of-origin chromatin states; (ii) METABRIC uses a different mutation calling pipeline and tumor type composition than TCGA MC3; and (iii) the hotspot definition and control sampling strategy differed between datasets. Importantly, the relative ordering of model variants was preserved (full > sequence-only > epigenomic-only), and the multimodal gain (+ 3.1 AUROC percentage points over sequence-only) was larger on METABRIC than on TCGA. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that chromatin features contribute most when sequence-only predictions are less informative, supporting their conditional rather than uniform role.

  Because sequence features are the dominant determinant of performance, the sequence-only mode can serve as a standalone default configuration, whereas the chromatin-enhanced mode provides the greatest additional benefit for the Large-shift subset of loci and is recommended when matched chromatin data are available. Both modes use the same trained weights and are selected through a command-line flag in the released code.

  Several limitations should be considered. First, hotspot definition based on recurrence thresholds may bias the analysis toward highly recurrent loci, potentially overlooking lower-frequency events. Second, the use of reference epigenomic data introduces a mismatch between chromatin features and tumor-specific contexts, which may attenuate the contribution of epigenomic signals. Because chromatin features are optional in MutFormer, users are not required to supply mismatched chromatin data; the sequence-only mode can be used when matched data are unavailable, and matched chromatin features can be added when they exist. Third, mutational signatures were not explicitly modeled, despite their known association with sequence context and cancer type [6]. Incorporating signature-aware features may further improve model performance. Fourth, we did not benchmark MutFormer against recent genome foundation models (e.g., Nucleotide Transformer, DNABERT-2, HyenaDNA), as these large pretrained models are designed for general sequence representation rather than locus-level hotspot prediction and would require task-specific fine-tuning beyond the scope of this work; comparison against fine-tuned foundation models is an important direction for future work. Fifth, in the present study we focused on discriminative metrics (AUROC, AUPRC, F1); probability calibration and decision-curve analysis at realistic genome-wide hotspot prevalence (~ 3%) were not formally assessed and represent an additional important direction for downstream variant prioritization applications. Sixth, the primary analysis uses K562 as a default reference epigenome rather than tumor-of-origin-matched chromatin; cross-cell-line concordance varies by mark (Spearman r = 0.43–0.81 for K562 vs. MCF-7, Supplementary Fig. S8), and although we provide a MCF-7-matched variant and a tumor-methylation-augmented variant, fully tumor-type-matched chromatin profiling would be required to remove this caveat. Seventh, the primary analyses are performed on hg19 to match the MC3 mutation call set, which has no authoritative native hg38 release. We provide a liftOver hg19 to hg38 reciprocal coordinate-stability validation (94.12% retention) plus a full hg38-native evaluation (all 28 features re-extracted on hg38; AUROC = 0.929, Supplementary Fig. S9, Supplementary Tables S7, S10, S12) demonstrating that biological conclusions are preserved across the genome build. A full hg38-native re-training of MutFormer (left as a follow-up because of GPU compute requirements) would be expected to recover the 0.97–0.98 AUROC range. Eighth, ChromHMM states vary across cell types; we have therefore replaced the cell-line-specific K562 15-state annotation with the Roadmap Epigenomics 25-state pan-tissue model and added a per-locus chromatin-state entropy feature, but tumor-type-specific ChromHMM annotations are not uniformly available across TCGA cohorts. Ninth, genome-wide methylation coverage from Illumina HumanMethylation450 arrays is sparse relative to nucleotide-resolution hotspot prediction, resulting in extensive missingness within hotspot-centered windows. We therefore evaluated TCGA-BRCA methylation (Supplementary Table S11) as an external biological validation rather than incorporating it into the primary feature set. Tenth, MutFormer was benchmarked against logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and a reimplemented DeepSEA baseline; we selected DeepSEA because it provides an interpretable and reproducible sequence-based deep learning reference, whereas more recent genome foundation models (DNABERT-2, Nucleotide Transformer, Enformer, HyenaDNA) require substantially different pretraining paradigms and computational resources. Future studies should also evaluate MutFormer against genome-wide mutability models that explicitly control for extended nucleotide context (e.g., pentanucleotide or heptanucleotide models). Eleventh, K562 was selected because of its extensive ENCODE coverage; multi-cell-line consensus epigenomes and tissue-specific chromatin representations are an obvious next step. Twelfth, functional enrichment analyses of the Large-shift subset (e.g., enrichment in promoters, enhancers, open chromatin, or cancer-associated genes) were not performed and remain an important direction for future investigation. Thirteenth, model calibration was not formally evaluated because the primary objective of the present study was ranking rather than absolute probability estimation; calibration curves and Brier scores at realistic genome-wide prevalence are needed for downstream variant-prioritization applications. We additionally note that 450 K-derived methylation features were not incorporated into the primary model because sparse probe coverage resulted in feature missingness at more than 80% of hotspot windows; the methylation analysis (Supplementary Table S11) therefore served as an independent biological validation of the CpG sequence features already in the model, rather than as an additional input feature. Finally, stratifying the strict-matched loci by ChromHMM class yielded consistent per-stratum AUROCs across all five collapsed ChromHMM classes (Supplementary Fig. S11, Table S9), indicating that model performance does not hinge on any single chromatin state.


## Conclusions
  Our results support a model in which sequence composition defines the baseline landscape of somatic mutation susceptibility, while chromatin context provides additional, locus-specific modulation. The selective re-ranking of genomic loci observed in this study suggests that chromatin features may help identify mutation-prone regions that are not fully captured by sequence-based approaches alone. MutFormer therefore provides a complementary, locus-level framework for prioritizing mutation-prone regions. In view of the small absolute AUROC gain, the value of chromatin integration in this framework is best understood as the reproducible conditional re-ranking of a defined subset of loci, rather than as a global performance improvement, and the open-source release of the model and trained weights facilitates future benchmarking and methodological extension.


## Methods

### Somatic mutation data and hotspot definition
  Somatic single-nucleotide variants were obtained from the TCGA MC3 project, which aggregates mutation calls generated by multiple pipelines across 33 tumor types [5]. All variants were mapped to the hg19 reference genome. Analyses were restricted to single-base substitutions, as these events are best suited for modeling mutational susceptibility at nucleotide resolution. A mutation hotspot was defined as a genomic position mutated in at least three independent tumors. To minimize technical artifacts, loci overlapping assembly gaps, low-mappability regions, ambiguous reference bases, or annotated segmental duplications were excluded. After applying these filters, 5,000 recurrently mutated positions were retained for downstream modeling.


### Control site selection
  Control sites were sampled at a three-to-one ratio relative to hotspots. Candidate positions were required to lie outside hotspot loci and their immediate flanking regions and to avoid annotated repetitive elements. To ensure comparable sequencing opportunity, only positions observed in at least ten tumors were considered. Because mutation density varies substantially across chromosomes, the number of control sites contributed by each chromosome was matched to the chromosome-wise hotspot distribution. The three-to-one control-to-hotspot ratio was chosen to maintain a reasonable class balance during training; alternative ratios were not systematically evaluated, and the optimal ratio may depend on the specific imbalance tolerance of the model.


### Stricter control matching
  To address the concern that controls matched only on chromosome and sequencing opportunity may not be stringent enough, we performed an additional matching procedure that pairs each hotspot with a control on five covariates: (i) the central trinucleotide context; (ii) GC content over the 1,001 bp window (± 5% bins); (iii) CpG-density quartile; (iv) ENCODE Repli-Seq replication-timing decile (wavelet-smoothed waveSignal, K562 ENCFF000WJZ as primary, HCT116 ENCFF001GLD as sanity check); and (v) ChromHMM class (active promoter, enhancer, transcribed, polycomb, heterochromatin, quiescent; collapsed from the Roadmap 25-state pan-tissue model). Matching is performed within-chromosome by nearest-neighbour search with strict 1:1 replacement and propensity-score balancing across the five covariates (Mahalanobis distance < 0.25). Hotspots that could not be matched on all five constraints (n = 613/5,000) were retained with relaxed (trinucleotide + GC) matching. Applying the pre-trained MutFormer to the 4,969 strict-matched pairs gives AUROC = 0.988 (95% CI 0.987–0.990) versus sequence-only 0.980 (ΔAUROC = + 0.0086). Full results are reported in Supplementary Fig. S7 and Supplementary Table S5.


### Sequence extraction and sequence-derived features
  For each hotspot and control position, a 1,001 bp window centered on the site (± 500 bp) was extracted. Windows containing ambiguous nucleotides were removed. From each window, we computed nucleotide frequencies, GC content, CpG density, CpG observed-to-expected ratio, sequence entropy, and dinucleotide metrics previously associated with mutational processes. All numerical features were standardized using statistics computed from the training set only, thereby preventing information leakage across evaluation partitions.


### Epigenomic features
  K562 was selected because it provides one of the most complete ENCODE reference epigenomes with harmonized genome-wide coverage across all chromatin modalities used in this study. Chromatin-associated features were derived from the ENCODE K562 reference epigenome, used here as a default reference because it is one of two ENCODE Tier 1 epigenomes with the most complete genome-wide track coverage (all six core histone marks, DNase, ATAC, ChIP-seq for chromatin regulators, ChromHMM and Repli-Seq); no individual TCGA tumor type has comparable depth of publicly available chromatin profiling. This choice trades tumor-of-origin specificity for genome-wide track completeness, and we quantify the cost of that choice in Supplementary Fig. S8 (K562 × MCF-7 chromatin concordance). (ENCODE Project Consortium) [33]. Signal intensities for H3K4me3 and H3K27ac were extracted from BigWig tracks and averaged over the same 1,001 bp windows used for sequence analysis. These two marks were chosen primarily for their availability as high-coverage ENCODE reference profiles; their association with active promoters and enhancers makes them reasonable proxies for local regulatory activity, though a broader chromatin panel would likely capture additional signal. Regions lacking measurable signal were assigned zero values. Reference epigenomic profiles were used to capture general chromatin constraints shared across cell types, rather than tissue-specific regulatory effects; this choice prioritizes cross-cancer generalizability at the cost of epigenome–tumor matching. In the revised analysis, the cell-type-specific ChromHMM annotation was replaced with the Roadmap Epigenomics 25-state imputed pan-tissue model (127 reference epigenomes [Kundaje et al., Nature 2015]), and a per-locus chromatin-state entropy (Shannon entropy across the 127 tissues) was added so that the model can distinguish positions with tissue-stable versus tissue-variable chromatin context. K562 data were selected because they represent one of the most complete ENCODE reference profiles with genome-wide coverage. We acknowledge that this introduces a mismatch between the training epigenome and the tumor contexts from which hotspots were derived, which likely attenuates the contribution of chromatin features relative to what tumor-matched data would yield.


### Tumor-matched DNA methylation features
  To complement the cell-line-derived chromatin annotations, we additionally integrated tumor-matched DNA methylation features from the TCGA Illumina HumanMethylation450 platform (GDC Data Portal). For each hotspot/control position, we computed the mean tumor β-value within the 1,001 bp window (mean_tumor_beta) and the tumor-versus-matched-normal difference (hotspot_specific_hypomethylation = δβ). Probes lacking coverage were imputed using the chromosome-wise median β-value. The methylation features were normalized using statistics computed from the training set only and concatenated to the epigenomic feature block (total = 30 input features in the methylation-augmented variant). The methylation-augmented model achieved AUROC = 0.982 on the chromosome-partitioned test set and AUROC = 0.884 on the METABRIC cohort (see Results §"External validation” and Supplementary Table S1).


### Model architecture
  MutFormer consists of two modality-specific encoders followed by a fusion module. DNA sequences were one-hot encoded and processed using a hybrid convolutional–Transformer encoder. Three convolutional blocks captured short-range sequence motifs, and their outputs were passed to a four-layer Transformer encoder to model longer-range dependencies within each window. Chromatin-derived numerical features were processed using a two-layer multilayer perceptron, with hidden dimensions selected to match the output scale of the sequence encoder. A cross-attention module enabled interaction between the sequence and chromatin representations. The fused embeddings were then passed to a classification head to estimate hotspot probability. MutFormer can be run in two modes that share the same trained weights: a sequence-only mode, in which chromatin features are withheld (zero-masked) so that prediction relies on sequence information alone, and a chromatin-enhanced mode, in which matched chromatin features are supplied. The mode is selected at inference through a --sequence-only flag in the released code, and no retraining is required. The full architecture was implemented in PyTorch and contains approximately 2.55 million trainable parameters.


### Training strategy
  To prevent information leakage arising from regional similarity, chromosomes were partitioned into distinct training (1–17), validation (18–20), and test (21, 22, X) sets. The model was optimized using AdamW [34] with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ and weight decay of 0.01. Focal loss [35] was employed to address class imbalance. A cosine annealing schedule with warm restarts controlled the learning rate, and early stopping was applied when validation AUROC failed to improve for ten consecutive epochs. Gradient clipping was used to stabilize optimization. All chromosome-partitioned experiments were repeated with three random seeds, yielding less than 1% variance in AUROC across runs The full hyperparameter search procedure (Bayesian optimization with Optuna TPE, 60 trials; sensitivity analysis; final values and selection criterion) is documented in Supplementary Table S7, and the corresponding Optuna study database is released alongside the source code for full reproducibility.


### Baseline models and evaluation
  For comparison, logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting were trained using the handcrafted feature set. We also evaluated a reimplemented DeepSEA baseline [31] applied to one-hot encoded sequence input; its hyperparameters were tuned on the same validation split for fair comparison. Hyperparameters for all baselines were tuned on the validation split. Performance was evaluated on held-out chromosomes using AUROC, AUPRC, precision, recall, accuracy, and F1 score. Statistical comparison of AUROC between MutFormer and each baseline used DeLong’s test with Bonferroni correction. Standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were computed to quantify feature-level differences between hotspot and control windows.


### Reference genome and coordinate stability
  TCGA MC3 mutation calls and ENCODE K562 reference tracks were generated on the hg19 build, and the primary analyses were therefore performed in hg19 coordinates. To verify that our conclusions are not specific to the hg19 build, we lifted every hotspot and every control position to hg38 using the UCSC liftOver chain hg19ToHg38.over.chain.gz (--minMatch 0.95), retained only positions with a unique reciprocal hg38 → hg19 mapping (18,823 of 20,000 positions = 94.12% retention (1,113 unmapped, 64 non-reciprocal)), re-extracted all sequence-derived features from GRCh38 (1,001 bp windows), and re-ran the chromosome-partitioned evaluation. We further re-extracted all 28 MutFormer features (26 sequence features from hg38 FASTA plus 2 epigenomic peak indicators from K562 hg38 ENCODE broadPeak files; ENCFF616DLO for H3K4me3 and ENCFF038DDS for H3K27ac, downloaded natively without liftover) and ran the pre-trained MutFormer on those hg38-native features. The full-feature AUROC on hg38 is 0.929 (95% CI 0.926–0.934), AUPRC = 0.841, with the multimodal-over-sequence-only delta-AUROC of + 0.006 essentially matching the + 0.004 on hg19. K562 hg38 H3K4me3 peaks are enriched 4-fold at hotspots versus controls (3.6% vs. 0.9%), confirming biological signal preservation. Full results are in Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S8.


### External validation
  Two validation settings were used: (i) a task-level validation on held-out TCGA data with an alternative hotspot definition, and (ii) a cohort-level validation on the independent METABRIC breast cancer dataset [29]. For the task-level setting, we performed external validation using a related mutation susceptibility prediction task. Mutation hotspots were defined as genomic positions harboring somatic mutations in at least five independent tumor samples from the TCGA MC3 dataset. For each hotspot, a GC-matched control position was randomly selected from regions with zero observed mutations, ensuring comparable sequence composition. Feature extraction followed the same pipeline as the primary analysis, yielding 18 sequence-derived features and 29 chromatin-associated features per position. Classification models (logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting) were trained using five-fold stratified cross-validation, with performance evaluated by AUROC and AUPRC.


### Signal-based versus peak-based epigenomic features
  To compare signal-based and peak-based representations of chromatin features, continuous signals were extracted from ENCODE BigWig files for eight epigenomic tracks, including six histone modifications (H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K36me3, H3K9me3), DNase-seq accessibility, and replication timing. For each position, summary statistics (mean, maximum, standard deviation, and median) were computed within a ± 1,000 bp window, along with center-enriched metrics within ± 100 bp. These signal-based features were compared against binary peak-based features derived from broadPeak annotations to evaluate the predictive value of quantitative chromatin measurements.
