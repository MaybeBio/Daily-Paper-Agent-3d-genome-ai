# Multi-task adversarial autoencoder for functional genomic element generation with preserved biophysical properties

## Introduction
  Computational genomics enables the design of synthetic DNA sequences, such as promoters and enhancers, to advance synthetic biology and therapeutic applications [1, 2]. These sequences must exhibit precise functional properties, including transcription factor binding and gene expression regulation, while maintaining biophysical characteristics like GC-content, k-mer frequencies, and chromatin accessibility. The ability to generate such sequences computationally supports applications ranging from gene therapy and mRNA vaccine design to metabolic engineering, where tailored genomic elements drive targeted biological outcomes [3, 4].

  Generating synthetic genomic elements remains challenging due to the complexity of preserving both functional and biophysical properties [5]. This is a long-standing challenge in synthetic biology, as traditional methods are often time-consuming, inefficient, yielding outcomes often insufficient for reliable application [6]. Despite the advent of deep generative models, these limitations persist. In vivo validation reveals that a significant portion, up to 40% of synthetic promoters still fail to achieve expected regulatory effects, often due to deviations in sequence composition or structural constraints [7]. These failures incur significant costs in time and resources, limiting the scalability of synthetic biology applications. The need for sequences that generalize across diverse cell types and maintain empirical statistical distributions highlights the urgency of addressing these challenges [6].

  Existing generative models, such as Variational autoencoder (VAEs) and Generative adversarial network (GANs), excel at capturing local motifs but suffer from critical limitations. VAEs often face posterior collapse [8, 9], reducing sample fidelity, while GANs struggle with mode collapse in underrepresented genomic classes [10]. More recent architectures, such as Transformer-based models, effectively handle long-range dependencies but are typically hindered by fixed-length constraints that are ill-suited to variable-length genomic elements like promoters [11, 12]. Multi-task learning (MTL) frameworks, such as those integrating chromatin accessibility and gene expression prediction, improve generalization across predictive tasks but rarely extend to de novo multi-omic generation or conditional synthesis [13]. Crucially, existing frameworks rarely incorporate multi-objective optimization to simultaneously balance functional accuracy with multiple biophysical constraints, nor do they provide interpretable latent representations for biological validation [14, 15].

  This work addresses three primary research gaps: the inability of existing models to handle variable-length elements, the absence of frameworks that jointly integrate multiple biophysical constraints, and the limited interpretability of generated sequences. To resolve these challenges, we developed a Multi-Task Adversarial Autoencoder (MT-AAE) capable of generating variable-length genomic elements with high functional and biophysical fidelity. The model applies an adversarial training regime to encourage realism and incorporates auxiliary tasks to regularize the latent space, which strengthens biophysical preservation and enhances predictive utility for downstream biological analyses.

  The key contributions of this study are:A novel Multi-Task Adversarial Autoencoder (MT-AAE) architecture that fuses adversarial training with biophysical auxiliary losses to alleviate mode collapse and posterior divergence in genomic generation.A context-aware length normalization strategy that allows a fixed-length generative model to accommodate variable-length genomic inputs while preserving functional content and avoiding artifacts associated with naive padding or truncation.An interpretable latent space representation designed to enhance trust in synthetic sequences for therapeutic use.A comprehensive evaluation protocol incorporating Train-on-Synthetic, Test-on-Real (TRTS) utility metrics and Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) to validate both functional and biophysical fidelity [16, 17].

  The MT-AAE framework addresses critical limitations in synthetic biology and facilitates more reliable data augmentation for rare genomic classes. This capability stands to accelerate genomic research, as high-quality synthetic data can mitigate privacy concerns in large-scale biobanks and facilitate the design of bespoke therapeutic constructs [12].

Section “Methodology” presents the proposed MT-AAE methodology. Section “Results” details the experimental setup and evaluation. Section “Discussion” discusses results and implications, and Section 6 concludes with future directions.


## Literature review

### Deep learning in genomic sequence analysis
  Deep learning has reshaped computational genomics by enabling models to learn hierarchical sequence-to-function mappings directly from raw DNA [1, 2]. Early architectures were dominated by convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which effectively identified regulatory motifs and cis-elements through localized pattern detection, analogous to image filters [6, 18]. Although CNNs achieved high accuracy in transcription factor binding site prediction, they struggled to capture distal dependencies along genomic sequences. This limitation led to the adoption of recurrent neural networks (RNNs), with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) variants emerging as the leading approach for modeling sequential relationships in variable-length inputs, enabling advances in applications such as splice site and RNA structure prediction [19].

  The current paradigm has shifted toward transformer architectures, which employ self-attention to model long-range dependencies without the vanishing-gradient limitations of RNNs [11, 20]. Attention mechanisms have proven central to motif discovery, allowing models such as DNABERT to outperform conventional position weight matrices by learning cooperative interactions among distant genomic regions [21]. Hybrid attention–CNN architectures further refined this capability, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy in de novo motif discovery across diverse cell types [22].

  Many of these advances are structured around encoder–decoder frameworks, adapted from sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) models originally developed for machine translation [23]. These frameworks are well-suited to genomics, bridging biological modalities such as translating genomic coordinates to functional annotations or predicting epistatic interactions [24, 25]. This encoder–decoder foundation, which maps sequences into latent representations and reconstructs functional outputs, established the architectural basis for the next frontier: de novo generative modeling.


### Generative models in computational biology
  Generative modeling in computational biology has evolved from classical probabilistic formulations to deep neural architectures capable of capturing the high-dimensional structure of genomic sequence space [26]. VAEs have become a foundational approach for sequence generation, using latent variable models to encode complex regulatory patterns into compact, continuous representations that capture major sources of biological variation [27]. For example, BindVAE incorporates Dirichlet priors to promote motif-aware structure in the latent space, enabling the synthesis of enhancer elements that retain cell-type–specific sequence features [28]. This capacity for probabilistic latent-space interpolation proves especially advantageous in low-data scenarios, where they can enrich rare genomic subclasses or underrepresented functional states without requiring explicit motif engineering.

  GANs provide a complementary paradigm, using adversarial training to encourage the production of high-fidelity, sharply defined sequences reflective of empirical regulatory landscapes [29]. GAN variants have been applied to promoter design, regulatory grammar exploration, and de novo construction of biologically active sequences [30]. Feedback-regulated GANs (FBGANs) further integrate functional or biophysical scoring signals during training, guiding the generator toward sequences that satisfy multiple biological constraints and enabling practical generation of sequences across diverse lengths [31]. Outside of classical regulatory genomics, GAN-based frameworks have also been used to engineer synthetic genomes optimized for DNA data storage, including error-resilient sequence mimics of large mammalian genomes [32]. Recent work has substantially expanded the toolkit available for generative genomic modelling. DNADiffusion [33] applies denoising diffusion probabilistic models to the design of cell-type-specific regulatory sequences, reporting high motif fidelity and chromatin accessibility in the generated elements. Evo [34], a Hyena-based autoregressive model trained at genome scale, achieves strong performance in long-range sequence generation and variant-effect prediction. Geneformer [35] fine-tunes transformer representations for gene regulatory network inference, operating primarily as a discriminative framework rather than a generative model. These approaches represent the current frontier of large-scale genomic sequence learning. Their primary design objectives differ from those of MT-AAE, however, as none jointly enforces biophysical constraints, class-conditional generation across imbalanced functional categories, and interpretable latent structure within a compact framework suitable for data augmentation under resource constraints.

  Despite these progress, generative frameworks for de novo functional genomic element design face core limitations. Many models impose fixed-length outputs, ill-suited to the intrinsic variability of regulatory elements. Even when length-flexible, their outputs often diverge from empirical distributions in critical higher-order statistics—such as k-mer composition and GC-content—which govern molecular stability and protein-DNA interactions [36, 37]. Architecturally, GANs remain prone to mode collapse [10], eroding diversity for rare classes, while VAEs frequently suffer from posterior collapse [8], leading to an impoverished latent space that fails to preserve essential regulatory structure.

  Although conditional architectures, reinforcement-guided optimization, and physics-informed constraints have improved specific facets of generative fidelity [38], a fundamental gap persists. Current methodologies lack the capacity to jointly optimize the multi-objective requirements of biological sequence design—functional discriminability, motif integrity, global statistical coherence, and biophysical plausibility. Instead, they often prioritize a subset of these criteria, failing to produce fully functional and biomimetically consistent sequences.

  The importance of biophysical coherence in generative genomics is further underscored by recent work on chromatin organization and epigenome-transcriptome coupling. Sefer [39] demonstrated that Hi-C interaction graphs can be decomposed in terms of histone modification pairs, identifying four histone marks (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27ac) as broadly predictive of chromatin interaction patterns across species and cell types. This analysis directly motivates the inclusion of biophysical constraints in generative models, since synthetic sequences that deviate from these higher-order chromatin signatures are unlikely to be functional in cellular contexts. Complementarily, Sefer [40] proposed ProbC, a probabilistic framework for jointly modeling epigenomic and transcriptomic effects in three-dimensional genome organization. ProbC highlights that functional genomic element generation cannot be evaluated solely at the sequence level but must account for the broader regulatory landscape encoded in chromatin structure. These works collectively reinforce the rationale of the MT-AAE’s multi-objective design: while prior generative models have focused primarily on sequence statistics, robust generation of biologically active elements requires alignment with the epigenomic and structural constraints that govern transcriptional competence in vivo.

### Multi-task learning in bioinformatics
  MTL enhances generalization in bioinformatics by enabling models to share representations across biologically related objectives [42]. In genomics, MTL is applied to concurrent prediction of chromatin accessibility, transcription factor binding, and methylation states, capturing the interdependencies that govern regulatory function [43]. Joint optimization of complementary tasks such as variant-effect prediction and motif scoring—supports transfer learning while reducing reliance on extensive task-specific fine-tuning [44].

  Transfer learning across sequence-derived properties, including gene expression, chromatin states, and local epigenetic contexts, mitigates overfitting in sparsely annotated genomic datasets [45]. Beyond data efficiency, MTL introduces intrinsic regularization: auxiliary tasks act as inductive biases that reinforce biophysical consistency and stabilize training dynamics [46]. For example, UnitedNet integrates single-cell multi-omics data to jointly infer gene expression and chromatin accessibility, revealing interpretable cross-omic relationships [41]. Bayesian extensions of this paradigm advance precision medicine by co-modeling drug responses with patient-specific genomic profiles [47].

  Despite these established benefits, current generative frameworks seldom employ MTL as an architectural mechanism for unifying functional, compositional, and structural constraints. This gap limits their ability to generate sequences that maintain biological coherence across multiple regulatory dimensions. The proposed MT-AAE addresses this limitation by embedding multi-task supervision directly into the generative process, thereby improving both sequence fidelity and regulatory plausibility. Evaluating such multi-constraint generative models requires integrated metrics capable of jointly assessing functional accuracy, statistical coherence, and structural validity.


### Evaluation metrics for synthetic biological data
  Evaluating synthetic genomic sequences requires multi-faceted assessment that extends beyond standard generative losses. The TRTS framework establishes biological utility by measuring downstream predictive performance on empirical datasets [48]. This functional evaluation reveals limitations often obscured by likelihood-based metrics, especially in regulatory element prediction contexts where synthetic sequences must retain discriminative capacity on authentic biological benchmarks [5].

  Distributional fidelity is assessed using statistical divergence measures such as Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD), Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) tests, and k-mer frequency analyses across multiple sequence resolutions [49, 50]. When interpreted through mutual-information-based symmetrization, JSD captures deviations in GC-content, dinucleotide composition, and related higher-order features that influence nucleosome positioning and DNA mechanics [51]. KS testing complements this approach by evaluating cumulative distribution agreement for biophysical properties, including melting temperature and structural flexibility profiles [52].

  Preservation of regulatory grammar is examined through motif-centered tools such as the MEME Suite and AIModules, which quantify transcription factor binding site enrichment, positional conservation, and motif co-occurrence patterns [53]. These frameworks facilitate comparison of motif logos between synthetic and empirical sequences, revealing distortions in cooperative binding architectures and combinatorial regulatory logic [54]. Despite their utility, existing evaluation pipelines face scalability challenges for ultra-long sequences and lack integration with privacy-preserving metrics such as differential privacy epsilon bounds [48], which limits their application to genome-scale synthetic data generation.


### Research gaps and unaddressed challenges
  Substantial advances in generative modeling have not eliminated core limitations that restrict the reliable design of functional genomic sequences. Variable-length generation remains a central challenge. Most architectures rely on fixed-length windows, which constrain representation of regulatory elements whose boundaries are inherently heterogeneous, including enhancers, introns, and operons [11, 55]. This constraint is further amplified by unresolved assembly gaps in reference genomes, which introduce structural uncertainties that propagate into learned sequence distributions during training [56].

  A second unresolved issue concerns the balance between functional and biophysical fidelity. Models often excel in capturing motif-level patterns while failing to reproduce broader compositional and structural properties such as thermodynamic stability, secondary structure profiles, and long-range k-mer statistics [26]. These fidelity gaps are compounded by limitations in evaluation scalability. TRTS assessment becomes infeasible for genome-scale sequences because memory consumption grows quadratically with sequence length, preventing comprehensive benchmarking across full genomic contexts [48].

  Interpretability remains underdeveloped in generative sequence frameworks. Current models provide limited visibility into how latent variables encode regulatory dependencies or biophysical constraints, which restricts their applicability in settings that demand mechanistic insight or rigorous safety assessment [57]. Addressing these limitations requires generative systems that integrate multi-task supervision, regulatory constraint modeling, and transparent latent structure. The framework introduced in this study advances this direction by unifying functional prediction, structural coherence, and interpretable sequence generation within a single architecture that directly addresses these interconnected challenges.

#### Class imbalance correction strategy
  Addressing the severe class imbalance inherent in genomic datasets, where regulatory elements such as snoRNAs and tRNAs are naturally underrepresented, required a dual-strategy approach [60]. We combined strategic oversampling with weighted loss functions to ensure adequate representation of minority classes. The stratified balanced sampling algorithm (Algorithm 1) enforced minimum representation thresholds through strategic oversampling while maintaining proportional sampling for majority classes. This approach worked in tandem with class-weighted loss functions and a custom monitoring callback that provided real-time feedback on minority class performance, enabling dynamic curriculum adaptation during training.

#### Multi-task prediction heads
  Sequence reconstruction was augmented by three auxiliary tasks designed to enforce preservation of functional annotations and structural properties essential for biological activity. The classification pathway processed pooled sequence representations through a deep network with comprehensive regularization, implementing a dimensional progression of 512 → 256 → 128 → C, where C denotes the sequence class count.

  Concurrently, the GC content regression head predicted a fundamental biophysical property encoded in sequence composition, explicitly linking sequence generation to nucleosome positioning preferences. Additionally, biologically plausible sequence length distributions were implicitly learned through the reconstruction objective and explicit padding mechanisms, thereby preserving the characteristic length profiles of their respective functional classes.


### Biophysical property preservation module

#### GC content regression for thermodynamic consistency
  The GC regression head enforced explicit preservation of thermodynamic characteristics critical for DNA function. GC content directly influences DNA flexibility, melting temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$T_m$$\end{document}), and chromatin accessibility; these structural determinants govern transcriptional competence and regulatory function [62]. By regressing this fundamental biophysical property, the model ensured generated sequences maintained appropriate thermodynamic profiles for biological plausibility.


#### Motif preservation via attention mechanisms
  The multi-head attention mechanism enabled explicit identification and preservation of biologically significant regulatory motifs. By learning attention weight distributions that highlight specific sequence positions, the model captured functional elements such as transcription factor binding sites (e.g., TATA-box, CAAT-box), splice donor/acceptor sequences, and promoter elements. This capability ensured these critical regulatory regions were maintained in generated sequences, preserving functional signatures essential for biological activity.


#### Global sequence statistics alignment
  Beyond motif-level preservation, the model aligned global sequence statistics to maintain evolutionary constraints encoded in nucleotide composition patterns. We enforced consistency in dinucleotide frequencies, sequence entropy, and length distributions, ensuring generated sequences exhibited statistical properties consistent with natural genomic elements. This comprehensive alignment maintained the complex interdependencies between sequence composition, structure, and function.

### Ablation study and efficiency measurement

#### Ablation study design
  A five-stage ablation study was conducted to quantify individual component contributions and assess potential task interference within the MT-AAE architecture. Modules were integrated incrementally:Base AAE: Reconstruction-only autoencoder establishing baseline generative capacity.+ Classification: Addition of a functional classification pathway enforcing class-specific pattern learning.+ GC Regression: Inclusion of a GC-content regression head imposing compositional and biophysical constraints.+ Balanced Sampling: Strategic oversampling to mitigate class imbalance and improve minority-class representation.Full MT-AAE: Complete multi-task model integrating all objectives.

  Performance was evaluated at each stage using functional and sequence-quality metrics to characterize both cumulative and synergistic effects of multi-task learning on functional retention, GC-profile alignment, and minority-class recovery.


#### Efficiency measurement
  Computational efficiency was systematically quantified to assess practical scalability and resource requirements for large-scale genomic applications. We measured total training time, sequence generation throughput (sequences/second), and memory footprint across three complementary phases: training convergence, sequence generation, and end-to-end pipeline execution. These measurements ensured the MT-AAE framework maintained practical applicability for tasks including synthetic regulatory element design, data augmentation for underrepresented functional classes, and high-throughput in silico mutagenesis studies.

### Reproducibility and implementation details

#### Software and hardware specifications
  The MT-AAE was implemented in TensorFlow 2.8, with model evaluation and statistical analysis performed using scikit-learn. Core components including the encoder, decoder, adversarial discriminator, and multi-task classification heads, were developed as modular units to ensure reproducibility and to facilitate the controlled ablation studies described in section “Ablation study: architectural component contributions”. All experiments were conducted on a Dell Precision 7540 workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon E-2276 M processor, 128 GB RAM, and solid-state storage. Table 6 summarises the complete hyperparameter configuration for the proposed MT-AAE model.


#### Hyperparameter selection
  All hyperparameters were selected via a structured grid search on the validation set, using the composite loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}} = 0.3\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} + 0.6\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{class}} + 0.1\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GC}}$$\end{document} as the selection criterion. The weights reflect the relative importance of the three training objectives: classification is given the highest weight (0.6) because TRTS accuracy is the primary evaluation metric; reconstruction fidelity is weighted second (0.3) to preserve sequence-level validity; and GC-content alignment receives a smaller contribution (0.1) as an auxiliary biophysical constraint. Table 6 reports the values searched and the selected configuration.

  The latent dimension of 128 provided the best balance between representational capacity and training stability. Dimensions below 64 showed posterior collapse indicators, while dimensions above 128 offered no measurable TRTS improvement but increased training time by approximately 40%. The asymmetry in loss weights is deliberate: prioritising functional classification over reconstruction prevents the encoder from collapsing to a trivial identity mapping, a known failure mode in adversarial autoencoders trained on structured discrete data.

#### Class-specific performance analysis
  Class-wise precision and recall from the TRTS evaluation (Table 9) revealed the model’s discriminability across functional categories. The MT-AAE achieved a macro-averaged F1-score of 0.75 and a weighted-average F1-score of 0.75, indicating consistent functional preservation across the majority of classes.

  Performance was strongest for regulatory elements and sequences with well-defined motifs. Both promoters (n = 25) and tRNAs (n = 2) were recovered with 100% recall (F1-scores of 0.88 and 1.00, respectively), confirming that distinctive structural features such as TATA boxes and cloverleaf secondary structures are accurately regenerated. Introns maintained a strong F1-score (0.85), while enhancers achieved 0.71, reflecting effective but variable capture of their characteristic sequence signatures. For minority and structurally complex classes, performance revealed specific generation constraints. The rare snoRNA class (n = 3) exhibited perfect precision (1.00) but limited recall (0.33), indicating authentic generation that under-samples this low-abundance category. Protein-coding sequences showed perfect precision (1.00) but moderate recall (0.61), suggesting that while generated sequences are correctly identified as coding, some real coding sequences fall outside the generated distribution. Among moderately represented classes, ncRNA (n = 27) demonstrated the lowest F1-score (0.50), primarily due to recall limitations (0.48).

  Analysis of misclassification patterns provided further validation of the model’s biological fidelity. The primary confusion of snoRNAs with Pseudogenes likely reflects the conserved core motifs shared between active snoRNAs and their retrotransposed genomic remnants, a relationship well-documented in the evolutionary genomics literature [64]. The spectral overlap between ncRNA and the Biological Region class is consistent with inherent annotation ambiguity in genomic databases [65], where non-coding elements without a definitive functional assignment are frequently assigned to broad catch-all categories. The clean separation between tRNA and rRNA - two structurally distinct non-coding classes—confirms that the model encodes the fundamental biophysical differences between cloverleaf and stem-loop RNA architectures.

## Discussion
  A central challenge in generative genomics is progressing beyond statistical imitation of nucleotide frequencies toward acquisition of higher-order regulatory grammar. While recent architectures model local sequence statistics, they often fail to capture the long-range dependencies and functional semantics underlying biological viability. Our results indicate that the MT-AAE addresses this limitation, generating synthetic sequences that are thermodynamically consistent and functionally indistinguishable from natural human genes.

  In Sample 3, the generator produced a canonical TATA-box consensus (TATAAA) at 10 bp upstream of the functional core, within the −30 bp region relative to the transcription start site, without explicit rule encoding. At the population level, however, the motif was recovered across all 25 generated promoters (KS p = 0.90), indicating consistent compositional presence rather than statistically anchored positional placement. This differentiates the MT-AAE from Markovian or k-mer-based generators, which may reproduce motifs but rarely position them at biologically permissible distances [69]. This single-instance observation, corroborated by systematic 100% occurrence across all 25 generated promoters (section “Statistical motif enrichment across generated promoters”), indicates that adversarial training consistently recovers class-specific motifs at the sequence level. However, population-level KS testing showed non-significant positional clustering (p = 0.90), so the result reflects motif composition recovery rather than precise TSS-anchored placement. The high occurrence rate is also partly expected given the AT-rich composition of generated promoter sequences.

  The structure of the learned latent manifold provides further evidence for encoding of biological semantics. Confusion patterns in the TRTS evaluation between snoRNAs and pseudogenes reflect known evolutionary relationships [64] rather than generative error. Since snoRNAs are frequently retrotransposed as processed pseudogenes, the partial overlap indicates sensitivity to conserved sequence homology. Conversely, perfect separation between structurally distinct classes (e.g., tRNAs and rRNAs) demonstrates effective disentanglement where clear biophysical boundaries exist.

  Methodologically, the MT-AAE mitigates the mode-collapse pathology of standard GANs. Benchmarking showed that a baseline GAN, while producing syntactically valid DNA, failed to capture functional diversity (TRTS accuracy: 41.0%). By coupling adversarial learning with reconstruction and discriminative objectives, the MT-AAE enforces full data distribution coverage. Generation balance analysis quantitatively supports this, showing near-perfect parity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal}                                                   \usepackage{amsmath}                                                   \usepackage{wasysym}                                                   \usepackage{amsfonts}                                                   \usepackage{amssymb}                                                   \usepackage{amsbsy}                                                   \usepackage{mathrsfs}                                                   \usepackage{upgreek}                                                   \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt}                                                   \begin{document}$$R_g \approx 1.0$$\end{document}) even for rare classes like tRNAs ( < 2% of training data). This decoupling of generation quality from class prevalence enables effective genomic data augmentation, where rare but biologically significant variants are often underrepresented. As shown in subsection “Cross-species generalisation assessm” (Fig. 10), the human-trained MT-AAE does not generalise to Mus musculus sequences. The 35-fold increase in GC MAE and the near-zero TRTS accuracy indicate that the model has learned human-specific regulatory grammar rather than a universal sequence code. The GC distribution analysis (Fig. 10) reveals the primary cause: mouse RNA-seq data contain a large fraction of extremely GC-poor sequences (GC  <  10%), which are virtually absent in the human training distribution. The human-trained MT-AAE’s latent space and decoder were optimised to reproduce human GC biases; when applied to mouse inputs, the encoder maps them to out-of-distribution regions, and the decoder produces sequences that reflect human rather than mouse statistics. This failure is not simply a matter of k-mer composition (JSD = 0.355) but likely reflects deeper differences in promoter architecture, transcription factor binding site densities, and background nucleotide frequencies between the two species. ### Limitations and future directions
  Three limitations warrant consideration. First, the current architecture operates on fixed-length sequences (363 bp). Although alignment analyses demonstrate effective handling through contextual extension or core extraction, native variable-length generation remains an open challenge for sequences whose functional boundaries are inherently heterogeneous.

  Second, while the model captures cis-regulatory syntax, it does not incorporate higher-order chromatin organisation (e.g., topologically associating domains), which Sefer [39] and Sefer [40] have shown to be essential determinants of transcriptional competence in three-dimensional nuclear space.

  Third, cross-species validation on Mus musculus sequences confirmed that the MT-AAE is a species-specific model. Applying the human-trained model to mouse mammary RNA-seq data yielded a TRTS accuracy of 0.051 and a GC MAE of 0.349—approximately 35 times the human in-domain error—reflecting the well-documented divergence between human and murine non-coding regulatory elements. This specificity is expected and appropriate for human-focused applications. Cross-species generalisation would require fine-tuning on target-species data or a multi-species training corpus with species-conditioning; extending MT-AAE to species-conditioned generation represents a natural direction for future work.
