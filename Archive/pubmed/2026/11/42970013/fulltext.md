# Super-enhancers: regulatory mechanisms, biological functions, diseases, and therapeutic targets


## Abstract
  Super-enhancers (SEs) are operationally defined as clusters of cis-regulatory elements associated with high transcriptional activity and cell-identity genes. They display dense transcription factor and coactivator occupancy, strong H3K27 acetylation, and enhancer–promoter communication, but their annotation varies across platforms, algorithms, and thresholds. This review examines SE architecture, identification, and validation, distinguishing chromatin-based annotation from functional dependence demonstrated by locus-specific perturbations. Mechanisms of SE-associated transcription include pioneer factors, chromatin remodelers, three-dimensional genome organization, transcriptional condensates, non-coding RNAs, and extrachromosomal DNA. Physiological functions in development, immunity, and metabolic homeostasis are considered alongside context-dependent dysregulation in cancer, inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, neurological disorders, and metabolic diseases. Breast cancer provides a model for examining subtype-specific regulation and transcriptional plasticity. Particular attention is given to enhancer remodeling during endocrine therapy, cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibition, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted treatment, and chemotherapy, which may support bypass signaling, stemness, and persistent resistance. Causal regulatory changes must nevertheless be distinguished from correlates of treatment selection. Therapeutic approaches targeting bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) proteins, CDK7/9, chromatin regulators, enhancer RNAs, and regulatory loci are critically evaluated. Definitional uncertainty, model dependence, limited reproducibility, systemic toxicity, and insufficient clinical validation remain major barriers. Priorities include standardized annotation, temporally resolved perturbation, single-cell and spatial profiling, artificial intelligence-assisted integration, patient-derived models, and biomarker-guided therapeutic development. Together, these perspectives position SEs as a context-dependent framework linking regulatory architecture to disease progression and treatment resistance, while emphasizing the evidence required to establish therapeutic vulnerabilities.


## Introduction
  Precise control of gene expression relies on the coordinated actions of transcription factors, chromatin regulators and non-coding cis-regulatory elements that shape chromatin accessibility and three-dimensional genome organization [1–3]. The term super-enhancer was introduced in 2013 by studies identifying unusually large enhancer domains with dense occupancy of lineage-defining transcription factors and Mediator at genes central to cell identity [4, 5]. Super-enhancers (SEs) are generally described as clusters of constituent enhancers characterized by extensive cofactor recruitment and high levels of active chromatin modifications, particularly histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) [4, 5]. This concept has provided a useful framework for identifying regulatory domains with strong transcriptional activity and cell-type specificity. However, SEs are commonly classified using relative epigenomic signals and ranking algorithms rather than universal fixed biological criteria [6–8]. Because enhancer activity often varies along a quantitative continuum, SE calls are sensitive to experimental platform, peak-calling strategy, stitching distance, signal normalization and threshold selection [6–8]. Therefore, SEs should be interpreted as context-dependent regulatory domains whose biological relevance requires functional evaluation, rather than as a uniform discrete class of enhancers.

  Beyond their association with highly expressed genes, SEs have been implicated in lineage specification, embryonic development, immune regulation and metabolic homeostasis [5, 9–11]. Their activity is shaped by lineage-defining and signal-responsive transcription factors, enhancer–promoter communication, coactivator condensation and non-coding RNA-dependent regulation, allowing extracellular signals, chromatin states and nuclear organization to be integrated into cell-state-specific transcriptional outputs [4, 12–14]. Reconfiguration of these regulatory systems has been associated with cancer and other diseases where cellular identity or adaptive transcription is disrupted [5, 15]. Breast cancer provides an informative model where estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)-associated and triple-negative breast cancer-specific SE programs have been identified and functionally investigated [16, 17]. In contrast, ERBB2-associated enhancers have been reported, but the classification as a functionally validated ERBB2-associated SE and its remodeling during human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted therapy remain less well established [18]. These observations illustrate an important distinction: disease-associated SE signatures may identify altered transcriptional states but do not necessarily establish the regulatory dependencies required to maintain the corresponding phenotype.

  Despite substantial progress, several conceptual and translational challenges continue to limit the interpretation of SE biology. Many SE annotations and SE–gene assignments remain correlative and require validation through locus-specific genetic or epigenetic perturbation, physiologically relevant models and clinically annotated datasets [6–8, 19, 20]. Moreover, pharmacological approaches directed at SE-associated transcription generally engage broadly used chromatin and transcriptional regulators, raising concerns regarding selectivity, systemic toxicity and therapeutic windows [15, 21]. Emerging approaches—including single-cell and spatial multi-omics, chromatin conformation analysis, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based perturbation and computational integration—provide opportunities to refine SE annotation and identify context-specific regulatory dependencies [8, 12, 19, 20, 22–25]. An integrated assessment is therefore needed to distinguish descriptive SE maps from functionally validated regulatory dependencies, reconcile evidence across physiological and disease contexts, and critically evaluate the translational potential of SE-associated vulnerabilities. In this review, we first examine the structural, functional and methodological frameworks for SE identification, target-gene assignment and functional validation across complementary experimental models. We then discuss the molecular mechanisms and physiological functions of SEs, their dysregulation across human diseases—with in-depth consideration of breast cancer—and the evidence and limitations surrounding therapeutic intervention. Finally, we integrate therapeutic targeting with treatment-induced SE reprogramming and resistance, critically appraising current translational barriers and prioritizing actionable strategies to render SE research more reproducible, mechanistically grounded, and clinically informative.


## Structural, functional, and methodological framework of SEs

### Structural organization and molecular features of SEs
  Super-enhancers (SEs) are operationally defined regulatory domains comprising clusters of enhancer elements with exceptionally high aggregate occupancy by transcription factors, coactivators, and strong enrichment of active chromatin marks, most commonly H3K27ac [4, 5, 7, 8, 26] (Fig. 1). They are not specified by a unique DNA sequence, fixed size or invariant molecular composition. In commonly used annotation workflows, nearby candidate enhancers are stitched and ranked according to their aggregate chromatin signal. The resulting domains generally exhibit broader and stronger regulatory signals than typical enhancers, although their inferred boundaries depend on the experimental assay, biological context and analytical parameters. SEs should therefore be regarded as a context-dependent operational category rather than a fixed anatomical feature of the genome.   At the molecular level, SEs typically encompass accessible chromatin, dense clusters of transcription-factor binding sites, active histone modifications, RNA polymerase occupancy, enhancer transcription and long-range contacts with target promoters [27]. Mediator complex subunit 1 (MED1), bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) and other cofactors can accumulate at these domains and engage in multivalent interactions that concentrate components of the transcriptional machinery [13, 28, 29]. Such condensate-like organization may facilitate transcription at selected loci, but it is neither universal nor sufficient to define an SE. Constituent elements are also functionally heterogeneous. Genetic dissection has identified classical enhancers with intrinsic activity, partially redundant or hierarchically organized elements, and facilitators that augment neighboring enhancers without acting as strong autonomous activators [6, 30, 31]. Accordingly, domain size or chromatin-signal intensity alone cannot establish which constituent elements are functionally indispensable. An SE is therefore better viewed as a context-dependent regulatory ensemble than as a uniform collection of equivalent enhancers. Its transcriptional output reflects the combined effects of local element composition, cofactor availability, three-dimensional genome organization and promoter responsiveness. This architecture may confer transcriptional robustness in some contexts while creating dependence on a limited subset of regulatory elements in others.


### Functional properties and biological roles of SEs
  SEs are frequently associated with genes that establish cell identity and support high-output or rapidly inducible transcriptional programs [4, 5]. This association, however, should not be equated with functional dependence. Their regulatory importance extends beyond transcriptional magnitude and may reflect their capacity to integrate lineage-defining transcription factors with developmental, hormonal, inflammatory and metabolic signals [9–11]. At some loci, clustered enhancers provide transcriptional robustness through partially redundant inputs; at others, cooperative or hierarchical interactions confer heightened responsiveness to changes in signaling or transcription-factor availability [6, 30, 31]. These behaviors are context dependent and cannot be inferred from H3K27ac intensity or domain size alone. Regulatory output is further shaped by promoter compatibility, enhancer–promoter contact frequency and the insulating properties of the surrounding chromatin domain [12, 27, 32]. Consequently, similarly annotated SEs may exert distinct quantitative or gene-specific effects across cell types and physiological states.

  Functional testing is therefore essential for distinguishing chromatin-defined SEs from regulatory dependencies. Constituent-element deletion can produce effects ranging from minimal changes to near-complete loss of target-gene expression, depending on the degree of redundancy and hierarchical organization within the domain [6, 30, 31]. Endogenous deletion, CRISPR interference and locus-specific epigenome editing can test the necessity of a candidate SE or individual constituent, whereas activation-based approaches can help assess sufficiency [20, 32]. These perturbations should be combined with transcriptional measurements and chromatin-contact analysis to distinguish direct target genes from genes associated only by genomic proximity. In normal tissues, functionally supported SE-associated circuits contribute to development, immune-cell specification and metabolic homeostasis [5, 9–11]. In disease, oncogenic transcription factors, altered chromatin regulators and aberrant signaling can redirect the same regulatory logic toward programs that support proliferation, lineage plasticity and survival [15, 33]. SE maps can therefore identify cell-state markers and candidate vulnerabilities, but annotation alone does not establish a therapeutic target. Translational relevance requires evidence that the dependency is causal, maintained in clinically relevant models and sufficiently selective to be separated from broadly required transcriptional processes.


### Methodological framework for SE identification and functional validation
  SE analysis involves three distinct tasks: nominating candidate domains, assigning their target genes, and testing regulatory function. Chromatin activity and cofactor occupancy support the initial annotation; transcriptional and three-dimensional genome data then narrow the possible target genes. Genetic or epigenetic perturbation is required to determine whether the candidate domain is necessary or sufficient. Cell lines, organoids, animal models, and patient-derived material address different aspects of biological and translational relevance. The subsections below follow this sequence and then consider the limitations that affect reproducibility.


#### Integrated approaches for SE identification, target-gene assignment, and functional validation
  The identification and characterization of SEs have been enabled by advances in high-throughput genomic and epigenomic technologies that capture chromatin states, regulatory activity and three-dimensional genome organization (Fig. 2). Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq) remains the most widely used approach for SE annotation, with histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) serving as a key marker of active enhancer regions [34] (Table 1). Computational frameworks, most notably the Rank Ordering of Super-Enhancers (ROSE) algorithm, integrate ChIP–seq signal intensity and genomic proximity to distinguish SEs from typical enhancers. Complementary assays, including assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC–seq), provide information on chromatin accessibility and facilitate the identification of regulatory regions permissive for transcription factor binding [38]. More recently, low-input chromatin profiling methods such as cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN) and cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) have improved the resolution and sensitivity of chromatin mapping, enabling the profiling of histone modifications and transcription factor occupancy from limited cellular material [36, 37].   The regulatory architecture of SEs is closely linked to higher-order chromatin organization. Chromosome conformation capture–based technologies have provided important insights into the spatial interactions that connect SEs with their target genes [41]. High-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) has enabled genome-wide mapping of chromatin interactions, whereas Capture Hi-C increases resolution at selected genomic loci [40]. More targeted approaches, including Hi-C coupled with chromatin immunoprecipitation (HiChIP) and chromatin interaction analysis with paired-end tag sequencing (ChIA–PET), combine chromatin interaction mapping with protein-centric enrichment strategies to identify enhancer–promoter contacts associated with specific regulatory factors [42]. These studies have demonstrated that SEs frequently function within three-dimensional regulatory domains, where long-range chromatin interactions contribute to the coordination of gene expression programs [32].

  Recent advances in single-cell and multimodal technologies have further refined the investigation of SE biology by enabling the analysis of regulatory landscapes at cellular resolution [48]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides insights into transcriptional heterogeneity, whereas single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (scATAC-seq) enables the characterization of cell-specific chromatin accessibility profiles [23, 49]. The integration of transcriptomic and epigenomic measurements through multi-omics platforms has facilitated the direct linking of chromatin states to gene expression within individual cells. In parallel, spatial transcriptomics preserves tissue architecture while capturing gene expression patterns in situ, providing additional context for the investigation of SE-associated regulatory networks. Collectively, the integration of chromatin profiling, functional interrogation, three-dimensional genome mapping and single-cell multi-omics has established a comprehensive framework for defining SE landscapes and elucidating their regulatory functions in both physiological and pathological settings [50].


#### Experimental models for mechanistic and translational validation
  Experimental models are required to determine whether annotated SEs represent functional regulatory dependencies and whether these dependencies persist across biological contexts. Two-dimensional (2D) cultures remain the most accessible platform for chromatin profiling, locus-specific perturbation, and high-throughput drug screening [51, 52] (Fig. 2). They enable controlled comparison of untreated, genetically modified, and therapy-exposed states. However, prolonged culture can select stable subclones, while the absence of tissue architecture, stromal interactions, and physiological gradients limits analysis of microenvironment-dependent or treatment-induced SE remodeling [53]. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) provide greater biological complexity by preserving aspects of tumor architecture, patient-specific genomic features, and treatment response [53–55]. They are also compatible with longitudinal drug exposure and molecular profiling. Nevertheless, conventional PDOs incompletely reproduce immune, vascular, stromal, and systemic endocrine inputs, and their regulatory states can be influenced by culture media and extracellular matrix [56].

  Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) allow SE-associated mechanisms to be examined within intact tissues and defined genetic backgrounds [57–59]. Temporal or lineage-restricted perturbation can distinguish effects on tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Their principal limitation is species specificity, because enhancer sequences, transcription factor occupancy, and chromatin organization may differ between mouse and human tissues [60]. Patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) complement GEMMs by maintaining human material in vivo and preserving clinically relevant genomic heterogeneity and selected regulatory programs [61–65]. However, conventional PDXs require immunodeficient hosts. Human stromal components are progressively replaced by murine cells, and engraftment may select aggressive subclones, thereby altering tumor-microenvironment interactions and underrepresenting intratumoral diversity [61, 66].

  Each model answers a different question. Two-dimensional cultures are well suited to controlled perturbation, PDOs retain more patient-specific architecture, GEMMs allow lineage- and time-restricted studies in intact tissues, and PDXs preserve human tumor material in vivo. A convincing validation strategy should use these systems in a complementary sequence rather than treat any one of them as definitive. Agreement across models strengthens causal inference; disagreement may expose a dependency on species, microenvironment, treatment schedule, or clonal composition [65, 67]. Model origin, passage history, cellular composition, and treatment conditions should therefore accompany every SE analysis.


#### Challenges and limitations in SE identification and annotation
  Despite substantial advances in genomic technologies and computational methodologies, the identification and annotation of SEs remain subject to several conceptual and technical challenges. Most current approaches rely on epigenomic features, particularly H3K27ac enrichment, to distinguish SEs from typical enhancers [37]. However, SE annotation is highly dependent on the analytical framework employed. The widely used Rank Ordering of Super-Enhancers (ROSE) algorithm defines SEs by stitching adjacent enhancer elements and ranking them according to signal intensity, making SE calls sensitive to parameters such as stitching distance and threshold selection [8]. Consequently, different analytical settings can generate substantially different SE landscapes, limiting reproducibility and cross-study comparisons. Moreover, enhancer activity frequently exists along a continuum, raising ongoing debate as to whether SEs represent a distinct class of regulatory elements or the upper end of a quantitative enhancer spectrum [20].

  The increasing application of single-cell and multimodal technologies has provided new opportunities to investigate SE biology while introducing additional analytical complexity. Single-cell datasets are inherently sparse and susceptible to technical variability, complicating the accurate identification of regulatory elements [47]. In tumor samples, cellular heterogeneity, variable tumor purity and the presence of stromal and immune populations can further confound the interpretation of SE activity [68]. Robust normalization, batch-effect correction and cell-type deconvolution strategies are therefore essential for resolving cell-specific regulatory programs [43].

  Additional challenges arise from the integration of diverse omics datasets. Linking chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, chromatin interactions and transcriptional outputs remains computationally demanding and often depends on platform-specific analytical assumptions [69]. Furthermore, SE landscapes are highly dynamic and can be remodeled during development, differentiation and disease progression [70]. Future efforts should focus on establishing standardized criteria for SE annotation, incorporating functional perturbation data into enhancer classification and developing integrative frameworks capable of capturing regulatory dynamics across biological contexts [71]. Such advances will improve the reproducibility and biological interpretability of SE studies and facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of enhancer-mediated gene regulation [20].


## Dynamic regulatory mechanisms governing SE activity
  Mapping a candidate SE does not explain how the domain forms, persists, or changes with cell state. Those processes involve transcription factors, chromatin remodelers, three-dimensional genome architecture, non-coding RNAs, and, in some cancers, extrachromosomal DNA. Transcriptional condensates may contribute at selected loci, but their role should be distinguished from the broader effects of cofactor occupancy and chromatin contact. The following sections therefore separate mechanisms tested by perturbation from associations inferred mainly from spatial proximity or chromatin enrichment.


### Pioneer factors, chromatin remodelers, and the epigenetic priming of SEs
  The establishment of many SEs involves the coordinated actions of pioneer transcription factors and chromatin-remodeling machinery that collectively convert inaccessible chromatin into transcriptionally permissive regulatory domains [72, 73] (Fig. 3). Pioneer factors can engage nucleosomal DNA within relatively inaccessible chromatin and thereby facilitate the recruitment of additional transcriptional regulators. In several epithelial and hormone-responsive lineages, FOXA1 represents a prototypical pioneer factor that promotes chromatin accessibility by displacing nucleosomes and enabling the binding of lineage-defining transcription factors and coactivator complexes.   The formation and stabilization of SEs are reinforced through extensive epigenetic remodeling. Histone acetyltransferases such as p300 and CREB-binding protein (CBP) catalyze the deposition of histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac), a hallmark of active enhancers that facilitates coactivator recruitment and sustains an open chromatin state [76, 77]. The accumulation of H3K27ac further promotes transcription factor occupancy, generating a feed-forward regulatory circuit that amplifies enhancer activity and contributes to the emergence of SEs [78].

  In luminal breast cancer, SE establishment is closely linked to the coordinated activity of estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), FOXA1 and GATA3 [17, 79]. ERα functions as a central transcriptional regulator that recruits coactivators and chromatin modifiers to hormone-responsive regulatory elements, whereas FOXA1 enhances chromatin accessibility and facilitates ERα binding [80, 81]. GATA3 further contributes to the maintenance of luminal transcriptional programs by stabilizing enhancer–promoter interactions and higher-order chromatin organization. Together, these factors form interconnected regulatory networks that sustain lineage identity while promoting oncogenic transcriptional programs. Dysregulation of pioneer factor activity can remodel SE landscapes and redirect transcriptional outputs, thereby contributing to tumor initiation, progression and therapeutic adaptation. These observations illustrate how pioneer factors and epigenetic remodeling contribute to SE architecture and context-specific gene expression programs.


### Spatial genome organization and phase separation in SE function
  The regulatory activity of SEs is closely linked to higher-order chromatin organization, which facilitates communication between distal regulatory elements and their target genes. SEs are frequently located within topologically associating domains (TADs), self-interacting chromosomal regions that constrain enhancer–promoter interactions and contribute to regulatory specificity [12]. Architectural proteins, including CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) and cohesin, play central roles in establishing and maintaining these chromatin domains by mediating long-range looping interactions between SEs and target promoters [82, 83]. Through this three-dimensional organization, SEs can coordinate the expression of lineage-defining and oncogenic genes while minimizing inappropriate regulatory contacts [84].

  Disruption of chromatin architecture can profoundly alter SE function. Structural variations, deletions or rearrangements affecting TAD boundaries may generate aberrant enhancer–promoter interactions, a phenomenon often referred to as enhancer hijacking [85, 86]. In T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, for example, disruption of domain boundaries enables ectopic interactions between distal regulatory elements and the MYC locus, resulting in oncogenic transcriptional activation [87, 88]. These observations highlight the importance of chromatin topology in maintaining the fidelity of SE-mediated gene regulation.

  In addition to chromatin looping, increasing evidence supports a role for biomolecular phase separation in SE function [89, 90]. SE-associated coactivators, including BRD4 and MED1, contain intrinsically disordered regions that facilitate multivalent molecular interactions and may promote the formation of dynamic transcriptional condensates [13]. These condensates concentrate transcription factors, Mediator complexes and RNA polymerase II within discrete nuclear compartments, thereby enhancing the efficiency of transcriptional initiation and elongation [29]. Unlike static regulatory structures, phase-separated condensates exhibit dynamic assembly and disassembly in response to cellular and environmental cues, providing a mechanism for rapid modulation of gene expression. The functional significance of phase separation is further supported by pharmacological studies demonstrating that bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) inhibitors, such as JQ1, disrupt BRD4-associated condensates and preferentially suppress SE-driven transcriptional programs [13]. Conversely, genetic alterations that enhance condensate formation may reinforce aberrant transcriptional activity and contribute to malignant phenotypes. Together, chromatin topology and condensate-like regulatory assemblies provide complementary mechanisms through which SEs coordinate context-dependent transcription in normal physiology and disease.


### Non-coding RNAs in SE-mediated transcriptional regulation
  Increasing evidence indicates that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are integral components of SE-mediated regulatory networks, contributing to chromatin organization, transcriptional regulation and cellular state maintenance. Among these, enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) are transient transcripts generated from active SE regions that have emerged as important modulators of enhancer function [14, 91]. Although some eRNAs may reflect enhancer activity, accumulating evidence indicates that selected eRNAs can contribute functionally to enhancer-promoter communication [92]. In selected contexts, interactions between eRNAs and components of the cohesin and Mediator complexes have been reported to reinforce higher-order chromatin architecture, thereby supporting efficient transcriptional activation of SE-associated target genes. In breast cancer, eRNAs derived from ESR1-associated SEs contribute to estrogen-responsive chromatin interactions and sustained transcriptional activity, suggesting a potential contribution to endocrine adaptation in selected luminal breast cancer models [93].

  Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) provide an additional layer of SE regulation through their ability to function as molecular scaffolds that coordinate protein complexes and chromatin-modifying enzymes [94]. Accumulation of lncRNAs at regulatory loci can facilitate the assembly of transcriptional machinery, modulate local chromatin states and influence enhancer activity [95]. Some SE-associated lncRNAs have also been shown to regulate distal genomic regions through long-range chromatin interactions [96]. For example, the SE-derived lncRNA CCAT1-L promotes chromatin looping and enhances MYC transcription in colorectal cancer, illustrating how lncRNAs can extend the regulatory influence of SEs beyond their local genomic environment [97, 98]. In addition, interactions between lncRNAs and epigenetic regulators, including Polycomb-associated proteins, contribute to the dynamic remodeling of transcriptional programs in both normal and malignant cells.

  Collectively, eRNAs and lncRNAs function as important regulatory components linking chromatin architecture, epigenetic regulation and transcriptional control [99]. Through their ability to coordinate enhancer activity and regulatory complex assembly, ncRNAs contribute to the establishment and maintenance of cell-type-specific gene expression programs [100]. Dysregulation of ncRNA–SE circuits has been implicated in tumor progression, metastasis and therapeutic resistance, suggesting that these regulatory interactions may represent potential targets for therapeutic intervention [101].


### ecDNA-associated SEs and oncogenic transcriptional amplification
  Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) has emerged as an important mechanism of oncogene activation in cancer [102, 103]. These circular DNA elements can harbor oncogenes together with enhancer or SE-like regulatory elements, creating highly active regulatory units that drive transcriptional amplification [104]. Unlike chromosomal SEs, which are generally constrained by local chromatin architecture and topological boundaries, ecDNA-associated SEs can engage in extensive regulatory interactions that facilitate high levels of oncogene expression [105]. The clustering of ecDNA molecules within the nucleus further promotes the local accumulation of transcription factors, coactivators and RNA polymerase II, thereby enhancing transcriptional output [106].

  The ecDNA–SE axis also contributes to tumor evolution and therapeutic resistance [107, 108]. Dynamic changes in ecDNA copy number enable rapid adaptation to selective pressures and promote transcriptional heterogeneity [109]. Recent studies suggest that ecDNA-driven transcriptional programs exhibit heightened dependence on SE-associated regulatory machinery, which may create sensitivity to BET and (cyclin-dependent kinase 7) CDK7 inhibition in selected models [110]. However, ecDNA amplification and structural reorganization may provide alternative routes to therapeutic escape [111]. These findings identify ecDNA-associated SEs as a distinct mechanism of transcriptional dysregulation and a potential target for therapeutic intervention in cancers characterized by oncogene addiction.


## Biological functions of SEs in development and tissue homeostasis
  The mechanisms described above matter biologically only when they alter cell-state-specific transcription. In normal tissues, SE-associated circuits have been linked to developmental transitions, lineage specification, immune responses, and metabolic homeostasis. Their activity changes with developmental stage, cell identity, and environmental input. The examples below distinguish functional evidence from chromatin association, because an SE annotation alone does not establish that a regulatory domain is necessary for a physiological process.


### SEs in lineage specification and cell identity maintenance
  SEs are preferentially associated with genes that control cellular identity, lineage commitment and tissue-specific function [112] (Fig. 4). By integrating the activity of lineage-defining transcription factors, coactivators and chromatin regulators, SEs help establish transcriptional programs that support stable cell-state maintenance [113].   A characteristic feature of SE-mediated regulation is the formation of interconnected autoregulatory and feed-forward circuits involving lineage-specifying transcription factors [115]. Such networks reinforce transcriptional memory and sustain the expression of identity-defining genes, thereby preserving lineage fidelity during development and tissue homeostasis [116]. In parallel, SEs coordinate chromatin accessibility with context-dependent transcriptional activation, enabling the establishment and maintenance of specialized cellular functions [117].

  Disruption of SE activity can alter lineage-specific transcriptional programs and destabilize cellular identity [118]. Aberrant remodeling of SE landscapes has been linked to defective differentiation, inappropriate cell-state transitions and disease-associated transcriptional reprogramming [119, 120]. Together, these observations indicate that SEs contribute to lineage specification, transcriptional stability and tissue homeostasis across diverse biological contexts.


### SEs in embryonic development and tissue patterning
  Building on their roles in lineage specification, SE landscapes can be dynamically remodeled as embryonic cells transition between developmental states. By concentrating lineage-determining transcription factors and transcriptional coactivators at key developmental loci, SEs support high-level and context-dependent expression of genes involved in germ-layer specification, morphogenesis and tissue patterning [121, 122].

  During early Xenopus development, the maternal transcription factors Foxi2 and Sox3 bind cis-regulatory modules before gastrulation and subsequently facilitate EP300 recruitment and H3K27ac deposition. These regulatory modules are preferentially enriched within ectoderm-specific SEs, where they promote robust activation of ectodermal gene-expression programs during germ-layer specification [123]. In mouse cranial neural crest cells, a distal regulatory region containing multiple SEs establishes long-range inter-TAD interactions with the Hoxa cluster. Two components of this region, HIRE1 and HIRE2, regulate Hoxa2 and Hoxa3 expression in spatially defined cranial neural crest populations, thereby contributing to pharyngeal-arch and craniofacial patterning [124].

  SE-associated regulation also contributes to organogenesis. During mouse cardiac development, the Upperhand (Uph) lncRNA locus overlaps the Hand2-associated SE. Transcription through this locus, rather than the mature Uph transcript itself, was reported to maintain active enhancer chromatin and RNA polymerase II occupancy, thereby supporting Hand2 expression. Premature termination of Uph transcription reduced Hand2 expression and caused right-ventricular hypoplasia and embryonic lethality, although subsequent studies suggested additional contributions from local DNA regulatory elements [125]. In mouse trophoblast stem cells, SE profiling identified a core regulatory network involving CDX2, GATA3 and TEAD4, which is dynamically remodeled during trophoblast differentiation and placental lineage specification [9].

  These developmental examples do not support SEs as autonomous timing devices. Instead, they show that specific enhancer assemblies coordinate transcription-factor occupancy, active chromatin, and long-range contacts at defined loci and developmental stages. Disruption of these regulatory architectures can impair lineage specification and tissue morphogenesis, providing a mechanistic link between SE dysfunction and congenital developmental abnormalities.


### SEs in immune cell activation and functional plasticity
  In the immune system, SEs regulate both activation dynamics and functional plasticity [126]. Following T cell receptor (TCR) stimulation, cytokine-associated SEs at loci such as IL2 and IFNG acquire increased H3K27ac, supporting rapid transcriptional activation during T cell effector responses [87, 127]. This response is associated with the recruitment of stimulus-responsive transcription factors, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), activator protein 1 (AP-1) and nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) [128].

  In regulatory T cells (Tregs), SEs maintain lineage-defining FOXP3 expression, whereas these regulatory elements are less active or epigenetically constrained in conventional effector T cells, supporting lineage segregation within CD4 + T cell subsets [129, 130]. This differential SE activity contributes to the establishment and maintenance of immune cell identity [131].

  Immune-cell plasticity also involves stimulus-dependent enhancer remodeling. LPS exposure increases activity at pro-inflammatory loci such as TNF and IL6 in macrophage models [132], whereas IL-4 promotes regulatory regions associated with tissue repair, including Arg1 and Mrc1 [133]. Under chronic inflammatory conditions, SE configurations can become stably biased toward pro-inflammatory states, contributing to sustained immune activation and disease persistence [134]. These examples show how enhancer use follows cellular state, but the extent to which each remodeled domain meets functional SE criteria must be established separately [135].


### SEs in metabolic homeostasis and environmental adaptation
  SEs contribute to metabolic homeostasis by integrating nutrient, hormonal and environmental cues with tissue-specific transcriptional programs [11, 136]. In pancreatic β-cells, glucose-responsive SEs at the insulin locus are occupied by lineage-defining transcription factors, including PDX1 and MAFA, thereby supporting insulin expression and secretion in response to elevated glucose levels [137, 138]. In hepatocytes, fasting-associated SEs at gluconeogenic genes such as G6PC and PCK1 are activated through CREB- and FOXO1-dependent pathways, promoting hepatic glucose production during nutrient deprivation [139].

  Environmental stress can reshape metabolic enhancer programs. Cold exposure activates β-adrenergic signaling in brown adipose tissue and increases activity at thermogenic loci, including UCP1 and PGC1α, through PRDM16-associated complexes [140, 141]. This response supports heat production and energy expenditure [142]. Chronic overnutrition, by contrast, can increase enhancer activity at inflammatory loci such as CCL2 and IL1β in adipose tissue [143]. Such changes may contribute to low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction. SE-associated regulation thus connects environmental signals with tissue-specific transcription, but causality must be evaluated at individual loci [144].


## SE dysregulation in human diseases
  The regulatory properties that support normal cell identity and physiological adaptation can become pathogenic when they are redirected by genetic lesions, altered signaling, or epigenetic dysregulation. Disease-associated SEs may activate aberrant transcriptional programs, reinforce maladaptive cell states, or increase dependence on specific regulatory factors. These effects remain strongly context dependent and should not be inferred solely from elevated H3K27ac or cofactor occupancy. This section examines the mechanisms and evidence underlying SE dysregulation in cancer and in immune, neurological, and metabolic diseases.


### General principles of oncogenic SE activation and rewiring
  Oncogenic SE states can emerge through transcription-factor redistribution, epigenetic remodeling, altered three-dimensional genome organization, and genetic alterations. Lineage-defining or oncogenic transcription factors may occupy pre-existing enhancers, recruit coactivators, and strengthen or assemble aberrant regulatory domains [15, 33, 100]. Oncogenic factors and altered architectural proteins can also reorganize enhancer–promoter contacts, thereby redirecting regulatory activity toward growth-promoting genes [84, 90]. Genetic lesions provide a distinct route. Structural variants can juxtapose active enhancers with proto-oncogenes, whereas focal or extrachromosomal amplification can co-amplify oncogenes and their regulatory elements [85, 145, 146]. On ecDNA, intermolecular enhancer–gene interactions within nuclear hubs may further increase oncogene transcription [104, 105]. Enhancer hijacking or co-amplification, however, should not automatically be equated with SE formation; this classification requires locus-resolved evidence of clustered regulatory activity. Although mechanistically distinct, these processes can converge on sustained or inducible expression of genes that promote proliferation, survival, and lineage plasticity.

  Oncogenic SE states are further shaped by lineage and environmental context. Extrinsic and oncogenic signaling can alter transcription-factor occupancy, cofactor recruitment, and chromatin accessibility without changing the underlying DNA sequence [33, 100, 147]. These inputs may activate previously inactive elements, strengthen existing enhancer clusters, or redirect regulatory contacts toward alternative target genes. Multivalent coactivator interactions and aberrant chromatin looping can reinforce selected oncogenic programs [13, 29, 90]. However, condensate-like behavior and strong H3K27ac enrichment are neither universal features nor sufficient evidence of causal dependency. The functional consequence of an annotated SE depends on promoter compatibility, three-dimensional topology, and the requirement for its target gene within the relevant tumor lineage.

  This distinction is essential when interpreting cancer epigenomes. A tumor-enriched SE may reflect oncogenic transcription, copy-number alteration, or differences in cellular composition without constituting a selective vulnerability. Establishing causality requires copy-number-aware chromatin analysis, locus-resolved target-gene assignment, and endogenous perturbation of the regulatory element, ideally followed by phenotypic rescue [8, 20, 32, 146]. Validation across cell lines, patient-derived models, and normal cellular counterparts is also needed to determine the persistence and selectivity of the dependency. Breast cancer provides an informative setting because its molecular subtypes couple distinct lineage and receptor-signaling programs to different enhancer landscapes. Functional evidence is comparatively strong for selected ERα-associated and triple-negative breast cancer circuits, whereas proposed HER2-associated SE dependencies remain less comprehensively validated [16–18, 147–149]. The following sections therefore use breast cancer to compare established regulatory dependencies with candidate associations for which causal evidence remains incomplete.


### Breast cancer as a model of subtype-specific SE regulation
  Breast cancer is examined in depth here because its major molecular subtypes provide a comparatively well-defined system for relating lineage factors, hormone-receptor and receptor-tyrosine-kinase signaling, tumor microenvironmental cues and treatment adaptation to distinct regulatory states [148]. Functional perturbation and multi-omic studies have identified selected SE dependencies in luminal and triple-negative models, while the evidence for other proposed circuits remains more limited. Breast cancer therefore provides a useful framework for comparing the strength and context dependence of SE evidence across luminal, HER2-positive and triple-negative disease [16, 17, 147, 149].


#### ERα-associated enhancer and SE circuits in luminal breast cancer
  In luminal breast cancer, estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), encoded by ESR1, operates within a lineage-specific regulatory network comprising pioneer factors, coactivators, and distal regulatory elements. Following estrogen stimulation, ERα is recruited to a subset of accessible chromatin, with its occupancy shaped by local DNA sequence, cooperating transcription factors, and chromatin context [80, 150]. This regulatory landscape is not invariant. Profiling of primary breast tumors revealed substantial interpatient heterogeneity in ERα binding, whereas the most recurrent sites exhibited stronger enhancer activity and enrichment for breast cancer risk variants [151] (Fig. 5).   The BRD4–ERα–RET circuit provides a functionally characterized example. Estrogen-induced BRD4 occupancy at ERα-associated SEs activates a group of estrogen-responsive genes, including RET. RET signaling, in turn, enhances ERα phosphorylation and transcriptional activity, establishing a positive feedback loop in selected ER-positive models [17]. Estrogen stimulation also induces transcription at numerous ERα-bound enhancers [150]. However, eRNA detection primarily indicates regulatory activity rather than functional necessity. At selected loci, eRNA depletion impairs enhancer–promoter communication and estrogen-responsive target-gene activation, supporting locus-specific functions for these transcripts [152]. Similarly, ERα occupancy, H3K27ac enrichment, and eRNA abundance alone cannot establish SE dependency or identify the direct target promoter. Endogenous perturbation of individual constituents, combined with transcriptional and chromatin-contact analyses, is required to demonstrate causality [20, 32]. Luminal breast cancer thus illustrates how hormone-responsive enhancers integrate lineage identity with reversible signal-dependent transcription, while emphasizing that candidate SE dependencies must be resolved at the locus level and validated across clinically relevant models.


#### ERBB2-associated enhancer regulation in HER2-positive breast cancer
  HER2-positive breast cancers commonly exhibit ERBB2 amplification and high HER2 expression, but the structure of the 17q12 amplicon and its transcriptional output vary across tumors [153]. Regulatory elements have been identified within and around the ERBB2 locus. CRISPR-mediated deletion of Her2-Enhancer1, a candidate expressed enhancer located within the ERBB2 gene body, altered HER2 expression and downstream signaling in breast cancer cells [18]. These findings support enhancer-mediated regulation of ERBB2 but do not establish a uniformly present or functionally required ERBB2-associated SE. Moreover, because Her2-Enhancer1 is intragenic, deletion-based experiments may confound enhancer activity with disruption of local gene structure. Orthogonal approaches that preserve the underlying DNA sequence are therefore needed. ERBB2 amplification alone is also insufficient to establish SE status because increased DNA abundance can elevate aggregate chromatin-profiling signals. Copy-number-aware normalization is required before H3K27ac or cofactor enrichment is interpreted as regulatory activity.

  A rigorous model of ERBB2 regulation therefore requires several layers of evidence. Chromatin profiling should define the active elements and their boundaries, whereas three-dimensional contact data should establish physical linkage to the ERBB2 promoter. Endogenous CRISPR interference, epigenome editing, and combinatorial perturbation are needed to test the necessity of individual elements and the broader regulatory domain [8, 20, 32]. These analyses should include multiple HER2-positive models with different amplicon architectures, hormone-receptor states, and genomic backgrounds [153], together with nonmalignant mammary controls. Current evidence therefore more firmly establishes ERBB2 as an amplification-associated oncogene subject to enhancer regulation than as a universally SE-dependent locus. Therapy-induced regulatory adaptation is considered separately in Section “Adaptive SE rewiring during HER2-targeted therapy”.


#### Subtype- and cell-state-specific SE circuitries in triple-negative breast cancer
  Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) comprises molecularly and clinically heterogeneous tumors [148]. An initial transcriptomic classification identified six TNBC subtypes [154], which was subsequently refined to the TNBCtype −4 framework—basal-like 1 (BL1), basal-like 2 (BL2), mesenchymal (M) and luminal androgen receptor (LAR)—after immune and stromal signals were recognized as major contributors to the previously defined immunomodulatory and mesenchymal stem-like groups [155]. These tumor-intrinsic categories differ in cell-cycle activity, DNA-damage responses, growth-factor signaling, mesenchymal programs and androgen receptor dependence [155]. However, subtype assignments can vary with cohort composition, sampling strategy and analytical framework [148, 155]. Mapping these transcriptomic categories onto reproducible SE landscapes remains incomplete. Available multi-omic studies instead indicate substantial intertumoral and cell-state-associated SE heterogeneity rather than a single regulatory program shared across TNBC [16, 149].

  The strongest functional evidence comes from integrated H3K27ac and transcriptomic profiling combined with CRISPR-mediated perturbation, which identified FOXC1 and MET as genes regulated by TNBC-specific SEs and provided additional evidence for SE-associated regulation of ANLN [16]. Subsequent studies have linked TWEAK–Fn14-dependent SE reprogramming to metabolic and metastatic phenotypes [147]. Complementary CRISPR screens of transcribed SEs have identified regulatory elements required for tumor progression in selected TNBC models [19]. These findings support specific, model-dependent SE dependencies but do not establish uniform dependence across BL1, BL2, M and LAR tumors or across patient populations. Immune-associated SE signals should likewise be interpreted cautiously because bulk profiling may conflate tumor-cell-intrinsic enhancer activity with signals contributed by infiltrating immune and stromal cells [155]. TNBC should therefore be viewed as comprising multiple, context-dependent SE circuitries whose therapeutic relevance requires subtype-resolved profiling, locus-specific perturbation and validation in clinically annotated cohorts.


#### Tumor microenvironment-associated enhancer remodeling and candidate SE regulation in breast cancer
  The tumor microenvironment can alter enhancer-associated transcription through paracrine signaling, metabolic stress and spatially restricted cellular interactions [156]. In breast cancer stroma, JUN-dependent enhancer reprogramming has been shown to maintain cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) activation and promote tumor-cell invasion through non-cell-autonomous mechanisms [157]. Other studies implicate FGFR signaling and fibroblast-derived immunoregulatory programs in shaping tumor–stromal interactions [158, 159], but these observations do not by themselves establish SE formation or a tumor-cell-intrinsic SE dependency. Pro-inflammatory signaling has been linked to activation of a CD47-associated SE in breast cancer cells [160], providing locus-specific evidence that extracellular signals can engage SE-associated regulation. However, this finding does not establish that CAF-derived cytokines, including IL-6, broadly create or maintain functionally required SEs across breast cancer subtypes.

  Hypoxia can alter hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-dependent transcription, chromatin accessibility and enhancer–promoter communication. In breast cancer cells, hypoxia-induced chromatin interactions at the MALAT1 locus provide direct evidence for locus-specific enhancer rewiring [161]. By contrast, current evidence does not establish genome-wide or functionally required HIF-dependent SE reconfiguration in primary breast tumors. HIF-responsive gene expression or changes in immune-checkpoint expression should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of SE dependency in the absence of chromatin-level and perturbation-based validation. Overall, available data more securely support microenvironment-dependent enhancer remodeling than stable SE reprogramming. Establishing the latter will require cell-type-resolved chromatin profiling, longitudinal sampling and locus-specific perturbation in patient-derived models.


### SE dysregulation in other solid tumors and hematologic malignancies
  SE dysregulation is not restricted to breast cancer. In solid tumors, recurrent mechanisms include enhancer hijacking, ecDNA-associated regulatory amplification and lineage-specific transcriptional addiction. Examples discussed in mechanistic studies include MYC regulation by the SE-derived lncRNA CCAT1-L in colorectal cancer, LSD1-dependent SE programs in castration-resistant prostate cancer, ALDH1A1-associated SE activity in ovarian cancer and MYCN amplicon architecture in neuroblastoma [97, 98, 145, 162, 163]. These examples illustrate distinct modes of SE-associated regulation rather than a single pan-cancer mechanism.

  Hematologic malignancies provide additional evidence for lineage- and oncogene-centered SE dependencies. T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia can acquire ectopic MYC activation through altered regulatory-domain architecture [87], whereas SE-driven TOX2 regulation has been reported in natural killer/T-cell lymphoma [164]. BET, CDK9 and related transcriptional regulators have also been evaluated in lymphoma and leukemia contexts [165–169]. Across both solid and hematologic malignancies, therapeutic interpretation should remain contingent on endogenous locus perturbation and context-specific validation rather than SE annotation alone.


### Enhancer and SE-associated dysregulation in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases
  Disease-associated enhancer remodeling, including changes at regions operationally annotated as SEs, has been reported in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [170]. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), synovial fibroblasts exhibit active enhancer landscapes at inflammatory and tissue-remodeling loci, including genes encoding cytokines, chemokines and osteoclastogenic factors [171–174]. SE-associated regulatory regions near TNF, IL6, IL1B and TNFSF11, which encodes receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), may integrate NF-κB and AP-1 signaling, thereby supporting persistent inflammatory gene expression, synovial activation and bone-destructive processes [175]. Enhancer RNAs transcribed from these regions may further contribute to enhancer–promoter communication, although their functional contribution requires context-specific validation. Preclinical studies suggest that perturbing SE-associated transcriptional regulators or selected enhancer-derived RNAs can attenuate inflammatory outputs, but therapeutic translation remains at an early stage.

  In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), enhancer and putative SE remodeling has been associated with the exaggerated type I interferon response that characterizes many patients [176, 177]. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells and other immune populations can exhibit increased enhancer activity at interferon-stimulated genes, including IFIT1, MX1 and OAS1. Moreover, genetic risk variants associated with SLE and other autoimmune diseases are frequently enriched within non-coding regulatory regions, including SE-like elements, where they may alter transcription factor binding and chromatin activity [178, 179]. BET inhibition has been shown in experimental models to dampen inflammatory and interferon-associated transcriptional programs, providing proof of principle that SE-associated regulatory dependencies may be therapeutically tractable. However, given the broad role of SEs in normal immune activation and tolerance, future studies will need to define disease-specific regulatory circuits, cell-type-selective vulnerabilities and therapeutic windows before SE-directed approaches can be advanced for autoimmune disease treatment.


### SE dysregulation in neurological and neurodevelopmental disorders
  SE dysregulation has been implicated in neurological and neurodevelopmental disorders through altered control of neuronal, glial and immune-related transcriptional programs. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), disease-associated enhancer remodeling has been observed in microglia, particularly at loci involved in neuroinflammation, lipid metabolism and phagocytic responses [180, 181]. Regulatory regions near genes such as TREM2, APOE and CLU may acquire increased H3K27ac during disease progression, suggesting that altered SE activity may contribute to sustained inflammatory and stress-response states in the aging brain. Single-nucleus chromatin accessibility analyses further indicate that AD-associated non-coding risk variants, including variants near BIN1 and PICALM, are enriched in microglial regulatory elements, supporting a link between inherited genetic risk and cell-type-specific enhancer function [182, 183]. BET inhibition has shown anti-inflammatory effects in experimental models, but whether these effects are mediated directly through disease-relevant SEs requires further mechanistic validation.

  In neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), genetic and epigenetic disruption of SE-associated regulatory networks may impair neuronal differentiation, synaptic maturation and activity-dependent gene expression [184, 185]. De novo mutations and non-coding risk variants can affect genes or regulatory elements associated with synaptic function, chromatin remodeling and neuronal lineage specification, including SHANK3, NRXN1, CHD8 and ARID1B [186]. Variants within neuronal SEs may alter transcription factor occupancy, including factors such as MEF2C and TBR1, thereby perturbing developmental gene regulatory programs [187]. CRISPR-based epigenetic editing has been explored in patient-derived cellular models to modulate disease-associated regulatory elements, but these approaches remain preclinical. Overall, SE-centered analyses provide a useful framework for linking non-coding genetic variation, cell-type-specific chromatin states and disease-associated transcriptional dysregulation in the nervous system.


### SE dysregulation in metabolic diseases
  SE dysregulation has been implicated in metabolic diseases through altered transcriptional programs that control lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, inflammation and cellular stress responses [188]. In non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hepatocyte enhancer landscapes are remodeled at genes involved in de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid metabolism and gluconeogenesis, including SREBF1, FASN, SCD1, PCK1 and G6PC [189, 190]. These regulatory changes can be influenced by nutrient-responsive transcription factors such as carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein (ChREBP) and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c), and may contribute to hepatic lipid accumulation and insulin resistance [191]. Enhancer RNAs derived from metabolic regulatory regions may further modulate enhancer–promoter communication, although their functional relevance should be defined in a cell-type- and disease-stage-specific manner. In parallel, Kupffer cells and other liver-resident immune populations can exhibit inflammatory enhancer activation, with increased chemokine and cytokine expression at loci such as CCL2 and IL1B. These immune regulatory programs are likely to participate in the inflammatory progression from steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [192].

  In type 2 diabetes (T2D), pancreatic β-cell dysfunction is associated with disruption of regulatory elements controlling insulin production, glucose sensing and stimulus–secretion coupling, including loci such as INS, GCK and KCNJ11 [193]. Chronic glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines may weaken β-cell identity-associated enhancer programs while increasing regulatory activity at stress-response and pro-apoptotic genes, including BAX and BID. In selected preclinical models, histone deacetylase or BET inhibition influences β-cell survival and inflammatory responses, but tissue specificity, long-term safety, and metabolic effects remain unresolved. SE-centered analyses can therefore help relate metabolic stress to cell-type-specific chromatin remodeling and transcriptional dysfunction in NAFLD, NASH, and type 2 diabetes [194, 195], although individual dependencies require direct testing.


## Therapeutic targeting of SEs and resistance-associated reprogramming
  The identification of disease-associated SE programs raises the possibility of therapeutically disrupting regulatory dependencies that sustain pathological cell states. Such dependencies can be targeted indirectly through transcriptional and chromatin regulators or more selectively through perturbation of individual enhancers, enhancer RNAs, or associated signaling circuits. However, an annotated SE is not necessarily a tractable or selective therapeutic target. This section evaluates current targeting strategies, the evidence supporting their mechanisms, and the adaptive SE reprogramming that can limit treatment durability and promote resistance.


### Small-molecule inhibitors targeting SE-associated transcriptional machinery
  Therapeutic targeting of SEs is based on the observation that SE-associated genes can show increased sensitivity to perturbation of transcriptional cofactors and elongation machinery. Small-molecule strategies have therefore focused on SE-associated transcriptional regulators and chromatin-modifying enzymes, including BET family members, CDK7, CDK9 and histone deacetylases [196] (Fig. 6). BET inhibitors, including the tool compound JQ1 and related clinical-stage agents, can limit BET protein binding to acetylated chromatin and thereby reduce SE-associated transcriptional activity [197] (Table 2).   Recent efforts have sought to improve selectivity and therapeutic windows. More selective CDK7 and CDK9 inhibitors, as well as targeted protein degradation approaches, have been developed to reduce broad transcriptional suppression [216]. BET-directed proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), including agents such as ARV-825 and MZ1, promote degradation of BET proteins rather than transient inhibition, leading to more sustained disruption of SE-associated transcription in preclinical models [217, 218]. However, these approaches remain constrained by on-target effects in normal tissues that also rely on SE-regulated transcriptional programs.

  Combination strategies are being explored to improve efficacy and delay resistance. BET inhibition has been combined with CDK7 or CDK9 inhibition to target multiple layers of SE-dependent transcriptional control [165]. Histone deacetylase inhibitors may also alter enhancer acetylation states and increase dependence on BET-mediated transcription, providing a rationale for combination with BET inhibitors in selected contexts [219]. Nevertheless, such regimens require careful evaluation because broad inhibition of transcriptional regulators can produce dose-limiting toxicity. Biomarker-driven patient selection, based on SE dependency, transcriptional addiction or chromatin state, will be important for defining contexts in which these therapies are most likely to be effective [200].


### CRISPR-based epigenetic editing of SE activity
  CRISPR-based epigenetic editing provides a complementary strategy for modulating SE activity without introducing double-strand DNA breaks or altering the underlying genomic sequence. Catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9) fused to chromatin-modifying domains can be directed to SE regions to either activate or repress enhancer function [203]. For example, dCas9-p300 or dCas9-CBP systems can deposit H3K27ac and enhance regulatory activity, whereas dCas9-KRAB or dCas9-MeCP2 can promote repressive chromatin states and reduce SE-driven transcription [220, 221]. These tools have been used to investigate enhancer function and could provide a useful experimental foundation for developing therapeutic approaches that modulate disease-associated SEs.

  Additional platforms have expanded the range of epigenetic perturbations that can be applied to SEs. dCas9-LSD1 can reduce enhancer-associated histone H3 lysine 4 monomethylation (H3K4me1), whereas dCas9-DNMT3A can induce DNA methylation and potentially more durable transcriptional silencing [162, 204]. RNA-targeting CRISPR systems, including Cas13-based approaches, may also be used to reduce enhancer RNA levels and disrupt eRNA-associated chromatin interactions. Multiplexed CRISPR epigenome editing further enables simultaneous perturbation of multiple regulatory elements within a disease-associated transcriptional network [222].

  Despite their conceptual advantages, CRISPR-based SE-targeting approaches remain largely preclinical [223]. Delivery efficiency, cell-type specificity, editing durability, immunogenicity, and off-target chromatin changes are persistent obstacles [224]. For now, these platforms are used mainly to define SE function mechanistically; advances in delivery and control of editor activity will determine whether they can support therapeutic applications [225].


### SE modulation in immunotherapy and tumor–immune interactions
  SEs and SE-associated regulatory elements have been implicated in immune checkpoint expression, T cell differentiation and tumor immune evasion, suggesting potential opportunities for combining SE modulation with immunotherapy [226]. In chronically stimulated T cells, enhancer remodeling at exhaustion-related loci such as PDCD1 and HAVCR2 can help maintain inhibitory receptor expression [227]. Pharmacological or genetic perturbation of SE-associated transcriptional regulators may alter these exhaustion programs and improve effector function in selected experimental models [228].

  In tumor cells, SE-associated regulatory elements can contribute to immune evasion by supporting expression of checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1, as well as genes involved in cytokine signaling and antigen presentation [211, 229]. Oncogenic pathways such as MYC, STAT3 and hypoxia-inducible signaling can converge on enhancer landscapes to regulate these immune-modulatory genes [230]. CRISPR-based epigenetic tools could, in principle, be used to repress checkpoint-associated regulatory elements or activate antigen-presentation programs, although the specificity and durability of these effects remain to be established [231].

  Adoptive cell therapies may also benefit from improved control of SE-regulated transcriptional states [232]. Engineering strategies that limit exhaustion-associated regulatory programs or enhance persistence-associated transcriptional states could improve chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T) cell function, particularly in solid tumors [233, 234]. However, the therapeutic manipulation of SEs in immune cells requires caution, as broad disruption of enhancer networks may impair normal immune activation, differentiation or tolerance. Future work should define which SE-associated immune programs are therapeutically actionable and which are required for normal immune homeostasis.


### Therapy-induced SE reprogramming, resistance, and retargeting opportunities
  The same regulatory plasticity that creates therapeutic vulnerabilities can also enable escape after treatment. The following subsections therefore consider resistance as a consequence of treatment-imposed selection and adaptive enhancer rewiring, and then assess whether the resulting states create opportunities for biomarker-guided combination or sequential retargeting.


#### SE remodeling in endocrine and CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance
  In luminal breast cancer, resistance to endocrine therapy often involves adaptive remodeling of estrogen receptor signaling and related regulatory landscapes [235]. ESR1-associated SEs, together with lineage-defining factors such as FOXA1, contribute to sustained ERα-dependent transcription under estrogen-responsive conditions. During prolonged exposure to endocrine therapies, including tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors, resistant cells may acquire ESR1 mutations such as Y537S and D538G, which support ligand-independent ERα activity and maintain ERα-driven transcriptional programs despite estrogen deprivation [236, 237]. In parallel, enhancer remodeling at alternative growth factor signaling loci, including FGFR, IGF1R and ERBB3, may provide bypass proliferative signals and reduce dependence on canonical ERα signaling.

  Resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors can also involve transcriptional adaptation [238, 239]. In resistant cells, regulatory elements associated with cell-cycle control and E2F-dependent transcription may become reactivated, supporting expression of genes such as CCNE1 and CDK2 and restoring RB phosphorylation [240]. These changes can enable cell-cycle progression despite pharmacological CDK4/6 blockade [241]. Preclinical studies suggest that inhibition of SE-associated transcriptional regulators, including BET proteins or CDK7, may attenuate some resistance-associated transcriptional programs and restore sensitivity in selected models. However, the clinical relevance of these combinations requires further evaluation, particularly given the toxicity associated with broad transcriptional inhibition.


#### Adaptive SE rewiring during HER2-targeted therapy
  HER2-positive breast cancer is treated with HER2-directed antibodies, tyrosine kinase inhibitors and antibody–drug conjugates, including trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) [242, 243]. Although these agents have improved clinical outcomes, acquired resistance remains common. Suppression of HER2 signaling can impose selective pressure on tumor cells and promote adaptive transcriptional states. In this context, enhancer or SE remodeling may support activation of alternative receptor tyrosine kinase and survival pathways, including EGFR, MET, AXL and downstream PI3K–AKT or MAPK signaling [244]. Such changes may reduce dependence on ERBB2-associated transcriptional programs and provide compensatory mechanisms for continued proliferation and survival.

  The mechanisms of HER2-directed agents should be distinguished when considering their potential effects on enhancer regulation. Trastuzumab is an HER2-directed antibody without a cytotoxic payload, whereas trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) delivers the microtubule inhibitor DM1. By contrast, T-DXd is an antibody–drug conjugate that releases DXd, a membrane-permeable exatecan-derived DNA topoisomerase I (TOP1) inhibitor, following HER2-dependent internalization and linker cleavage [245]. DXd stabilizes TOP1–DNA cleavage complexes and induces replication-associated DNA damage, thereby linking T-DXd activity to DNA-damage and repair pathways [246].

  Oncogenic SEs can experience transcription-coupled DNA breaks and engage TOP1- and RAD51-associated repair, providing a mechanistic basis for their potential sensitivity to perturbations of DNA topology and repair [247]. Nevertheless, direct evidence that T-DXd or free TOP1 inhibitors selectively disrupt an ERBB2-associated SE is currently lacking. In particular, it has not been established that these agents consistently reduce H3K27ac, BRD4 or MED1 occupancy, alter enhancer–promoter contacts or abolish the functional requirement for ERBB2 regulatory elements. Any effect of the DXd payload on HER2-associated enhancer activity should therefore be regarded as mechanistically plausible and context-dependent, rather than as an established component of T-DXd action.


#### SE-associated programs in chemotherapy resistance and cancer stemness
  Beyond adaptive rewiring following targeted therapy, conventional DNA-targeting chemotherapy can also alter enhancer-associated transcription, although it does not exert a uniform effect on SEs [248–250]. Topoisomerase poisons, DNA-crosslinking agents and replication-stress-inducing drugs can acutely interfere with transcription, DNA topology and chromatin accessibility while activating DNA-damage and repair pathways [247, 251]. These immediate responses should be distinguished from the more persistent regulatory states that emerge through selection or adaptation during repeated treatment. Such states may retain pre-existing enhancer dependencies, acquire new enhancers or SEs, or alter enhancer–promoter communication to support survival [248–250]. Thus, reductions in global transcription following acute DNA damage do not necessarily predict the enhancer landscape of residual or drug-resistant tumor cells.

  Evidence for treatment-associated enhancer reprogramming is strongest in selected experimental systems. In platinum-resistant ovarian cancer models, chemotherapy-induced distal enhancers and SEs maintain transcriptional programs that differ from those required in untreated cells, supporting a functional contribution to the resistant state [248]. Doxorubicin-resistant breast cancer cells similarly exhibit extensive changes in chromatin accessibility and three-dimensional genome organization, including altered enhancer–promoter interactions [249, 252]. SE-associated regulation may also intersect with cancer stem-like phenotypes, which can be enriched following chemotherapy and contribute to tumor persistence and relapse. In breast cancer stem-like cell models, HDAC7 contributes to H3K27ac enrichment at SEs and the expression of stemness- and plasticity-associated genes, including MYC, CD44 and SNAI2 (SLUG); direct depletion or HDAC1/HDAC3 inhibition attenuates these programs, at least partly through HDAC7 downregulation [253]. In ovarian cancer, BRD4-dependent regulation of an ALDH1A1-associated SE and its enhancer RNA sustains ALDH activity, whereas BET inhibition suppresses the outgrowth of cisplatin-treated cells in vitro and in vivo [163]. Conversely, BRD4 can also participate in SE-associated transcriptional repression, and disruption of a BRD4–LSD1–NuRD regulatory complex has been linked to broad drug resistance in breast cancer [254]. These findings caution against treating SE activity as uniformly pro-resistance or assuming that pharmacological disruption of SE-associated machinery will have predictable effects across contexts.

  A central unresolved question is whether treatment-associated enhancer changes drive resistance or merely mark the cell populations that survive therapy. Addressing this distinction will require time-resolved profiling of H3K27ac, transcription-factor and cofactor occupancy, chromatin accessibility and genome topology before, during and after treatment, ideally in matched patient-derived samples. Locus-specific genetic or epigenetic perturbation should subsequently determine whether candidate enhancers, SEs or enhancer RNAs are required for drug efflux, DNA repair, anti-apoptotic signaling or maintenance of stem-like states. DNA-targeting chemotherapy should therefore be considered capable of reshaping enhancer-associated transcription in a drug-, dose-, time- and cell-state-dependent manner, rather than as uniformly activating or suppressing SEs.


## Challenges and future directions in SE research
  Therapeutic studies highlight both the potential and the limitations of targeting SE-associated transcription. Context-dependent enhancer usage, incomplete target-gene assignment, model-specific dependencies, systemic toxicity, and treatment-induced regulatory plasticity continue to impede clinical translation. Addressing these barriers requires methodological standardization, longitudinal functional validation, and models that better capture cellular heterogeneity and therapeutic evolution. This section discusses these unresolved challenges and considers how single-cell and spatial profiling, computational integration, patient-derived models, and biomarker-guided strategies may improve the rigor and translational relevance of SE research.


### Definitional uncertainty, model limitations and data standardization
  Several unresolved issues continue to limit the reproducibility and translational relevance of SE studies. A central challenge is the absence of a universally accepted definition of SEs [22]. Most studies rely on operational criteria based on H3K27ac enrichment, transcriptional cofactor occupancy and ranking-based algorithms such as ROSE [8]. However, these approaches define SEs relative to other enhancers within a given dataset rather than by fixed biological properties [6]. As a result, SE annotations are sensitive to peak calling, stitching distance, signal normalization and threshold selection. This variability complicates comparisons across cell types, disease states and independent studies.

  Experimental models introduce additional limitations. Two-dimensional cell lines and patient-derived organoids provide tractable systems for perturbation studies, but they incompletely recapitulate immune, stromal, vascular and systemic regulatory inputs [52, 53]. Genetically engineered mouse models offer in vivo context, but species-specific differences in enhancer architecture and transcription factor binding can restrict direct extrapolation to human disease. Patient-derived xenograft models preserve aspects of human tumor biology but often lack intact immune and stromal compartments [61]. These limitations underscore the need to interpret SE function in light of model-specific constraints.

  Data standardization remains a further barrier. Differences in ChIP-seq protocols, antibody performance, sequencing depth, sample purity and bioinformatic pipelines can produce divergent SE landscapes [35, 255]. Integrating scATAC-seq, scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics and chromatin interaction data introduces additional batch effects and platform-specific assumptions. Community-level reference datasets, quality-control standards and transparent computational workflows will be needed to improve reproducibility and facilitate cross-study validation [256].


### Single-cell, spatial and live-cell approaches
  Single-cell technologies can resolve regulatory states obscured in heterogeneous tissues. scATAC-seq profiles cell-specific chromatin accessibility, whereas scRNA-seq measures transcriptional output; their integration can associate accessible regulatory elements with cell-state-specific gene expression [44, 257–260]. A practical workflow includes quality control and filtering based on transcription-start-site enrichment and doublet detection, followed by construction of peak-by-cell and gene-by-cell matrices, dimensionality reduction, clustering, cell-type annotation, pseudobulk peak calling and peak-to-gene inference. Batch correction should be evaluated to ensure that biological variation is retained across samples and platforms. However, chromatin accessibility alone does not define an H3K27ac-based SE. Candidate cell-state-specific SEs therefore require orthogonal profiling of enhancer-associated histone marks or cofactor occupancy, whereas demonstration of functional dependency ultimately requires locus-specific perturbation. In cancer, this framework may help identify rare cell states, resistant populations and lineage transitions that are obscured in bulk analyses.

  Spatial transcriptomics adds anatomical context to these regulatory maps [45, 46]. Platforms such as 10 × Visium, multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH) and Slide-seq can localize gene expression programs within native tissue architecture, helping to distinguish tumor-cell-intrinsic programs from stromal, immune or hypoxia-associated signals. Although spatial transcriptomics does not directly measure SE activity, its integration with epigenomic and chromatin-accessibility data can support inference of spatially organized regulatory states and tumor–microenvironment interactions [24].

  Complementary combinations of transcriptional profiling, chromatin-accessibility assays and structural imaging may further refine SE analysis. Super-resolution imaging and CRISPR–dCas9-based locus labeling can visualize locus positioning, enhancer–promoter proximity and their spatial relationship to transcriptional condensates [261, 262]. Live-cell imaging may additionally provide temporal information on regulatory dynamics during differentiation, stress responses and drug exposure [263, 264]. Nevertheless, spatial proximity or condensate association does not by itself establish a functional SE dependency and should be integrated with perturbation-based evidence.


### Artificial intelligence and computational integration
  Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning, is increasingly being applied to SE research for regulatory network inference, drug prioritization and multi-omics integration [265]. Deep learning models can incorporate chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, transcription factor occupancy, chromatin interactions and gene expression to predict enhancer–promoter relationships and disease-associated regulatory circuits [266, 267]. Graph-based methods may be especially useful for modeling SE–promoter interactions within three-dimensional chromatin networks [268].

  AI-assisted approaches may also support therapeutic discovery [269]. Computational models that integrate SE annotations with drug-response profiles can prioritize candidate vulnerabilities associated with transcriptional dependencies. In parallel, AI-guided approaches may support degrader and molecular-glue design, as well as the prioritization of rational combination regimens targeting SE-associated regulators, including BET proteins, CDK7, CDK9 and p300/CBP [270]. However, these predictions require rigorous experimental validation. Many models remain limited by training-data bias, incomplete annotation of non-coding regulatory elements and insufficient representation of patient-derived datasets [25].

  Target discovery is also moving beyond candidate-based selection toward functional CRISPR screens, chemical–genetic profiling and multi-omic dependency mapping, whereas library generation increasingly includes focused epigenetic inhibitor sets, covalent-ligand collections and modular degrader libraries [19, 47, 216–218]. These approaches can broaden target and scaffold space, but biochemical target engagement and chromatin-level validation remain necessary before a hit can be interpreted as an SE-associated dependency.

  For clinical translation, AI-based models should be evaluated against clinically annotated cohorts, longitudinal samples and treatment-response datasets [271, 272]. Predictive frameworks will be most useful when they integrate enhancer state with genomic alterations, tumor subtype, microenvironmental context and pharmacological sensitivity. Without such validation, computational predictions should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than clinically actionable [273].


### Translational priorities and next-generation therapeutic strategies
  The clinical translation of SE-targeted therapies is limited by the fact that SEs also regulate essential transcriptional programs in normal tissues [166]. BET inhibitors can cause hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicities, whereas CDK7 and CDK9 inhibitors may affect bone marrow function and other rapidly renewing tissues [201]. These toxicities reflect the broad roles of transcriptional cofactors in normal physiology and underscore the need for selective dosing strategies, rational combinations and predictive biomarkers [167].

  Delivery remains a central barrier for CRISPR-based or nucleic-acid-based SE modulation [205]. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are among the most advanced non-viral delivery platforms and can package messenger RNA (mRNA), guide RNAs or ribonucleoprotein components [274, 275]. Their transient expression profile may reduce prolonged exposure and off-target editing. However, current LNP formulations often show preferential accumulation in the liver, spleen and bone marrow, limiting efficient delivery to many solid tumors [206]. Ligand modification may improve tumor targeting, but it also increases manufacturing complexity and may introduce additional safety considerations.

  Virus-like particles (VLPs) provide an alternative platform for delivering genome or epigenome editing components as ribonucleoprotein complexes, potentially reducing risks associated with persistent nuclease expression or genomic integration [207, 276]. Engineered VLPs can be further optimized for cell-type-selective delivery, although improvements in in vivo stability, biodistribution, packaging efficiency and scalability are still needed [208, 277]. Overcoming these delivery and safety barriers will be essential before CRISPR-based SE modulation can be considered for routine therapeutic use.

  These barriers define the priorities for next-generation intervention: disease- and cell-type-selective delivery, locus- or target-selective modulation, pharmacodynamic biomarkers that demonstrate engagement of the proposed regulatory dependency, and longitudinal sampling to detect compensatory enhancer rewiring. CRISPR-based epigenetic editing, targeted protein degradation and synthetic regulatory systems may improve precision, but remain largely preclinical and will require rigorous evaluation of durability, off-target chromatin effects, immunogenicity and manufacturability [278–280].

  Future progress in SE research will depend on integrating mechanistic biology with clinically relevant models and standardized datasets [281]. Multi-omics approaches that combine SE maps with transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, phosphoproteomics and genome-wide association data may help connect regulatory architecture with disease phenotypes [282]. Such integrative analyses could identify inherited or somatic variants that alter SE function and contribute to disease susceptibility, progression or treatment response [149].

  SE-based biomarkers also require more rigorous development [283]. Tissue-based SE burden scores, circulating enhancer-derived RNAs, chromatin-state measurements from cell-free DNA and single-cell regulatory heterogeneity indices may provide complementary information, but their clinical value remains to be established [284]. Prospective validation will be necessary to determine whether these biomarkers can support patient stratification, treatment monitoring or early detection of therapeutic resistance [285].


## Conclusions and future perspectives
  The super-enhancer (SE) framework has provided a useful means of linking dense enhancer occupancy to genes that support cell identity and context-dependent transcription. However, SEs do not constitute a mechanistically uniform class of enhancers. Individual SE loci can contain functionally heterogeneous elements, including classic enhancers, facilitators and partially redundant components. Furthermore, their annotation depends on relative epigenomic signal strength, computational ranking, stitching distance and threshold selection. Thus, SE rank or H3K27ac enrichment alone should not be interpreted as evidence for regulatory necessity. Mechanistic inference requires the integration of chromatin profiling with locus-specific perturbation and experimentally supported enhancer–promoter assignment.

  Across physiology, SE-associated regulation has been implicated in lineage specification, trophoblast development and pancreatic β-cell function. In disease, altered SE or enhancer landscapes can accompany aberrant cellular states, but the strength of causal evidence varies considerably. Breast cancer illustrates this distinction: functional evidence supports selected ERα-associated and triple-negative breast cancer-specific regulatory circuits, whereas evidence for a functionally required ERBB2-associated SE remains comparatively limited. Similarly, SE remodeling has been reported in rheumatoid arthritis, whereas studies in neurological disease more often identify disease-associated regulatory elements or chromatin states without establishing SE dependence. Disease-associated SE maps should therefore be distinguished from regulatory dependencies that are required to maintain pathological phenotypes.

  Therapeutic disruption of SE-associated transcription remains a promising but experimentally and clinically immature strategy. BET, CDK7 and CDK9 inhibitors can suppress transcriptionally demanding programs, but these agents act on widely shared regulatory machinery rather than selectively targeting individual SEs. Early-phase studies and selected later-stage trials demonstrate that these target classes have entered clinical evaluation, but do not in themselves establish that treatment responses are mediated by SE dependence. BET-targeted degraders may produce durable protein depletion in preclinical models, though their selectivity, pharmacokinetics and resistance mechanisms require further evaluation. CRISPR-based epigenetic editing offers greater locus specificity, but delivery, durability, off-target chromatin effects and cell-type restrictions remain substantial barriers. Clinical translation will therefore require mechanism-informed biomarkers and therapeutic windows that distinguish disease-associated dependencies from essential transcriptional programs in normal tissues.

  Several research questions should now be prioritized. What quantitative, molecular or topological features distinguish a functionally required SE from a highly active but dispensable cluster of enhancers? How are constituent enhancers organized into hierarchical, cooperative or redundant relationships, and how do these relationships change with genetic perturbation or treatment? How stable are SE dependencies across cell states, anatomical regions, treatment histories and patient populations? How are enhancer networks assembled and maintained on extrachromosomal DNA, and do enhancer hijacking, ecDNA hubs or mitotic inheritance create context-specific transcriptional dependencies that contribute to tumor evolution or therapeutic adaptation (see Fig. 3)? Finally, can predictive biomarkers identify patients in whom the SE-associated transcriptional machinery can be perturbed without unacceptable effects on normal tissue homeostasis? Addressing these questions will require experimental designs that explicitly distinguish association, functional necessity and therapeutic action.

  Progress will depend on standardized annotation criteria, transparent computational workflows and perturbation-based validation in physiologically relevant systems. Patient-derived organoids and xenografts can provide complementary information about tissue architecture, clonal heterogeneity and treatment response, although neither fully recapitulates human physiology. Integration of these models with single-cell, spatial and three-dimensional epigenomic approaches should help resolve SE activity across cellular, spatial and temporal scales. Computational and artificial intelligence-based models may aid regulatory network inference and candidate prioritization, but their outputs should remain hypothesis-generating until validated in independent, clinically annotated cohorts. Ultimately, the translational value of SE research will depend on moving beyond descriptive maps towards reproducible, mechanistically validated and context-specific regulatory dependencies.
