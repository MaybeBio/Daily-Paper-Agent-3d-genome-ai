# Varied Ensembles of Long-range ChROmatin interactions drive chromatin compartmentalization


## Abstract
  The chromatin components that are involved in organizing eukaryotic genomes into active and inactive compartments remain unclear. In analysing viable Caenorhabditis elegans mutants lacking different chromatin-regulatory proteins, we found that the HP1-type factor HPL-2 and the MBT-domain protein LIN-61 jointly contribute to chromatin compartmentalization. Genomic regions bound by both proteins and their cognate histone mark H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) form broad interfaces of 30–50 kilobases that engage in combinatorial interactions, which we term ‘Varied Ensembles of Long-range ChROmatin interactions’ (VELCROs). Remarkably, VELCROs are defined by high density of H3K9me2, HPL-2, and LIN-61 sites, but not by the intensity of their individual peaks. Mutants that display defective chromatin compartmentalization are impaired in VELCROs. Polymer simulations show that VELCROs can drive a phase transition that segregates B from A chromatin compartments depending on peak densities. Our work identifies VELCROs as stochastic ensembles of interactions constrained within defined chromatin hubs that shape genome compartmentalization.


## Introduction
  Eukaryotic genomes are organized into spatially segregated active/euchromatin and inactive/heterochromatin domains that cluster into two distinct A and B compartments within the three-dimensional nuclear space. Such organization depends on interactions between heterochromatin or euchromatin components and the interplay of chromatin with additional nuclear elements. In this context, it has been demonstrated that chromatin interactions with the nuclear periphery impact on heterochromatin compartmentalization, e.g. through the formation of lamina-associated domains (LADs) [1, 2]. Perturbing such interactions can have profound impact on chromatin compartmentalization, as illustrated for example during cellular senescence by the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF) [3–5].

  Interactions within chromatin impact its organization at multiple levels, not only at the scale of A/B compartments [6–10] but also through the formation of chromatin loops and topologically associating domains (TADs) that feature elevated local contacts within individual chromatin units [11–14]. While detailed mechanisms for the establishment of chromatin loops and TADs have been put forward [15, 16], the molecular mechanisms driving genome compartmentalization remain unclear.

  In reductionistic modelling approaches, weak attractive forces establishing homotypic (i.e. eu-/eu- and hetero-/heterochromatin) interactions can recapitulate eu- versus heterochromatin segregation in flies and mammals [17–22]. However, the molecular cues of these interactions and their fundamental determinants have been difficult to establish due to experimental challenges in identifying and perturbing the chromatin-regulatory proteins involved. In the test tube, chromatin has been shown to differentially self-associate depending on its level of histone acetylation and association with linker histone H1 [23]. Furthermore, it seems that the histone modification H3 lysine 9 methylation (H3K9me) plays a key role in the establishment and maintenance of heterochromatin across many species [24–26]. Since reconstituted chromatin systems with or without H3K9me in vitro behave biophysically very similar [27, 28], it has been proposed that the cellular function of the modification depends on the recruitment of specific binding proteins [29, 30]. The prototypic H3K9me-binding proteins are the Heterochromatin Protein 1 (HP1), which are found in all higher organisms where heterochromatin has been defined [31]. HP1 homologs from different species can bridge nucleosomes [32] and condense H3K9me chromatin arrays in vitro [27, 33]. Genetic as well as molecular evidence implies a functional role of these factors in different types of cellular heterochromatin (e.g. at the pericentromeres) [25, 26, 31, 34, 35].

  To investigate the fundamental mechanisms of chromatin compartmentalization, we resorted to C. elegans as a model system: (i) At a relatively small genome size (100 Mb), C. elegans cells reflect many of the fundamental features seen in the chromatin organization of higher eukaryotes [36, 37]. (ii) The pathways of heterochromatin formation and segregation in this model system are not linked to centromeres, which are essential structures in higher eukaryotes [38]. (iii) Different scales of chromatin compartmentalization ranging from large domains to the level of small subcompartments have been identified [16, 39, 40]. (iv) Molecular players implied in the formation of heterochromatin (e.g. H3K9me and HP1) are conserved in C. elegans. (v) In contrast to higher eukaryotes (fly, mouse, and human), enzymes catalysing H3K9me and its binding proteins are not essential in the nematode [41, 42].


## Materials and methods

### General C. elegans work
  C. elegans was cultivated on nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50-1 [43]. NGM plates supplemented with ten times excess peptone were used to grow worms for Hi-C and ChIP-seq. N2 was used as the wild-type strain. The genotypes of the mutant C. elegans strains used are: lin-61(tm2649) [44], hpl-2(tm1489) [45], set-32(red11); met-2(n4256) set-25(n5021) [41], and cec-4(ok3124) [46]. The lin-61; hpl-2 mutant strain was generated from the individual mutants by standard crossing. Strains used for fluorescence imaging were generated by SunyBiotech (Fuzhou, China) by sequence knock-in of fluorescent tags at the 3′-end of the ORF of target genes using CRISPR/Cas9. These are: PHX3274 with genotype hpl-2a::muGFP(syb3274) and PHX2892 with genotype lin-61::mKate(syb2892). The following sgRNA sequences were used in this process: hpl-2a-sg1: 5′-CCAAGCTAACAATCCAAGACCCA-3′, hpl-2a-sg2: 5′-CCGACGAGCTTTAATCACTCCCC-3′, lin-61-sg1: 5′-CCATGATATTCTACCGATAGGAT-3′, lin-61-sg2: 5′-CCGATAGGATGGTGTGAAGCGCA-3′. The genotypes of all C. elegans strains were regularly verified by single worm PCR. Western blot analysis was carried out as described before [44] using anti-HPL-2 (Novus Biologicals 38630002), anti-LIN-61 [44], and anti-H3 (Abcam ab 18521) antibodies. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Biosafety and Bioethics Committee of King Abdullah University of Science and Technology and in accordance with all national and international regulations.


### Treatment of C. elegans embryos with α-amanitin
  Embryos obtained by bleaching gravid adult worms were washed trice in M9 buffer. The resulting embryo pellet was resuspended in Egg Buffer (25 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.3, 118 mM NaCl, 48 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, and 2 mM MgCl2,). Chitinase (2 U/ml, Sigma Germany) was added at RT until embryos were permeable to trypan blue. At that point, an equal volume of fresh Egg Buffer containing 1% (w/v) BSA was added to quench the reaction. After centrifugation, the permeabilized embryos were resuspended in Egg Buffer containing 100 μg/ml α-amanitin and incubated on a nutator for 60 min. Embryos were then processed for crosslinking and nuclei isolation as described below.


### elegans embryo crosslinking and nuclei isolation
  Chromatin crosslinking of C. elegans embryos and nuclei preparation was done as described [47]. Briefly, gravid adult worms were collected and bleached to obtain at least 1 or 4 ml of late-stage embryo pellet for ChIP-seq or Hi-C experiments, respectively. Embryos were washed twice in 1× PBS, flash frozen in liquid N2, and stored at −80°C. Frozen embryos were thawed on ice and crosslinked in 50 ml of 2% (v/v) formaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at room temperature on a nutator. Following crosslinking, embryos were washed once in 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, twice in 1× PBS, and once in lysis buffer [10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 10 mM NaCl; 0.2% (v/v) Igepal CA-630 (Sigma, Germany); 5 mM DTT; 0.5 mM EGTA; 1– protease inhibitor (cOmplete™, Sigma, Germany)]. Embryos were dounced 25 times while on ice using pestle A and 25 times using pestle B (PYREX® 7 ml Dounce Tissue Grinder, Corning, USA). The extract was centrifuged at 100 × g at 4°C, and the supernatant with nuclei was collected. The process of extraction was repeated up to six times while analysing the supernatants for the presence of DAPI-stained nuclei under a fluorescence microscope. Supernatants containing pure nuclei were pooled and centrifuged for 5 min, 2000 × g at 4°C for collecting material for further experiments.


### Hi-C of C. elegans nuclei
  Hi-C was performed as described with minor modifications [47]. Freshly isolated nuclei were distributed into 6 × 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes and resuspended in 300 μl 1.25× DpnII buffer. After adding 38 μl of 1% (w/v) SDS, nuclei were incubated at 65 °C and shaking at 600 rpm for 10 min on a thermomixer (Eppendorf, Germany). 34 μl of 20% (v/v) Triton X-100 were added, and samples were incubated at 37°C and 600 rpm for 1 h. 12.5 μl of DpnII (NEB, USA) were added before incubation overnight at 37°C and 2000 × g. Following this, 52 μl of 10% (w/v) SDS were added (65°C for 20 min, shaking at 1000 rpm). Reactions were then added to 7.6 ml of ligation master mix [10% (v/v) Triton X-100, 10X T4 ligation buffer (500 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 100 mM MgCl2; 100 mM DTT), 80 μl of 10 mg/ml BSA, 80 μl of 100 mM ATP, and 5.96 ml nuclease-free water]. 100 μl T4 DNA ligase (Invitrogen, USA) were added and the reactions were incubated for 4 h on a rotator at 16°C. 50 μl of 10 mg/ml proteinase K were added (65°C, overnight). The following day, an additional 50 μl of 10 mg/ml proteinase K was added (65°C, 2 h) followed by 2 μl of 1 mg/ml RNaseA (Sigma, Germany) (37°C, 30 min). The ligated DNA was extracted using a Midi prep kit (Macherey-Nagel™, Germany; elution in 200 μl of nuclease-free water) and stored at −20°C. 2 μg of the eluted DNA in a total volume of 130 μl water were sheared using a Covaris E220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris, USA) in microtubes (microTUBE AFA Fiber Snap-Cap) with the following settings: duty factor, 10%; peak incident power 140 W; cycles/burst, 200; time, 55 s. The sheared DNA was then size selected for fragments of average size of 400 bp using SPRI beads (SPRIselect, USA). DNA was quantified using Qubit Fluorometric Quantification (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and libraries were prepared for sequencing using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on Novaseq 6000 PE150 S4 flowcells (Illumina®, USA). All Hi-C experiments were conducted as biologically independent duplicates.


### ChIP-seq of C. elegans nuclei
  ChIP was performed as described with minor modifications [48]. After crosslinking, C. elegans embryos were washed with 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, followed by two washes with FA buffer [50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5; 1 mM EDTA; 1% (v/v) Triton X-100; 0.1% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate; 150 mM NaCl; 1× protease inhibitor (cOmplete™, Sigma, Germany)]. Nuclei were sheared as for Hi-C and centrifuged at 13 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to collect extracts. Immunoprecipitations were set up with extracts equivalent to 3 mg and 3–5 μg of antibodies in a total volume of 500 μl of FA buffer. 10% of the reactions were set aside before addition of antibodies as input. After 16 h incubation at 65°C while rocking, complexes were recovered by adding 20 μl protein A/G magnetic beads (Pierce™, USA) and washed twice with FA buffer supplemented to 1 M NaCl, followed by two washes with FA buffer supplemented to 500 mM NaCl. Beads were treated with 1% (w/v) SDS in TE buffer containing 250 mM NaCl at 65°C for 30 min to elute the complexes. Samples and inputs were treated with 2 μl of 10 mg/ml Proteinase K for 1 h at 55°C. Decrosslinking was done at 65°C overnight in a thermomixer. DNA was purified with a PCR Clean Up Kit (Macherey-Nagel™, Germany) and quantified using Qubit Fluorometric Quantification (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Libray preparation was carried out using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on Novaseq 6000 PE150 S4 flowcells (Illumina®, USA). The following antibodies were used for ChIP experiments: anti-HPL-2 (Novus Biologicals 38630002), anti-LIN-61 (Novus Biologicals, USA: 29590002), anti-LEM-2 (Novus Biologicals, USA: 48540002), anti-H3K9me2 (Abcam, UK: ab1220), anti-H3K9me3 (VWR, USA: 309M3-B), anti-H3K4me1 (Abcam, UK: ab8895), anti-H3K4me3 (Active Motif, USA: 39159), anti-H3K27me3 (Abcam, UK: ab6002), and anti-H3K27ac (Active Motif, USA: Ab39133). All ChIP-seq experiments were conducted as biological independent triplicates.


### Extraction of RNA from C. elegans and RNA-seq
  C. elegans RNA was extracted as described [49]. Briefly, late-stage embryos were collected from worms grown on five NGM plates and flash frozen in liquid N2. The frozen pellet was grinded to a fine powder and dissolved in ten volumes of TRIzol™ (Invitrogen, USA). Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol, flash frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80°C. Library preparation and sequencing for RNA-seq (done in replicates) were performed by Novogene (Novogene Co., Hong Kong). mRNA was purified from total RNA with poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Random hexamer primers were used to synthesize first-strand cDNA, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis using dUTP for strand-specific libraries using a stranded RNA kit (NEBNext® Ultra™II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit, NEB, USA). Qubit Flourometric Quantification (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and real-time PCR were used for quantification and quality control. The size distribution of the library was analysed using an Agilent bioanalyzer system (Agilent 2100, USA). Quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on Novaseq 6000 PE150 S4 flowcells (Illumina®, USA).


### Real-time RT-PCR
  For cDNA synthesis, 100 ng of total RNA were incubated with 0.25 μM random hexamer primers and 0.5 mM dNTPs at 65°C for 5 min in a total volume of 15 μl in water. After cooling, RT buffer was added to 1× (250 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 375 mM KCl, 15 mM MgCl2; 50 mM DTT), 5 units/μl maxima H minus reverse transcriptase were added and the reactions were brought to a total volume of 20 μl. Reaction were incubated at 25°C, 50°C, and 85°C for 10, 15, and 5 min, respectively. cDNAs were stored at −20°C. Real time RT-PCR was performed using 1 μl of cDNA, 0.4 μM forward and reverse primers, 1× iQTM SYBR® green supermix (NEB, USA) in a final volume of 20 μl. Cycling conditions were: 95°C, 15 min; 95°C, 15 s; 55°C, 20 s; 72°C, 30 s, read plate] X40 followed by melting curve analysis. For normalization of the data, housekeeping gene rpl-26 (GAPDH) was used. The fold difference was calculated using the following equation: ΔCt = Ct goi – Ct ref; Linear fold change = −2ΔΔCt; where, Ct: cycle threshold; goi: gene of interest; ref: reference gene.

### Analysis of ChIP-seq data
  Replicates of ChIP-seq data were aligned to the C. elegans WBcel235 reference genome using BWA with default parameters. PyMaSC was used to determine fragment length, assess quality, inspect the distances between ± strand reads, and perform read extension. All ChIP-seq samples were normalized against their respective input using bamCompare (deepTools) with the following parameters: –minMappingQuality 20, –binSize 10, –smoothLength 30, –extendReads (determined by PyMaSC), –scaleFactorsMethod readCount, –operation log2, and –pseudocount 1. ChIP-R was employed to evaluate the irreproducible discovery rate (IDR) using peak calling results from MACS2. For ChIP-seq data from GEO, sites were defined by the detected peaks from every ChIP-seq and were intersected among all replicates. The combinatorial analysis of H3K9me2, H3K9me3, LIN-61, and HPL-2 peaks was performed using merge (resp. intersect in bedtools) with default parameters, thereby defining their common sites (e.g. HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2).

  Coverage normalization and differential binding analysis were performed using bamCompare (deepTools). By comparing the ChIP-seq signal to input controls, this generated normalized bigWig files. Log2 ratio transformation was applied for signal visualization across samples. Significant differential binding regions identified through ChIP-R analysis were visualized using integrative genomics viewer (IGV). Peak intensity was defined by the maximal value of normalized ChIP-seq reads in a bin. Peak (or site) density was defined by the number of peaks identified in every bin. Peak (or site) intensity or density were calculated for several binning resolutions from 1 to 50 kb. To plot peak density or intensity (or their variations), the parameter was centre normalized.

  Overlapping of sites was tested using a Fisher exact test between all ChIP-seq peaks identified in this work and peaks obtained from the GEO database. The variations in binding were calculated as z-scores using WiggleTools and deepTools by computing the variation of the signal (difference between mutant and wild-type) normalized to the standard deviation (difference between replicates of the same condition). The computeMatrix scale-regions command (deepTools) was used to generate a profile matrix for scaled regions ±2000 bp upstream and downstream of a body length of 200 bases. The sorting of regions was maintained as specified. Default settings were used for all other parameters. Heatmaps over the ±2 kb windows surrounding the ChIP-seq peaks (position 0; as detected in the wild-type condition) were then generated by ranking the variations of the ChIP-seq signal between mutant and wild-type embryos (z-scores), normalized to the variability of the signal between replicates. Each site was then ranked by the z-score bigwig signal of the given condition by using the importData function of the trackViewer R package. Global correlation was calculated using the Pearson correlation function and network_plot (R package).


### Analysis of RNA-seq data
  RNA-seq data were processed by aligning raw reads to the C. elegans reference genome WBcel235 using STAR. SAMtools was used for indexing the sorted BAM signal. Read quantification was performed using htseq-count, employing the Ensembl release 99 (WBcel235.99.gtf). Quality checks and normalization of the data were done using bamCoverage (in deepTools with parameters of binSize 10, smoothLength 30, and minMappingQuality 20). BigWig files were generated running RPKM (deepTools). Differentially expressed genes were obtained using default parameters in DeSeq2. Normalized RNA-seq reads were then extracted depending on the localization of the TSS of genes in the A or B compartment (see Hi-C analysis section). Counts were normalized with DESeq2’s median-of-ratios method. Levels in every mutant were compared to the levels of the same genes in wild-type embryos after applying a threshold on gene expression (>5 read counts) and running a Wilcoxon pairwise test. For this, the normalized counts function (normalized parameter set to TRUE) from DeSeq2 was used.

  For the analysis of repeat-element expression (Supplementary Fig. S7F–H), RNA-seq reads were re-aligned to the WBcel235 genome with STAR v2.7.10a in two-pass mode (–twopassMode Basic; genome index built with the Ensembl release 99 annotation), using default settings for multi-mapping reads (reads aligning to up to 10 loci retained). Repeat copies were taken from the UCSC RepeatMasker annotation of ce11 [71], restricted to chromosomes I–V and X and to copies of at least 100 bp (39 244 copies), each copy being treated as a separate feature. Reads were assigned to copies with featureCounts v2.1.1 in stranded mode (-s 2), multi-mapping reads being counted fractionally (-M –fraction) so that a read aligned to n loci contributed 1/n to each. Gene-level counts were obtained with featureCounts (Ensembl release 99 annotation), and variations were analysed with DESeq2 by taking the median-of-ratios for counts on repeats. Copies overlapping an annotated gene, copies of the rRNA or ARTEFACT classes, and tentative RepeatMasker assignments were excluded, leaving 13 251 intergenic copies. Counts were summed per RepeatMasker family, and the 21 families with > 20 intergenic copies were analysed. Each mutant was compared with wild-type (with two biological replicates per genotype) using DESeq2 v1.44.0 (design ∼genotype, parametric dispersion fit, Wald test); P-values were adjusted by a Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Total repeat expression was calculated as the ratio of the sum of normalized counts of these families in the wild-type condition. For positional analysis, each intergenic copy was assigned to a 10-kb bin by its midpoint. The fraction of a family’s copies located in B-compartment bins of wild-type embryos (lowest 12 of 50 quantiles of wild-type PC1 at 25-kb resolution) was compared with that of all other intergenic repeat copies by two-sided Fisher’s exact test, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (nine tests performed for three families, with three genomic features).


### Analysis of Hi-C data
  Hi-C reads were aligned to the C. elegans reference genome WBcel235 via BWA mem v0.7.17-r1198-dirty. Raw 3D contact matrices were then constructed using hicBuildMatrix (HiCExplorer). Post matrix construction was performed using hicAdjustMatrix (HiCExplorer) with the action mask parameter to filter out interactions involving regions listed in the ce11-blacklist.v2.bed file (https://github.com/Boyle-Lab/Blacklist/raw/master/lists/ce11-blacklist.v2.bed.gz). For a more accurate representation of chromosomal interactions, HicCorrectMatrix (HiCExplorer) was used for Knight-Ruiz (KR) matrix balancing to correct known biases of Hi-C data [72]. The corrected matrices were then subjected to hicNormalize (HiCExplorer), with the –normalize norm_range option to scale the read counts for comparisons between different genomic regions or samples.

  2D plots were computed by averaging Hi-C contact frequencies across a grid of cells with each bin representing the average contact frequency calculated from a corresponding 30 × 30 grid of cells in the original Hi-C matrix. Hi-C contact matrices were pre-processed to the HiCExplorer-compatible .h5 format. The matrices were binned at a fixed resolution of 25 kb. Outliers of contact matrices were filtered out as follows: Rows and columns with contact sums outside the interquartile range (IQR) ± 1.5 × IQR were masked, contact points with extreme values beyond the 5th and 95th percentiles (±1.5 × IQR) were masked and diagonal exclusion (i.e. proximal interactions over the diagonal ± 2 adjacent bins were removed to avoid bias from near-diagonal contacts). Contact matrices were log₂-transformed; zero entries were treated as missing values. hicCompareMatrices (HiCExplorer) was used to compare Hi-C matrices using the ratio operation. Prior to TAD detection, matrices were normalized using hicCorrectMatrix correct (HiCExplorer) or an equivalent normalization workflow to remove biases. Visualization tracks were generated using pyGenomeTracks. A configuration file was created with make_tracks_file (pyGenomeTracks) including the Hi-C contract matrix, TADs (BED file), eigenvector tracks (computed from the ICE-normalized matrices with hicPCA of HiCExplorer), and selected ChIP-seq density profiles.

  The detection of TADs was conducted using hicFindTads (HiCExplorer) with default parameters (minimum maximum width of 20 kb and 1 Mb; threshold comparison 0.05) configured to identify domains using a stepwise refinement approach maximizing resolution sensitivity. Hi-C Matrices were ICE normalized using hicCorrectMatrix (HiCExplorer). This method integrates a false discovery rate (FDR) correction to adjust for multiple hypothesis testing, ensuring that identified TADs represent statistically significant chromatin interactions. Key parameters included a minimum depth of 20 kb, a maximum depth of 50 kb, and a delta of 0.01, facilitating the delineation of boundaries with high precision. The results from hicFindTADs (HiCExplorer) were further processed to generate TAD scores, quantifying the strength and boundary sharpness of each detected domain (Supplementary Fig. S3). These scores were initially stored in a binary matrix format. For enhanced accessibility and visualization, the scores were converted from binary matrix (bm) to bigWig (bw) format using a custom R script (R package).

  Clustering of the detected TAD-like structures based on ChIP-seq peaks of H3K4me1, H3K4me3, HPL-2, LIN-61, H3K9me2, H3K9me3, LEM-2, and H3K27me3 was performed using the hclust function (R package stats) on a scaled distance matrix (with Euclidean distance). Silhouette score was used to estimate the quality of the separation depending on the number of clusters with an ideal score for four clusters. Plots were then generated using the dendro_data_k with 4 cluster parameters (cuttree parameter = 4) and plot_ggdendro functions of ggdendro. The PC value of each TAD-like structure was calculated as an averaged value of PC1 of all bins pertaining to the same structure. The relative proportion of each TAD class was then calculated after sorting all TAD-like structures based on this assigned PC value.

  Saddle plots were computed from KR-normalized Hi-C matrices at 25-kb resolution. For each chromosome, bins were ranked by their wild-type PC1 or PC2 value and divided into 50 quantile groups; the same ranking was applied to all conditions. Each saddle pixel was calculated as the mean over all intrachromosomal bin pairs of the corresponding pair of quantile groups, excluding the main diagonal and the first off-diagonals, and genome-wide saddle plots were obtained as the pixel-wise median of the six chromosome matrices (chromosomes I–V and X). Differential saddle plots were computed from the ratio of mutant to wild-type matrices (hicCompareMatrices, –operation ratio), so that a value of 1 denotes no change. For display, each differential matrix was rescaled to its own range, whereas all quantifications used unscaled ratios. For quantification, three 5 × 5 corners were extracted from the genome-wide differential matrix: B–B (quantile groups 1–5 × 1–5), A–A (46–50 × 46–50), and A–B (1–5 × 46–50). Each corner was summarized by the median of its 25 pixels, and the ratios B–B/A–A and B–B/A–B of these medians were computed for each genotype. 95% confidence intervals were obtained by percentile bootstrap (2000 iterations, resampling the pixels of each corner with replacement).

  For meta-profile analysis of compartment transitions, PC1 and PC2 values were obtained for each condition with hicPCA (HiCExplorer) from KR-normalized Hi-C matrices binned at 25 kb. Note that in C. elegans, A/B compartmentalization is captured by both PC1 and PC2 parameters with PC1 delineating mostly interactions between larger regions and PC2 delineating interactions within subcompartments (size < 100 kb, see Supplementary Fig. S2A) [39]. A/B transition were defined on the wild-type condition and kept for all conditions, from regions with at least >4 consecutive (non-positive) bins assigned to the B compartment, followed by >4 consecutive (positive) bins assigned to the A compartment. A-to-B boundaries were then orientated so that the B side was always on the left of the plots. For each transition, PC values were extracted over 12 bins (300 kb) on each side of the boundary. Each profile was baseline-corrected, per transition and per condition, by subtracting its mean over the 150 kb on the B side of the boundary. Meta-profiles show the mean of these profiles across transitions, and 95% confidence interval (shadow) as calculated by resampling transitions (2000 iterations). Amplitudes between the A and B compartment were quantified as the mean PC value over the 150 kb bins from the A side minus the mean PC over the 150 kb on the B side (amplitude of zero for a flat profile). For each condition, the presence of a transition was tested by a two-sided one-sample t-test of the amplitudes against zero, and mutants were compared with wild-type by two-sided paired t-tests over the same transitions, without correction for multiple testing.

  For hierarchical clustering of B-compartment contact profiles, B-compartment bins were defined on wild-type data, and the same bin set was then applied to every condition. The PC2 track (obtained by running hicPCA of HiCExplorer on the KR-normalized wild-type matrix at 25 kb) was averaged. Every bin with PC2 ≤ 0 was assigned to the B compartment. The analysis was further restricted to bins located on the chromosome arms [1]. Then, the intra-chromosomal contact matrices were loaded chromosome by chromosome and the main diagonal ±1 bin was set to exclude proximal contacts (<1 bin). For each chromosome, the sub-matrix restricted to the selected bins was extracted and flattened into a vector. The per-chromosome vectors were concatenated, yielding one vector of B–B contact values per condition with an identical bin-pair ordering across the compared conditions. The resulting condition × bin–pair matrix was standardized bin-pairwise and Euclidean distances between conditions were computed. Conditions were clustered with hclust (R package). The dendrogram was cut into four groups (cutree, k = 4) for branch colouring only and drawn with ggdendro (R package).

  For cumulative plots, observed/expected Hi-C matrices at 25-kb resolution were processed chromosome by chromosome: bins and individual contacts outside [Q5 − 1.5 × IQR, Q95 + 1.5 × IQR] were masked, the diagonal ± 2 bins were removed, and non-zero values were log2-transformed. Bins were assigned to the B (wild-type PC1, or PC2, < 0) or A (≥ 0) compartment, and intrachromosomal contacts were classified as A–A, B–B, or A–B. For each condition, log2(observed/expected) values were converted to z-scores using the mean and standard deviation of all contacts (so that z-scores express the position of each class relative to the others). Empirical cumulative distributions were plotted for each interaction class (ecdf, R package stats). Because this standardisation couples the classes (a loss of B–B contacts mechanically shifts A–A and A–B upwards), changes were quantified on the untransformed log2(observed/expected) values by comparing classes within each contact map: the B–B (or A–A) preference was defined as the median log2(O/E) of B–B (or A–A) contacts minus that of A–B contacts of the same map, and the segregation of A–B contacts as the median log2(O/E) of A–B contacts minus the mean of the A–A and B–B medians. Differences between each mutant and wild-type were assessed by an m-out-of-n bootstrap: in each of 2000 iterations, 1000 contacts per interaction class and condition were resampled with replacement, the within-map quantities were recomputed, and the wild-type value was subtracted. The two mutants with impaired compartmentalization (lin-61; hpl-2 and set-32; met-2 set-25) were compared with the other genotypes on the same bootstrap iterations, as the difference between their mean and the mean of wild-type and the three single mutants. 95% confidence intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled differences, and two-sided P-values were computed as 2 × min[P(Δ ≤ 0), P(Δ ≥ 0)] (minimum 1/2 000), without correction for multiple testing.

  For correlation analysis at compartment or TAD scale, TAD strength was obtained by summing the contact probability from KR normalized contact matrices in wild-type condition and in each mutant. The net variations in TAD strength (ΔTAD strength) were then computed as: ΔTAD strength Mutij = (TAD strength Muti)j/(TAD strength wild-type)j, where i is the ith mutant condition and j is the jth TAD detected in the wild-type condition. The corresponding net variations in compartmentalization (ΔPC) were computed by subtracting the mean variation of the PC1 values of mutant conditions from the corresponding values of the wild-type condition for each 25 kb genomic bin. A regression line was then fitted using stat_smooth (method ‘lm’) and the correlation coefficient statistic was obtained with stat_cor using default parameters (ggplot2, R package).

  Aggregate Peak Analysis (APA) was performed on Hi-C contact matrices at a resolution of 10 or 25 kb as described with some adaptations [8, 73, 74]. For APA on TAD borders (also called ‘boundaries’), the genomic coordinates of TADs detected using hicFindTADs (HiCExplorer) were imported. For each boundary, submatrices centred on the TAD start and end coordinates were extracted from log₂(observed/expected) contact matrices. The local regions around boundaries were set to ±20 bins (±200 kb at 10 kb resolution). To quantify enrichment and reduce the influence of background signal, matrices were quantile-normalized both globally and locally. APA plots were generated by averaging all boundary-centred submatrices across autosomes and separately by chromosome. For each APA, a minimum distance threshold of 200 kb was applied between each pair of peaks to capture significant chromatin interactions. For statistical validation, a sampling of the interactions was performed to assess 50 independent times the interactions of 30 000 pairs in every condition. The variation was then tested using a Wilcoxon pairwise test for the central square values (3 × 3 central square of each of the 50 aggregated matrices) between the top and control group (stat_compare_mean from ggpubr, R package). To assess the influence of peak density, we reiterated the APA 50 times by sampling 30 000 couples each time from high or medium densities of sites using the sample function (replace = T, R package stats). We then compared the CS values (3 × 3 central square of each of the 50 aggregated matrices) between the top and control group applying a Wilcoxon pairwise test (stat_compare_mean from ggpubr, R package).

  To assess the mutual influence of loops and VELCROs, we performed APA on A and B compartments separately. We conducted chromatin loop detection using the high-resolution Hi-C data previously normalized and balanced for biases. KR-normalized Hi-C contact matrices were converted from the HDF5 (.h5) to the Cooler (.cool) format using hicConvertFormat (HiCExplorer). Loop detection was performed using Mustache configured to detect loops at 1 kb resolution (with parameter -r 1 kb -pt 0.1 -st 0.88). Loops computed at 1 kb resolution were assigned to 10 kb bins by strict overlap. APA couples generated for A and B filtered compartments were categorized as loop-dependent if at least one site (or bin) in each pair was involved in one of the loops detected by Mustache.

  A custom python script was used to extract and analyse the positions of the chromatin beads for each simulation. Briefly, Hi-C contact matrices for each simulation step were built by counting contacts between pairs of beads with an inter-particle distance shorter than 2.5 σ. The simulation parameters described in the previous sections were chosen to minimize the global SSR.

## Results

### Different mutations impair long-range interactions of the C. elegans genome
  To assess genome organization in late-stage C. elegans embryos, we conducted independently repeated high-resolution chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) experiments and generated 3D interaction maps at high resolution (5 kb, Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). Hi-C interaction matrices showed features reminiscent of other experimental systems. TAD-like structures could be identified on the X chromosome and on the autosomes (marked by black triangles in Fig. 1A, and Supplementary Fig. S1B,C) as reported earlier [37, 47, 78]. Further, preferential interactions between distal, non-neighbouring chromatin regions, reflective of genome compartmentalization, could be identified. As was shown previously [39, 40, 47], on large scales the interacting regions aligned well with the first principal component (PC1) of the Hi-C data, thereby defining large compartments that partially reflect chromosome arm-to-arm interactions. Notably, many interactions of smaller regions down to ~100 kb were also captured by PC1 (Supplementary Fig. S1D). It was shown recently that the second principal component (PC2) also captures long-range interactions of the C. elegans genome preferentially at the smaller scale of subcompartments. These align well with the transcription status of the involved genomic regions [39]. Accordingly, subcompartments could be identified by PC2 at a mean size of around 100 kb within the large compartments (Supplementary Fig. S1D, examples marked by the dashed lines and blue and black rectangles in Fig. 1A).

  Quantification of interactions between genomic sites that were ranked according to the PC1 or PC2 values highlighted a natural separation of interactions among regions with negative PC values (B compartment) and regions with positive PC values (A compartment), as visualized on so-called saddle plots (Fig. 1B, top, and Supplementary Fig. S2). A more or less pronounced separation of the B compartment into two regions was observed depending on the subset of chromosomes that was analysed. Such a separation has also been observed in other species [17, 79]. When considering the large and small subcompartments identified, the 3D organization of the C. elegans genome in our analysis shows general features of what has been seen in other experimental systems [7, 8, 80].

  We reasoned that chromatin features contributing to the segregation of compartments might follow the PC values. To explore this, we focused on a set of chromatin marks (active: H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K27ac; repressive: H3K9me2, H3K9me3, H3K27me3) and proteins binding the H3K9me histone modification that have been suggested to play a role in chromatin organization, namely HPL-2, a HP1 ortholog [81], and LIN-61, an MBT-domain protein implied together with HPL-2 in heterochromatin silencing [44, 82]. We also included LEM-2, a protein that is involved in anchoring chromosomal domains to the nuclear membrane [1]. Assessing the abundance of these features relative to the PC1 values of the respective genomic sites readily segregated the repressive/heterochromatin from the active/euchromatin components (Fig. 1B, bottom). Interestingly, such separation was solely observed when considering peak densities, i.e. the number of peaks as quantified every 10 kb along the genome (also referred to as ‘bins’ of 10 kb), but not when analysing the intensities of the same peaks, i.e. the maximal levels of factor binding or the maximum modification levels (also calculated in 10 kb bins) (Fig. 1B, bottom). These results suggest that high densities of H3K9me2, HPL-2, LIN-61, and LEM-2 peaks may be linked to B compartmentalization.

  Next, we tested whether the repressive chromatin features were also set apart at the scale of TADs. As pointed out before, TAD-like structures on the autosomes of C. elegans could be annotated with widely used detection tools [58, 60], identifying around 150 large TADs ranging from approximately 50 to 600 kb (Supplementary Fig. S3A,B). Clustering of TAD-like structures separated repressive components (HPL-2, LIN-61, LEM-2, H3K9me2/3, and H3K27me3) from active components (H3K4me1 and H3K4me3) (Supplementary Fig. S3C–E) only partially. Likewise, the PC1 value could only partially separate inactive from active TAD-like structures (Supplementary Fig. S3F). These observations imply a molecular disconnect between the scales of TADs and of B compartments that are more specifically associated with HPL-2, LIN-61, LEM-2, or H3K9me2/3 sites. We note, though, that this disconnect could at least in part be a consequence of the difficulty to annotate TAD-like structures and their borders in C. elegans, particularly for autosomes, compared to other experimental systems [47, 83]. Taken together, our data suggest that the densities (and not intensities) of HPL-2, LIN-61, LEM-2, or H3K9me2/3 sites are more tightly linked to compartment organization, than to inactive TAD-like structures.

  Given the correlation of high densities of HPL-2, LIN-61, LEM-2, and H3K9me2/3 with negative PC values in the Hi-C analysis, we tested whether these factors contribute to chromatin compartmentalization. To this end, we performed Hi-C analysis on late-stage C. elegans embryos mutant for hpl-2 [45], lin-61 [44], cec-4 [46], and the histone methyltransferases met-2, set-25, and set-32 (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). CEC-4 is a H3K9me-interacting chromodomain factor anchoring heterochromatin to the nuclear lamina [46]. The histone methyltransferase MET-2 is implied in H3K9 mono- and dimethylation, while SET-25 is carrying out H3K9 trimethylation [42]. Several studies indicated absence of H3K9 methylation in met-2, set-25 mutant C. elegans [42, 84, 85]. However, it has also been reported that additional mutation of set-32 is required to abolish H3K9 methylation genome-wide [41]. SET-32 was recently further linked to H3K23 methylation [86, 87]. To assess potential changes in chromatin compartmentalization at different scales, we plotted and quantified the averaged PC1 (Fig. 2B,C) and PC2 (Supplementary Fig. S4B,C) values across borders between B and A compartments. C. elegans embryos lacking met-2, set-25, and set-32 displayed a significant flattening of the resulting curves compared to wild-type embryos. In contrast, a significant transition between compartments was retained in the hpl-2 and lin-61 single mutants, with an amplitude reduced by 25%–40%. In C. elegans embryos lacking cec-4, the amplitude of the PC1 transition was down to a third of the wild-type level, and no PC2 transition was detectable (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S4C). The finding was surprising since the analysed B and A regions showed a marked difference in peak densities for HPL-2, LIN-61, and LEM-2 (inner nuclear lamina protein used here as a proxy for CEC-4, since no antibodies for this protein are available) and H3K9me2 in the wild-type condition (Fig. 2D). We and others had previously found evidence for protein and genetic interaction of HPL-2 and LIN-61 [44, 82]. We, therefore, reasoned that these factors might work redundantly. Indeed, the lin-61; hpl-2 double-mutant showed no detectable transition between compartments (PC1: Fig. 2B,C and PC2: Supplementary Fig. S4B,C).

  Compartmentalization defects in the set-32; met-2 set-25 and lin-61; hpl-2 mutants were also detected when comparing the PC1 values of wild-type and mutant conditions genome-wide (Supplementary Fig. S4D), e.g. between the chromosomal arms which globally correspond to regions harboring high densities of H3K9me, HPL-2, LIN-61, and LEM-2 (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Similar differences were observed for the PC2 value when zooming in on the level of small subcompartments within segments of chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. S4F). While the different mutants have various phenotypes, monitoring the distribution of the number of nuclei in preparations of late-stage embryos did not show any differences to wild-type C. elegans. Further, none of the mutations under study caused major changes in the expression of hpl-2 or lin-61 unless the respective gene itself was targeted (Supplementary Fig. S4G,H). Based on these analyses, we conclude that HPL-2, LIN-61, and their cognate histone mark H3K9me are key components for establishing B compartmentalization in C. elegans embryos at different scales, from hundred kilobases (small subcompartments) up to multiple megabases (arm-to-arm level).


### Organization and features of genomes with impaired B compartmentalization
  Assessment of the prevalence of genome interactions as a function of genomic distances revealed a large reduction in long-range interactions for the mutants impairing B compartmentalization, with a steep initial decay for short genomic distances (<50 kb) and a shallower decay in the range between 1 to 5 Mb (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S5A). Polymer physics models have related the organization of chromatin to the distance-dependent decay of genomic contacts [7, 88], suggesting that the wild-type chromosome organization is compromised in these mutants.

  Conceptionally, a phenotype with impaired compartmentalization could arise due to loss of homotypic attractive interactions, i.e. interactions between loci within the same compartment, and/or due to gain of heterotypic interactions, i.e. interactions between loci located in different compartments. Differential saddle plots of mutants with impaired compartmentalization in comparison to compartmentalized mutants showed a genome-wide net loss in homotypic B–B interactions relative to homotypic A–A and heterotypic A–B interactions, regardless of PC1 (Fig. 3B,C) or PC2 (Supplementary Fig. S5B, C) being used for the analysis. Hierarchical clustering of the B-compartment contacts confirmed the similar behaviour of the set-32; met-2 set-25 and lin-61; hpl-2 mutants, relative to the compartmentalized wild-type or single mutant cec-4, lin-61, or hpl-2 conditions (Fig. 3D).

  To further analyse the relative changes in homotypic (B–B or A–A) or heterotypic (A–B) long-range chromosomal interactions, we generated cumulative plots of genomic contacts. For compartments called by PC1 (Fig. 3E) or PC2 (Supplementary Fig. S5D), the preference for B–B over A–B contacts was strongly reduced on the autosomes of the lin-61; hpl-2 and set-32; met-2 set-25 mutants, which differed from wild-type and the single mutants. Only smaller differences were observed for the X chromosome, which has been shown to have a unique chromosome organization principle in C. elegans [47]. The single, compartmentalized cec-4 and hpl-2 mutants also showed a significant but markedly smaller reduction of the B–B preference (about a third of that of the set-32; met-2 set-25 and lin-61; hpl-2 mutants for PC1, and half for PC2). lin-61 only showed a small reduction for PC2. The ratio of A–A over A–B contacts was generally reduced: for PC1, it did not differ between the two groups of mutants (apart from a small decrease in hpl-2), and for PC2 it increased slightly in the lin-61; hpl-2 and set-32; met-2 set-25 mutants. Congruent with the loss of the B–B preference and the impairment of compartmentalization, A–B contacts increased relative to homotypic contacts in the lin-61; hpl-2 and set-32; met-2 set-25 mutants and, to a lesser extent, in the cec-4 and hpl-2 mutants.

  Next, we determined if the reduced long-range homotypic interactions in the mutants with impaired compartmentalization are correlated with changes at the scale of TAD-like structures. To do so, we compared the changes in homotypic interactions within inactive or active TAD-like structures (intra-TAD) with the changes in long-range homotypic (B–B or A–A) interactions as defined by PC1 (intra-compartment). In line with TADs on the X chromosome being formed by a distinct mechanism that involves the X-specific loop extruder DCC [47] and despite the detection of small changes in TAD-like structures (Supplementary Fig. S6A,B), we found no correlation between the reduction in B–B interactions versus intra-TAD interactions for the set-32; met-2 set-25 and lin-61; hpl-2 mutants on the X chromosome (Supplementary Fig. S6C). On the autosomes, we found a weak correlation between the changes in B–B versus intra-TAD interactions for the set-32; met-2 set-25 mutant but an anticorrelation for the lin-61; hpl-2 mutant. None of the single cec-4, hpl-2, or lin-61 mutants showed any correlation. No correlation was detected for A–A versus intra-TAD interactions in any of the mutants (Supplementary Fig. S6D). With the caveat that TAD-like structures may be difficult to detect in C. elegans, we conclude that there is no strong correlation between compartments and TAD-like structures in C. elegans embryos.

  It has been proposed that genome organization is controlled by and at the level of transcription [9, 89]. To probe this linkage in our experimental system, we inhibited PolII transcription in late C. elegans embryos using α-amanitin (100 μg/ml). This treatment did not cause a marked change in genome organization (Supplementary Fig. S7A,B). We also quantified the levels of poly(A)-tailed RNA in the different experimental conditions using RNA-seq. While several differentially regulated gene transcripts were identified in mutant compared to wild-type C. elegans embryos (Supplementary Fig. S7C), we did not find a correlation between changes in RNA expression and changes in compartmentalization. Also, gene transcription in A versus B compartments was far from being equalized in mutants showing impaired compartmentalization (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S7D,E). Poly(A) based RNA-seq in C. elegans has been shown to contain reads derived from repeat elements, likely because many of these are embedded within introns and 3′UTRs, and since there is contamination of the libraries from non-polyadenylated transcripts besides the few transcripts from repeat elements that are polyadenylated [90, 91]. Changes in repeat-element expression did at large not segregate with the compartment-containing and compartment-impaired mutants except for transcripts of the CMC-Mirage class II DNA transposons (Supplementary Fig. S7 F,G). These are, however, not particularly enriched in the B compartment (Supplementary Fig. S7H). With the limitation of analysing late state embryos that likely reflect a maintenance state of compartmentalization and analysing poly(A)-tailed transcription that does not cover small-RNA pathways [85, 92], we deduce that segregation of A and B compartments in C. elegans embryos is not directly coupled to gene and repeat-element transcription.


### Chromatin signatures of compartmental segregation
  To investigate the molecular mechanisms of genome compartmentalization, we tested whether interactions involving HPL-2/LIN-61 may directly influence B compartments, i.e. at sites where these factors bind to chromatin. To this end, we quantified the defects in compartmentalization as the net changes in the PC1 value between mutant and wild-type C. elegans embryos for all their genomic sites. While impairment of compartmentalization did generally not correlate with the intensities of HPL-2, LIN-61 and H3K9me2/3 peaks (Supplementary Fig. S8A), these were aligned with the densities of these peaks but not with those of the H3K4me or H3K27ac euchromatin components (Fig. 4A). The fact that compartment defects readily occurred at sites harboring the highest densities of HPL-2, LIN-61, and H3K9me peaks supports the notion that high densities of sites bound by these heterochromatic factors, and potentially factors that are associated with them, might be needed for compartmentalization.

  To further explore the linkage between the distribution of H3K9me2/3 and its binding factors, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) in mutant and wild-type embryos side-by-side (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Sites simultaneously containing peaks of HPL-2 and LIN-61 significantly overlapped with sites containing H3K9me2 peaks (4685 triple-positive HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 sites) and to a lesser extent with sites containing H3K9me3 peaks (2720 triple-positive HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me3 sites) (Supplementary Fig. S8C). Inspecting the ChIP-seq data validated the reduction of H3K9me2/3 in set-32; met-2 set-25, of LIN-61 in lin-61, and of HPL-2 in lin-61; hpl-2 mutants. In contrast, no differences in the distribution of H3K27me3 under these conditions was seen (Fig. 4B). Although the decrease in HPL-2 signal was not always complete, heatmaps of the z-scores quantifying the net decrease of binding in mutant compared to wild-type C. elegans embryos showed a significant drop of the signal centred on thousands of peaks (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S8D). Moreover, the decrease in binding of HPL-2 correlated well for all mutant conditions with that of H3K9me2 and of LIN-61, but unexpectedly much less so with that of H3K9me3. No correlation was detected for lin-61 and lin-61; hpl-2, and only a moderate correlation was seen in set-32; met-2 set-25, in which both H3K9me2 and H3K9me3 are lost (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S8E). Taken together, these findings suggest that multiple interactions among dense arrays of HPL-2, LIN-61, and H3K9me2 sites are linked to the formation of the B compartment in C. elegans embryos.


### Varied Ensembles of Long-range CHROmatin interactions drive B compartmentalization
  To inspect the spatial 3D interactions in greater detail, we sought to analyse these directly at the sites bound by HPL-2 and LIN-61 (HPL-2 + LIN-61 double-positive sites). To do so, we used aggregation peak analysis (APA, Fig. 5A,B), which detects the averaged genome-wide signal at all sites bound by these factors, thereby allowing to probe 3D interactions at high resolution (<1 kb) [8, 73, 74]. The aggregated signal in the A compartment of wild-type embryos showed a specific pattern of long-range contacts at HPL-2 and LIN-61 double-positive sites as indicated by a cross-like shape that is consistent with loop extrusion or scanning mechanisms, i.e. an interaction that can progress along the chromatin fibre (a singular site interacting with other singular sites) [93]. In contrast, a unique, broad and dispersed stripe pattern indicative of increased interactions around the same combination of HPL-2 + LIN-61 double-positive sites over a region of 30–50 kb was seen in the B compartment. We interpret this pattern as many individual sites within broad genomic regions interacting with many individual sites in another broad genomic region across the ensemble of the Hi-C measurement, giving rise to a delocalized appearance in the APA. To differentiate these dispersed long-range contacts from the very localized long-range contacts that give rise to chromatin loops, which form through a sharp, focal interaction between two defined genomic sites (loop anchors), we refer to this pattern as ‘Varied Ensembles of Long-range ChROmatin interactions’ (VELCROs). Importantly, the genetic determinants for VELCROs and compartments were the same, as VELCROs were absent in mutants showing impaired compartmentalization, while they were still present in single, compartmentalized mutants (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S9A,B).

  To extend the analysis, we inspected average ChIP-seq profiles around the central contact of HPL-2 + LIN-61 double-positive sites. These showed a broad enrichment of H3K9me2 but not H3K9me3 that like HPL-2 and LIN-61 extended over a ∼30–50 kb chromatin domain (Fig. 5C). Intriguingly, lack of any of the three components HPL-2, LIN-61, or H3K9me2 at genomic regions of interest resulted in largely impaired VELCROs (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S9C).

  If the simultaneous presence of HPL-2, LIN-61, and H3K9me2 is a molecular feature of B compartmentalization, then the density of these triple-positive sites should distinguish compartment identities. Indeed, the PC1 value abruptly changed from A to B when genome bins were sorted according to the number of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 but not HPL-2 + LIN-61 sites they contain (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. S9D). In agreement, VELCROs were absent between regions with decreased HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 densities (Fig. 5F). Notably, VELCROs were found over multiple distance ranges including sites within the same TAD-like structures (Supplementary Fig. S9E–G). Since VELCROs are aligned with B–B interactions at multiple scales giving rise to B compartments, we deduce that they are key interaction hubs underlying A/B-compartmentalization in C. elegans embryos.

  We next assessed the relationship between VELCROs and chromatin loops. We found that VELCROs were weaker at HPL-2 + LIN-61 double positive sites coinciding with anchors of chromatin loops (detected as previously described [58, 60]) (Fig. 5G), suggesting that chromatin loops are not required to seed VELCROs. These findings agree with previous work suggesting an antagonistic relationship between the formation of chromatin loops/TADs and the formation of compartments [94–96, 97]. Engagement of an HPL-2 + LIN-61 double-positive site into a chromatin loop (as found in the A compartment) may compromise formation of VELCROs, possibly because genomic regions get ‘trapped’ and cannot sample other genomic regions anymore, and/or because loop extrusion actively counteracts the formation of VELCROs.

  Why do HPL-2 + LIN-61 drive VELCROs only when colocalizing with H3K9me2 in the B compartment? It is conceivable that the stability of a long-range genomic interaction is linked to the lifetime of the factors that mediate it, such that dynamically exchanging factors give rise to more transient interactions. To estimate the residence time of HPL-2 in the A and B compartments, we performed fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments (Fig. 5H). HPL-2 foci, which are also enriched in LIN-61, are plausibly part of the B compartment, while nuclear regions surrounding these foci likely reflect the A compartment. At both locations, we detected a fast and a slow (immobile) HPL-2 population. While the diffusion time of the fast HPL-2 population was very similar at both locations, the slow HPL-2 population was significantly larger inside of HPL-2 + LIN-61 foci as compared to outside of these foci. This shows that the B compartment contains considerably more HPL-2 molecules that exhibit long lifetimes, which are compatible with the formation of long-lived chromatin bridging interactions. Notably, the ratio between slow HPL-2 molecules in the B versus the A compartment resembled the ratio between HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites in both compartments (Fig. 5I). Accordingly, slow HPL-2 molecules may represent those molecules that bind LIN-61 + H3K9me2 and mediate VELCROs. Conversely, fast HPL-2 molecules found in both compartments might be unable to mediate VELCROs due to their short residence time on chromatin.


### VELCROs can drive a phase transition in silico
  Having identified interactions among genomic sites simultaneously containing HPL-2, LIN-61, and H3K9me2 as bona fide drivers of chromatin compartmentalization, we performed polymer modelling to gauge the effect of such interactions. Following previous simulation schemes [17−22, 96], we represented chromosomes as polymers consisting of beads that each represent 10 kb of DNA (Fig. 6A). Two types of attractive interactions were considered between the beads: one that scales with the number of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites and one that scales with the number of chromatin loop anchors in the respective 10 kb regions. Varying the two interaction strengths in multiple independent runs, we calculated Hi-C contact matrices once the simulations had reached a plateau (Supplementary Fig. S10A,B). Reassuringly, the results generally matched the main organizational features of the different chromosomes of C. elegans embryos (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S10C–H). Chromosomes were similarly compartmentalized as reflected by similar PC1 values (Fig. 6B), and chromosomal arms formed distinct contact domains that interacted with each other and that were separated from the central region. Structures in the A compartment were less well reproduced than those in the B compartment (Supplementary Fig. S10I), which is expected for a minimal model that contains only few free parameters describing heterochromatin self-association.

  To assess the presence or absence of VELCROs and to further investigate the involvement of loops, we used the simulations to generate APAs. Dispersed contacts were detected for genomic regions that contained HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites but no anchors of chromatin loops. In contrast, such contacts were significantly reduced when regions containing anchors of chromatin loops were investigated (Fig. 6C). The results imply that contacts between regions containing high densities of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 are sufficient to obtain key features of the 3D organization of C. elegans chromosomes.

  Next, we wondered whether compartmentalization driven by VELCROs shows hallmarks of a first-order phase transition [20, 21, 98]. More specifically, we asked if chromosomes toggle between two discrete states, adopting either a compartmentalized conformation or a state with impaired compartmentalization, or, alternatively, if there are stable intermediate conformations reflecting a continuous transition. To drive the transition in silico, we started from the compartmentalized situation and gradually reduced the density of triple-positive sites in the B compartment. This resulted ultimately in the loss of VELCROs and the loss of large-scale compartmental segregation (Fig. 6D–G). To define the characteristics of the transition, we assessed the interaction profiles among HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites (via simulated APAs), the size of the central chromosomal region (mostly A compartment), the size of the chromosomal arms (mostly B compartment), the chromatin compartmentalization reflected by the PC1 values, and the relative arm–arm separation, i.e. the spatial separation between both arms divided by that between arms and the central region (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. S11A). When HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites were gradually removed in the simulation, the interactions among the remaining sites persisted down to a critical density of 70%–80% and then became weaker and less delocalized (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. S11A,B). The size of the central chromosomal region was not affected by the dilution, while the chromosomal arms de-compacted gradually. The difference to the compartmentalization experimentally observed for the wild-type condition, which we quantified as the sum of the squared differences between both PC1 vectors, started to increase gradually when triple-positive sites were diluted below a critical density of 70%–80% (Fig. 6G, bottom left). Interestingly, the relative spatial separation showed bimodal distributions, reflecting distinct conformations with and without associated arms (Fig. 6G, bottom right). Below a critical density of ~40% of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites, the open conformation without associated arms prevailed. This behaviour is reminiscent of a first-order phase transition [20, 21, 98]. When HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites were scrambled within arms and within the central region, such that the average peak numbers in both types of regions were kept constant while local enrichments were dissolved, both VELCROs and large-scale genome compartmentalization were weakened (Supplementary Fig. S11C–F). According to these findings, local enrichments of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites are particularly potent in driving compartment segregation.

  To further characterize the folding state of the genome for different densities of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites, we assessed the genomic contact probabilities in the B compartment. In agreement with our interpretation of long-range interactions in mutants with impaired compartmentalization (Fig. 3A), the scaling exponent increased gradually as the densities of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites were reduced, indicating that the chromosomal arms transition from a more crumpled toward an equilibrium globule (Supplementary Fig. S11B). Overall, our simulation of a minimal model focused on the capacity of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites to organize the genome shows that the segregation of heterochromatin from euchromatin of C. elegans chromosomes can be recuperated by VELCROs. The analysis implies that their strength and distribution are determined by the density of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites in the genome.


## Discussion

### Simultaneous enrichment in HP1, an MBT-domain factor and H3K9me2 drive genome compartmentalization
  Our results imply that genomic sites that contain HPL-2, LIN-61, and H3K9me2 drive the segregation of eu- and heterochromatin in C. elegans embryos at multiple scales ranging from small to large compartments, e.g. subcompartments to chromosome arm-to-arm interactions. Regions that do not contain all three components, i.e. those containing only one or two of them are insufficient predictors of compartmentalization. The fact that neither hpl-2 nor lin-61 single mutants show impaired compartmentalization implies redundancy of these factors. At the molecular level, this redundancy introduces robustness. While redundancy in the factors working in conjunction with H3K9me in heterochromatin organization is supported by Drosophila and mouse pericentromeres still self-associating after loss of the H3K9-binding HP1a/HP1α [26, 99, 100], we note that the system of establishing triple positive HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 sites in C. elegans embryos is complex, since the H3K9 KMTs MET-2 and SET-25 are molecularly interlinked with HPL-2 and LIN-61, respectively [92, 101]. This is reflected by the hpl-2, lin-61 and lin-61; hpl-2 mutants showing variable changes in H3K9me2 and H3K9me3 distribution in addition to the loss of the respective proteins (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S8).

  Although our results indicate that H3K9me2 plays a crucial role in compartmentalization, developmental chromatin states are combinatorial and plastic [102]. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that the observed phenotypes are partly driven by the redistribution of other chromatin marks in the mutant conditions that we did not analyse. We also emphasize that entirely eliminating H3K9me causes diverse issues, such as repeat derepression, R-loop accumulation, and genome instability [41, 42, 85]. In a mammalian cell line system, ablating all H3K9me states via a six-KMT knockout completely dismantles heterochromatin organization [103]. Together with our findings, this implies that the chromatin mark is essential while the binding proteins are redundant.


### Chromatin compartments are linked to Varied Ensembles of Long-range ChROmatin interactions
  Our results indicate that genomic regions with high densities of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple-positive sites represent broad chromatin interaction interfaces that are not linked to isolated, deterministic and positionally defined contacts forming chromatin loops, but enable VELCRO interactions with other corresponding regions (Fig. 7). As these interaction patterns do not involve well-defined pair-wise associations but rather networks of combinatorial contacts, we propose the term VELCROs to describe them. VELCROs are intrinsically stochastic in nature. We favour the view that these are reflective of widely dynamic/stochastic interactions present in individual cells in a population (Fig. 7A). However, this interpretation requires further experiments. Because we analysed a mixture of cells at varying developmental stages, a wide range of stable and deterministic interactions present in late-stage C. elegans embryos might contribute to VELCROs. We note, though, that the preparations of late-stage C. elegans embryos used contain a limited window of 60–200 cell stages (i.e. 2–3 cell divisions), which might not provide enough heterogeneity to produce the broadly dispersed appearance of interactions seen for VELCROs.

  Notably, the size of VELCROs that is around 50 kb (see spread of the diffuse pattern in Fig. 5B) corresponds to the dimension of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple positive sites (Fig. 5C) and the size of subcompartments in C. elegans [39]. We point out that the combinatorial VELCRO concept aligns with the dynamic nature of heterochromatin that involves transient HP1 binding to H3K9-methylated nucleosomes [104]. Interestingly, the interactions of the B compartment with LADs seem also to rely on numerous and cooperative interactions [105], thereby suggesting a general principle for the compartmentalization of heterochromatin.

  Further work will need to clarify the link between VELCROs, TADs, and chromatin loops, since our data show that VELCROs also form inside TADs that may be the result of loop extrusion [15]. Also, the relation of lamina-dependent mechanisms of A/B compartmentalization and VELCROs will need to be defined. Lastly, it will be interesting to see whether other chromatin components (DNA and histone modification binding proteins, chromatin remodelling factors etc.) contribute to VELCROs.


### Molecular biology of VELCROs
  Since the direct contribution of H3K9me2 to chromatin interactions seems limited [28], VELCROs must largely depend on the specific biochemical features of the HPL-2 and LIN-61 proteins and their interaction partners. The chromodomain of HPL-2 and the MBT domains of LIN-61 have been shown to bind H3K9me, albeit with limited selectivity for binding H3K9me2 versus H3K9me3 [44]. Protein multivalency enabling bridging and liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) behaviour have been proposed as key driver of chromatin compartmentalization [106]. Via their chromoshadow domain, HP1-type proteins form dimers of H3K9me-binding domains [27] and their hinge region has been shown to drive LLPS [107, 108]. Little is known about the biochemical mechanisms that make LIN-61 molecularly similar to, and functionally redundant with, HPL-2. The VELCROs that we identified are consistent with bridging- and/or LLPS-driven mechanisms. To establish robust interactions between distal chromatin regions, both require local enrichment of a ‘gluing’ component, either for direct bridging or for exceeding the threshold concentration for LLPS.


### The compartmentalized state of the genome is buffered against perturbations
  While phase separation as a concept of polymer physics has been put forward to describe the segregation of eu- and heterochromatin [109, 110], experiments testing how such a compartmentalized state is reached in a living organism have not been available. Defining the minimal parameters that enable folding of C. elegans chromosomes allowed us to investigate this transition in silico, which is characterized by an abrupt change of the large-scale chromatin conformation upon a continuous change of the density and strength of VELCROs. Our reductionist system is based on a steady state consideration that is not designed to accurately reproduce the kinetics of the transition. These kinetics might both be relevant when considering de novo establishment and the cell cycle. Compartments are erased during mitosis and progressively reestablished after mitotic exit starting from longitudinally compressed loop arrays [111, 112]. Binding proteins such as HP1 are released from their target sites, while the H3K9me marks are retained [113]. While integrating the kinetics of compartmentalization across the cell cycle or during differentiation will require a more complex, heavily parameterized model, simulations with starting points loosely resembling interphase (i.e. more dispersed) and post-mitotic (i.e. more ordered and compacted) chromatin conformations produced the same compartmentalized outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S11G). The fundamental transitions we describe might, therefore, not only occur when compartments are established de novo [37] but also during the cell cycle.

  In our model, compartments arise when the net attraction among heterochromatin segments, which scales with the density of proteins that mediate chromatin bridging, exceeds a critical threshold. Beyond this threshold, the genome adopts a compartmentalized conformation, which is buffered against changes in the density of long-range interactions down to 80% of the wild-type level (Fig. 6G). This confers a robustness to the compartmentalized state that may be sufficient to keep the genome folded upon various molecular (e.g. DNA replication and repair) and physiological (e.g. developmental or environmental cues) challenges. Beyond the density of HPL-2 + LIN-61 + H3K9me2 triple positive sites, the binding kinetics of HPL-2 and LIN-61 might further contribute to a threshold behaviour that buffers the genome against perturbation.
