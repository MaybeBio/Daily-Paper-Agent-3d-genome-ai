# CCAN forms the spherical shell-like structure in interphase kinetochores


## Abstract
  The kinetochore is an essential structure for the faithful chromosome segregation during mitosis. The 16-protein complex known as the constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN) localizes the centromere throughout the cell cycle and forms the basis of the kinetochore. Although cryo–electron microscopy has provided a structural model of an individual CCAN unit, how multiple CCAN units are organized within cells remains unclear. Here, using in silico and in vivo analyses, we demonstrate that multiple CCAN units form a spherical shell-like structure in interphase DT40 kinetochores. CENP-T and CENP-N localize to the CCAN shell, whereas the CENP-C cupin domain clusters at the center. Molecular dynamics simulations of CCAN assembly suggest that CENP-C–DNA interactions constrain the orientation of CCAN units, supporting a spherical shell-like organization via CENP-C oligomerization. Super-resolution imaging reveals the spherical shell-like structure at DT40 kinetochores, and CENP-C knockout or expression of a CENP-C DNA binding deficient mutant abolishes the structure, consistent with the simulation-derived model. These findings provide important insights into the spatial organization of functional kinetochores within cells.


## INTRODUCTION
  The kinetochore is a large protein complex formed at the centromere of each chromosome. This ensures accurate segregation of the chromosomes to daughter cells during mitosis and meiosis in all eukaryotes. It is composed of two major complexes: the constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN) and the KMN (KNL1, Mis12, and Ndc80 complexes) network (1–6). In vertebrates, CCAN is represented by a 16-protein complex that forms the basis of interphase kinetochores, which are defined by the histone H3 variant CENP-A (7–15). During the late G2 and M phases, CCAN recruits the KMN network, which binds directly to spindle microtubules, forming a functional kinetochore (2, 3, 16–20).

  Cryo–electron microscopy (EM) analyses have proposed structural models of the human CCAN associated with the CENP-A nucleosome (6, 21, 22). Although these models provide valuable insights into the molecular organization of an individual CCAN unit, they lack critical structural information for understanding functional CCAN assembly. In particular, the C-terminal region of CENP-C, an essential CCAN protein (23–28), was truncated in the proposed CCAN structure models (21, 22). In addition, a substantial portion of CENP-C could not be mapped due to its disordered characteristics. Consequently, the contribution of CENP-C to kinetochore organization remains unclear. Furthermore, because each kinetochore contains dozens of CCAN copies, it is largely unknown how multiple CCAN units are organized spatially within cells.

  We previously examined the functional roles of CENP-C domains by combining genetics and structural biology using chicken DT40 cells (29–31). In these studies, we demonstrated that both the N-terminal KMN binding and CENP-A–binding domains of CENP-C are dispensable for mitotic progression. Instead, the CCAN-binding domain (CBD) or the C-terminal cupin domain is essential for CENP-C function. Furthermore, we showed that the vertebrate CENP-C cupin domain exhibits oligomerization activity, which is essential for CENP-C function (31). A fusion of the CBD and the C-terminal cupin domain (called “Mini-CENP-C”) rescues the CENP-C knockout phenotype. This suggests that an essential role of CENP-C is organizing CCAN via direct binding and oligomerization (6, 31). However, the contribution of CENP-C oligomerization properties to the higher-order assembly of multiple CCAN units and the actual structure of CCAN formed within cells remain unclear.

  In this study, we created coarse-grained (CG) models of the Mini-CENP-C and CCAN subcomplexes. We then performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to examine how CENP-C constrains the relative organization of CCAN units. Combined with biochemical analyses and mutational analyses using chicken DT40 cells, our MD simulations revealed that CENP-C–DNA interactions promote a face-to-face orientation between CCAN units. The simulations further support a model in which CCAN units adopt a spherical shell-like organization around a central cluster of CENP-C cupin domains. Last, super-resolution imaging revealed that CCAN forms a spherical shell-like structure in interphase DT40 kinetochores and that CENP-C–DNA interactions are required for its proper organization. These findings provide important insights into the spatial organization of the functional kinetochore within cells.


## RESULTS

### CENP-C contributes to the organized orientation of CCAN units in the presence of DNA
  To investigate how CENP-C cupin domain oligomerization contributes to higher-order kinetochore organization, we performed MD simulations on a simplified system consisting of two CCAN units bound to a CENP-C dimer. We first generated a dimeric chicken CCAN structural model as the initial model for the MD simulations by combining AlphaFold3 (AF3) predictions (32) with additional model building (Fig. 1A and fig. S1A; see Methods). To reduce the computational cost while retaining essential CENP-C functions, we used Mini-CENP-C, in which the CBD (amino acids 166 to 324) is fused with a C-terminal cupin-containing region (amino acids 677 to 864), instead of full-length CENP-C (Fig. 1A, left). A previous study has shown that Mini-CENP-C retains essential CENP-C functions (31). Mini-CENP-C contains a CENP-L/N–binding motif and a cupin domain connected by an intrinsically disordered region (IDR). Thus, two CCAN units are expected to be positioned in close proximity through cupin-mediated dimerization (Fig. 1A, right). We generated AF3-predicted structural models of the CCAN unit containing the N-terminal half of the Mini-CENP-C CBD (amino acids 166 to 235), which includes the CENP-L/N–binding motif. A 40–base pair (bp) DNA fragment was also included in the AF3 prediction of the CCAN unit because the CCAN complex forms a continuous DNA binding interface on its inner surface (21, 22). The predicted chicken CCAN model closely resembled the cryo-EM structure of the human CCAN core complex (21, 22), supporting the reliability of the predicted structure (fig. S1A). We also generated a model of the dimeric Mini-CENP-C cupin domain (amino acids 706 to 864) (fig. S1A). The IDR in each Mini-CENP-C fragment connecting the CBD and cupin domains was excluded from the AF3-predicted models because of low prediction confidence and was subsequently modeled using MODELLER (Fig. 1A, right). The two CCAN units and the dimeric cupin domains were then connected through the modeled Mini-CENP-C IDRs, resulting in a macromolecular assembly ∼50 nm in size (Fig. 1A and fig. S1B). CG-MD simulations based on the Martini model are suitable for systems of this scale (33, 34).

  Starting from the constructed models, we examined how two CCAN units tethered by Mini-CENP-C settle into equilibrium states during the CG-MD simulations (Fig. 1B and movie S1). During the initial 100 ns, the extended IDR in each Mini-CENP-C chain contracted from ∼20 to 7 to 8 nm, drawing the two CCAN units closer together (Fig. 1B and fig. S1C). The CCAN units subsequently rearranged while maintaining their separation and adopted a face-to-face orientation by 500 ns (Fig. 1B). No direct interaction between the CCAN units was observed. To assess dependence on the initial configuration, we generated six CCAN–Mini-CENP-C models with different relative orientations (figs. S1B and S2A). For each configuration, we quantified temporal changes in the relative orientation of the DNA-axis vectors associated with the two CCAN units (Fig. 1B; see Methods). Three independent CG-MD simulations were performed from each initial configuration. Despite the different starting orientations, the simulations consistently approached a face-to-face orientation within the simulated timescale (Fig. 1C and fig. S2, B and D). In the absence of Mini-CENP-C, by contrast, the CCAN units diffused independently and moved apart without orientational alignment (Fig. 1D and movie S2). Across six initial configurations, each simulated in three independent trajectories, no consistent face-to-face alignment was observed without Mini-CENP-C (Fig. 1E and fig. S2, C and E). These results suggest that Mini-CENP-C promotes face-to-face alignment of the CCAN units, whereas no such alignment was observed in the absence of Mini-CENP-C.

  Given that each CCAN unit binds DNA, we next examined whether DNA influences the Mini-CENP-C–mediated orientation of the CCAN units. In simulations lacking the DNA fragments, the two CCAN units remained in close proximity (Fig. 1F and movie S3). However, across six initial configurations, each simulated in three independent trajectories, their relative orientations were more diverse than those observed in the presence of DNA (Fig. 1G and fig. S2, F and G). These results suggest that DNA imposes additional constraints that restrict CCAN motion and stabilize the face-to-face orientation mediated by Mini-CENP-C.


### DNA binding by a basic stretch in the CENP-C CBD contributes to the organization of CCAN units
  To investigate the molecular basis of the DNA-dependent CCAN orientation, we focused on the interaction between Mini-CENP-C and DNA. In the AF3-predicted structure, the DNA contacts a cluster of positively charged residues [Lys216/Arg223/Arg226 (K216/R223/R226)] located near the CENP-L/N–binding motif of Mini-CENP-C (Figs. 1A and 2A). This contact was not observed in the cryo-EM structures of the human CCAN complex (21, 22). In the CG-MD simulations, the CENP-C–DNA contact was maintained for at least 500 ns (Fig. 1B and fig. S2B). This CENP-C region contains an extended basic stretch with additional potential DNA binding residues (Fig. 1A). Therefore, we performed MD simulations using a complex model with mutations introduced to this region [Lys216→Glu/Arg223→Glu/Arg226→Glu (K216E/R223E/R226E)]. Hereafter, these mutations are referred to as the 3E mutation. These residues are conserved across species (Fig. 1A). To characterize the CENP-C–DNA interaction at higher resolution, we performed 100-ns all-atom MD simulations of a model containing the Mini-CENP-C CBD (amino acids 166 to 237), CENP-L/N, and DNA. CENP-L/N was selected as a representative CCAN subcomplex because it forms a DNA binding grip within the CCAN-DNA complex. In the wild-type (WT) simulations, the CENP-C basic stretch remained associated with DNA and extended along the DNA axis (Fig. 2B). In the Mini-CENP-C3E simulations, the basic stretch moved away from DNA (Fig. 2C), supporting a role for this region in stabilizing CENP-C–DNA binding. Consistent with this simulation-derived prediction, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) revealed that the 3E mutation reduced the DNA binding ability of CENP-C CBD (Fig. 2D). We note that the DNA binding ability of the CENP-C CBD was not completely abolished, indicating that other regions of CENP-C may also contribute to DNA binding.

  We further examined the effect of Mini-CENP-C3E in the full CG-MD model. The two CCAN units showed reduced face-to-face orientational order in the presence of Mini-CENP-C3E (Fig. 2E and movie S4). Six distinct initial configurations were each simulated in three independent trajectories (fig. S2H). The Mini-CENP-C3E simulations consistently showed less orientational convergence than the WT simulations, suggesting that the 3E mutation destabilizes the orientational ensemble of the CCAN units (Fig. 2F and fig. S2I).

  To further evaluate the role of DNA, we examined the interaction between Mini-CENP-C and CENP-L/N using in vitro glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays in the presence or absence of DNA (Fig. 2G). DNA facilitated the interaction between Mini-CENP-C and CENP-L/N. Next, we prepared the Mini-CENP-C3E mutant and tested effects on its DNA binding activity for the complex formation with CENP-L/N by EMSA. The 3E mutation abolished the formation of the complex between Mini-CENP-C and CENP-L/N on DNA (Fig. 2H). We note that the 3E mutation did not compromise the interaction of Mini-CENP-C with CENP-L/N in the absence of DNA (fig. S2J). These results suggest that the DNA binding of CENP-C mediated by the basic stretch in the CBD region contributes to the maintenance of the molecular assembly and dynamic behavior of the CCAN units in the context of centromeric DNA.

  On the basis of the simulation and biochemical results, we conclude that dimeric CENP-C imposes spatial constraints on the organization of two CCAN units. Specifically, DNA binding through the conserved basic stretch of CENP-C contributes to stabilizing a defined face-to-face orientation of the two CCAN units.


### CG-MD simulations suggest a shell-like organization of multiple CCAN units around a central CENP-C core
  Each kinetochore contains several dozen CCAN copies (35, 36), which may form clusters within cells. To examine the structural feasibility and orientation of a multi-CCAN assembly, we generated an initial model containing eight CCAN units, each bound to 40 bp of DNA, and four Mini-CENP-C dimers [8 (CCAN+DNA) + 4 Mini-CENP-C dimers] (fig. S3A; see Methods). The four CENP-C cupin dimers were preassembled at the center using the oligomeric interface observed in the previously reported crystal structure (31). The eight CCAN units were assigned different initial orientations and positioned at radial distances of 160 to 180 Å from this cupin core. Three independent CG-MD simulations were performed for each condition.

  In the absence of Mini-CENP-C, the CCAN units did not acquire a defined mutual orientation, even in the presence of DNA (Fig. 3A and movie S5). This is consistent with the simulations of two isolated CCAN units (Fig. 1D). In the model containing the preassembled octameric Mini-CENP-C cupin core, the eight CCAN units were drawn toward the core, and the DNA associated with each CCAN unit adopted an inward orientation (Fig. 3B and movie S6). The IDRs of all eight Mini-CENP-C chains converged to similar lengths of 7 to 8 nm (fig. S1C). During the simulation, the constructed assembly maintained and refined a spherical shell-like organization, with the CENP-T/W/S/X and CENP-L/N subcomplexes positioned toward the outside and the CENP-C cupin domains remaining in the central region (Fig. 3B and fig. S3A). The alignment index approached ∼+1 in all three independent trajectories (Fig. 3C and fig. S3B), indicating that the DNA axis of each CCAN unit remained preferentially oriented toward the cupin core within the simulated timescale.

  Because the basic stretch of Mini-CENP-C contributed to the face-to-face orientation of two CCAN units, we examined its role in the multi-CCAN assembly. Simulations using Mini-CENP-C3E produced assemblies with reduced orientational order, although the CCAN-DNA units remained tethered to the cupin core (Fig. 3D and movie S7). The CENP-T/W/S/X and CENP-L/N subcomplexes adopted a more heterogeneous spatial distribution than in the WT simulations. Unlike the WT condition, the alignment index did not approach +1 in any of Mini-CENP-C3E trajectories, indicating that the 3E mutation disrupts the inward radial orientation of the CCAN-bound DNA axis (Fig. 3E and fig. S3C). Together, these simulations support the structural feasibility of a shell-like CCAN organization around an oligomerized cupin core and suggest that CENP-C–DNA interactions promote the inward orientation of individual CCAN units.


### CENP-C–DNA interaction is critical for kinetochore function
  MD simulations and biochemical analyses using recombinant proteins suggest that the CENP-C–DNA interactions contribute to proper CCAN organization. To experimentally test these results, we examined the functional importance of the CENP-C basic stretch in chicken DT40 cells. For this experiment, we used a tetracycline (Tet)–inducible conditional CENP-C knockout (cKO–CENP-C) DT40 cell line (29, 37). Two endogenous CENP-C alleles are disrupted, and CENP-C is expressed from a cDNA under the control of a Tet-responsible promoter in the cKO–CENP-C cells (37). After Tet addition, CENP-C expression was repressed in the cKO–CENP-C cells, which then died after mitotic arrest (Fig. 3F); however, expressing WT Mini-CENP-C (Mini-CENP-Cwt) rescued the phenotype (31). Consistent with the previous results, the mitotic index (less than 10%) did not increase in cKO–CENP-C cells expressing Mini-CENP-Cwt after the addition of Tet (Fig. 3F, Mini-CENP-Cwt). However, expression of Mini-CENP-C with the K216E/R223E/R226E (3E) mutation in the basic stretch failed to rescue mitotic accumulation in cKO–CENP-C cells after the addition of Tet (Fig. 3F, Mini-CENP-C3E). We tested three independent clones of cKO–CENP-C cells expressing Mini-CENP-C3E. After the addition of Tet, the mitotic index increased by 10 to 20% in all clones, comparable to that observed in cKO–CENP-C cells (Fig. 3F). In addition to the increased mitotic index observed in cKO–CENP-C cells expressing Mini-CENP-C3E, abnormal mitotic cells were frequently observed in these clones. Well-aligned chromosomes with bipolar spindles were observed in cKO–CENP-C cells expressing Mini-CENP-Cwt. However, multipolar spindles and unaligned chromosomes were observed in cells expressing Mini-CENP-C3E (Fig. 3G). These results indicate that the CENP-C basic stretch and its DNA binding activity are critical for CENP-C function.


### The spherical shell-like structure is observed in DT40 kinetochores
  Our CG-MD simulations support a model in which multiple copies of CCAN units adopt a spherical shell-like organization around oligomerized CENP-C cupin domains (Fig. 3B). To investigate the existence of a structure in DT40 cells similar to our simulated kinetochore model, we examined the localization of CENP-T and the C-terminal region of CENP-C. These are predicted to reside in the outer and inner regions of the kinetochore structure, respectively. To this end, we immunostained DT40 cells in which endogenous CENP-T or CENP-C was replaced with mScarlet–HA–SNAP–CENP-T (this study) or CENP-C–green fluorescent protein (GFP) (30), respectively, with anti–CENP-T or anti-GFP antibodies. Because high-resolution imaging is required to resolve the detailed architecture of the kinetochore, we used expansion microscopy (ExM) for observations (38). We basically compared the relative positions of kinetochore proteins to eliminate any influence of variation in the expansion factor, although our expansion method reproducibly expands samples as previously reported (38): The expansion factor typically varies within ±1.1. Using ExM, we observed CENP-T as a spherical shell-like structure with no signal detected in the central region (Fig. 4A and fig. S4A; see XZ and YZ plane images). In striking contrast, the C-terminal region of CENP-C appeared as discrete, evenly distributed dot-like structures throughout the entire kinetochore (Fig. 4B and fig. S4A; see XZ and YZ plane images). Next, we examined the relative localization of CENP-T and CENP-C within one kinetochore with a spherical shell-like structure. We expressed CENP-C fused with FLAG at either the N terminus or C terminus (FLAG–CENP-C or CENP-C–FLAG) in DT40 cells and observed their localization relative to CENP-T using ExM. The C terminus of CENP-C was positioned inside the CENP-T shell (Fig. 4C, top). To quantitatively assess their spatial relationship, we performed rotational averaging followed by intensity profiling of the averaged structure (Fig. 4C, bottom and right). The signals from the CENP-C C terminus appeared inside the CENP-T signal, indicating that the CENP-C C terminus localizes to the core of the shell-like structure. In contrast, the N terminus of CENP-C overlapped with CENP-T (Fig. 4D), suggesting that CENP-C positions its N terminus toward the shell surface. These findings are consistent with our MD simulation model of Mini-CENP-C in which the N-terminal CCAN binding site is positioned at the shell surface and the C-terminal cupin domain in the core (Fig. 3B). These two regions are linked by the IDR of Mini-CENP-C (Fig. 1A, right), which lacks the remaining portion of the IDR (amino acids 325 to 675) present in full-length CENP-C. To strengthen this model, we examined the CENP-T shell size in Tet-responsible cKO–CENP-C DT40 cells (+Tet) expressing Mini-CENP-C. As expected, the CENP-T shell size was reduced, compared with cells expressing WT CENP-C (fig. S4, B and C). Together, these results suggest that the CENP-T shell surrounds the CENP-C core.


### The CENP-T shell-like structure is a genuine feature of the interphase kinetochore
  Because the CENP-T staining profile that forms the spherical shell-like structure may be a technical artifact, we tested a series of experimental conditions to rule out this possibility. First, we suspected that the CENP-T antibody’s characteristic recognition property could result in shell-like staining. To investigate this possibility, we examined the recognition site of the anti–CENP-T antibody via Western blotting (fig. S5A). The antibody recognized the entire CENP-T region, suggesting that the shell-like staining was not due to antibody specificity. Second, we considered the effect of tagging. Because we used mScarlet–HA–SNAP–CENP-T for CENP-T staining in DT40 cells, we examined whether endogenous CENP-T exhibited a similar structure in WT cells (fig. S5B). A comparable spherical shell-like structure was observed, suggesting that tagging was not responsible. Third, we suspected poor antibody penetration. To address this possibility, we immunostained cells expressing mScarlet–HA–SNAP–CENP-T using anti–CENP-T and anti-HA antibodies. Both antibodies revealed similar spherical shell-like structures (fig. S5C). Because the kinetochore core was accessible to the anti-GFP antibody, as demonstrated by CENP-C staining, it is unlikely that the shell-like structure resulted from limited antibody penetration. Next, to rule out the possibility that the shell-like structure was an artifact of our ExM procedure, we applied an alternative ExM procedure: ultrastructure ExM (U-ExM), which has been reported to better preserve native structures (39). A similar spherical shell-like structure was observed again under U-ExM in combination with deconvolution or super-resolution microscopy (fig. S5D). Last, we examined the shell-like structure stained with an anti–CENP-T antibody using super-resolution microscopies without the expansion process. The shell-like structure was consistently observed by different super-resolution imaging methods as well (fig. S5, E and F). These analyses strongly suggest that the CENP-T shell-like structure is genuine. Together, they indicate that the CENP-T shell surrounding a CENP-C core is a bona fide feature of interphase kinetochore architecture.


### The position of CCAN proteins in the spherical shell-like structure
  Next, we analyzed the localization of CENP-N, another CCAN component that binds to CENP-C via the CBD located in the N-terminal region (amino acids 166 to 324). We generated DT40 cells in which the endogenous CENP-N and CENP-C were replaced with CENP-N–FLAG and CENP-C–GFP, respectively. Then, we investigated the localization of CENP-T with CENP-N and CENP-C–GFP with CENP-N using ExM (Fig. 5, A and B). CENP-N colocalized with CENP-T, exhibiting a shell-like profile (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the C-terminal region of CENP-C was positioned internally and adjacent to CENP-N (Fig. 5B). These observations are consistent with previous structural models, which suggest that the N-terminal region of CENP-C contacts CENP-L/N and that CENP-T directly contacts CENP-H/I/K/M and CENP-L/N (21, 22). Because the CENP-C C-terminal region binds to the CENP-A nucleosome (Fig. 1A), we examined the localization of CENP-A. We found that CENP-A colocalizes with CENP-C–GFP (Fig. 5C), suggesting that CENP-A resides at the CENP-C core. In addition, we note that the kinetochore forms within a 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)–sparse region surrounded by DAPI-dense chromatin (Figs. 4, A and B, and 5, A to C), as suggested by cryo–electron tomography analysis (40). Because CENP-A is localized at the core, centromeric chromatin appears to extrude from the surrounding heterochromatin and be incorporated into the kinetochore core.

  On the basis of the ExM analyses, we summarize the localization profile of the CCAN proteins in the interphase kinetochore structure (Fig. 5D). We conclude that the interphase kinetochore in DT40 cells forms a spherical shell-like structure, in which the CENP-C core is surrounded by the CCAN complex. This finding is consistent with our MD simulation model.


### The spherical shell-like structure is most pronounced during S to G2 phases
  Next, we investigated how the spherical shell-like structure forms during cell cycle progression. To this end, we used analog-sensitive cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1-as) DT40 mutant cells (41), which can be highly synchronized at defined cell cycle stages. Cells were treated with 1-NM-PP1 for 10 hours to arrest them at the G2 phase. Then, cells were released and samples for immunofluorescence were collected every hour (Fig. 6A). Using EdU incorporation as a S-phase marker, we confirmed that the time points at 1, 2, and 3 hours corresponded to the G1, early S, and mid S phases, respectively (fig. S6). ExM analysis revealed that CENP-T formed tight, small, dot-like structures during the G1 and early S phases. The shell-like structure gradually emerged at the mid S phase and was most pronounced at the G2 phase (for quantification, see Fig. 6, B and C). These results indicate that the formation of the spherical shell-like structure occurs predominantly from the S to G2 phases of the cell cycle.


### CENP-C–DNA interaction is required for proper formation of the spherical shell-like structure in interphase DT40 cells
  CENP-C is located at the core of the CCAN spherical shell, and our simulations suggest that it contributes to the alignment of CCAN units (Figs. 1 to 3). Therefore, we conducted experiments to test how CENP-C contributes to the formation of the CCAN structure. For this purpose, we examined CENP-T localization in Tet-responsible cKO–CENP-C DT40 cells using ExM. In the presence of CENP-C (no Tet addition), we observed a spherical, shell-like CENP-T structure [Fig. 7, A and B, CENP-C +: doxycycline (Dox) −]. The average size of the shell-like structure was 2.03 μm in ExM (Fig. 7C). In contrast, the shell-like structure of CENP-T was abolished in the absence of CENP-C (Tet addition: Dox +), and CENP-T signals were aggregated at the center. The kinetochore size in the cells without CENP-C expression (Tet addition: Dox +) was significantly smaller than that in the cells expressing CENP-C (no Tet addition: Dox −) (Fig. 7, A to C). These results support the idea that CENP-C is required for forming the spherical, shell-like CCAN structure in DT40 cells.

  Our MD simulations and biochemical analysis suggested that CENP-C–DNA interaction is required for the spherical shell-like structure formation via CENP-C oligomerization. Therefore, to examine the impact of the CENP-C–DNA interaction on the shell-like structure formation in cells, we used the CENP-C3E mutant, which is defective in DNA binding and exhibits reduced interaction with the CENP-L/N complex (Fig. 2). We expressed the FLAG-tagged full-length CENP-Cwt (FLAG–CENP-Cwt) or CENP-C3E mutant (FLAG–CENP-C3E) in Tet-responsible cKO–CENP-C DT40 cells and examined the shell-like structure formation in the presence of Tet. The shell-like structure was compromised in cells expressing FLAG–CENP-C3E, and the averaged shell size was significantly decreased from 2.16 μm (wt) to 1.79 μm (3E) (Fig. 7, D to F). The shell size in FLAG–CENP-C3E–expressing cells was significantly larger than that of cKO–CENP-C cells (fig. S7). These results indicate that the CENP-C–DNA interaction is required for proper formation of the spherical shell-like structure in DT40 cells.


## DISCUSSION
  CCAN is present in tens of copies at each kinetochore within cells (35, 36). Although various cryo-EM single-particle analyses have provided structural details of individual CCAN units, understanding how multiple CCAN units assemble within cells to form functional kinetochores remains one of the most central challenges in centromere/kinetochore biology. Here, we addressed this issue by combining MD simulations with high-resolution imaging. Our ExM observation demonstrates that multiple CCAN units form a finite-size spherical shell-like structure with an ∼200-nm size in interphase kinetochores, in which the CENP-C core is surrounded by the CCAN subcomplexes.

  This characteristic structure was not observed in mitotic kinetochores, which instead adopt disklike plate structures, as observed by electron microscopy and ExM (38, 42). These results suggest that the shell-like organization observed in our study is an interphase feature. However, a previous study reported ringlike organization of outer kinetochore proteins in mitotic Xenopus cells, which was disrupted by microtubule depolymerization (43). This ringlike organization may transform into a disklike structure after proper microtubule attachment. Andronov et al. (44) previously reported a CENP-A ringlike structure in human cells that is most prominent in early G1 and becomes compact as the cell cycle progresses. By contrast, the CCAN shell-like structure described here was compact in G1 and most pronounced in G2 (Fig. 6), suggesting that it is distinct from the early G1 CENP-A organization. A CENP-A–containing core was retained when the CENP-T shell was clearly observed in G2, consistent with previous observations (44). Because various outer kinetochore proteins bind to CENP-T (6, 29, 45, 46), the G2 CCAN shell may provide a platform for outer kinetochore assembly. In this context, efficient microtubule capture during the M phase requires a highly organized disklike kinetochore. The interphase shell-like organization may therefore represent a preorganized state that facilitates this transition. Consistent with our observations, a related shell-like structure has also been observed in Xenopus cells (47).

  Our results demonstrate that CENP-C plays a pivotal role in establishing the shell-like structure; this structure becomes disorganized in the absence of CENP-C (Fig. 7A). Because loss of CENP-C causes mitotic arrest, it remains possible that the disorganized shell-like structure arises from indirect effects associated with mitotic arrest rather than a direct role of CENP-C. Although this possibility cannot be completely excluded, disruption of the shell-like structure was observed 48 hours after Tet addition, whereas pronounced mitotic arrest becomes apparent at ∼72 hours (29). This temporal difference, together with the simulation results, favors a model in which CENP-C directly contributes to CCAN shell-like organization. Mini-CENP-C localizes to interphase kinetochores but is nearly absent from mitotic kinetochores, which nevertheless remain functional (31), suggesting that CENP-C contributes only minimally to the architecture of the mature mitotic kinetochore. However, mutations that impair CENP-C oligomerization cause mitotic abnormalities (31), suggesting that CENP-C–mediated CCAN organization during interphase may contribute to formation of a functional kinetochore upon entry into the M phase. Alternatively, because shell formation occurs mainly from S to G2, this organization could participate in interphase-specific events such as centromeric DNA replication or transcription. Further experiments are required to establish the physiological significance of this interphase-specific structure.

  In cryo-EM structures of the human CCAN-DNA complex, CENP-L/N forms a tunnel that grips the DNA strand extending from the CENP-A nucleosome core (21, 22). CENP-C contains the conserved CENP-L/N–binding motif, which is followed by a basic stretch (Fig. 1A). Our MD simulations suggest that this basic stretch can interact dynamically with DNA as it enters the CENP-L/N tunnel (Fig. 2A). Electrostatic contacts between the CENP-C basic residues and the DNA phosphate backbone were not resolved in the cryo-EM structures, potentially because of their transient nature. These interactions may nevertheless contribute to the orientational organization of higher-order CCAN assemblies (Fig. 3). Simultaneous interactions of CENP-C with both CENP-L/N and DNA may anchor the CCAN to chromosomal DNA and constrain the mobility of the CCAN-DNA complex within chromatin. Consistent with this model, Mini-CENP-C exhibited association with CENP-L/N on DNA in vitro, and this association was disrupted by the CENP-C3E mutant (Fig. 2H). At the assembly level, DNA fragments associated with individual CCAN units in the eight-CCAN CG-MD model were oriented preferentially toward the CENP-C cupin core. In contrast, the Mini-CENP-C3E model exhibited reduced orientational order (Fig. 3, B and D). The eight-CCAN CG-MD model is a simplified representation of the cellular assembly. However, it captures the relative topology and orientational organization of the CCAN components. ExM analysis further showed that the 3E mutation compromised shell-like CCAN organization in cells (Fig. 7, D to F), which supports the CG-MD model.

  In our model, cupin domain oligomerization brings multiple CENP-C–bound CCAN units into proximity, whereas the conserved CENP-C basic stretch constrains their orientation through DNA binding. We attempted to assess the role of cupin domain oligomerization using oligomerization-deficient CENP-C mutants (F756P/F757G [PG] and F756P/F757P [PP]) (31). However, these mutants showed markedly reduced kinetochore localization, preventing us from separating the effect of impaired oligomerization from that of reduced recruitment.

  Here, we characterized kinetochore organization at high resolution using ExM. Recently, other approaches, such as cryo–electron tomography, have become available for analyzing high-resolution structures of supramolecular complexes in cells (40, 48–51). However, the limited number of kinetochore copies precludes efficient averaging to obtain a high-resolution structure using cryo–electron tomography (40). In such cases, combining MD simulations with high-resolution fluorescence imaging provides a powerful strategy for investigating mesoscale cellular structures between individual molecules (nanoscale) and whole cells (microscale). Using this integrated approach, we revealed the structure of the interphase kinetochore. These findings provide important insights into the spatial organization of the functional kinetochore within cells.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Initial model generation for MD simulations
  Structural models of a CCAN unit and the dimeric CENP-C cupin-containing region (CENP-C cupin) were generated using the AF3 prediction server (DeepMind/Isomorphic Labs; https://alphafoldserver.com/). The CCAN unit components in each prediction are summarized in tables S1 to S3. For each prediction, five models were generated and ranked on the basis of the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores. Model confidence was further evaluated using the predicted aligned error (PAE) and, for multimeric predictions, the interface predicted template modeling (ipTM) scores. The highest-confidence model was selected for subsequent construction of the CCAN–Mini-CENP-C assembly. Regions with low AF3 prediction confidence were excluded from the initial model (table S2). Model visualization, confidence assessment, and subsequent manipulation were performed using the UCSF ChimeraX (52).

  To construct complete dimeric CCAN–Mini-CENP-C assembly models, two CCAN unit models and one CENP-C cupin dimer were positioned ∼180 Å apart, providing sufficient spatial separation within the modeling volume. Missing Mini-CENP-C segments connecting the CCAN-binding and cupin regions were generated using a loop-modeling protocol through the ChimeraX-MODELLER interface (53). In total, six CCAN–Mini-CENP-C dimer models with distinct relative CCAN orientations were generated.

  Models containing eight CCAN units were assembled from the AF3-generated CCAN core and dimeric CENP-C cupin models. Four CENP-C cupin dimers were preassembled at the center of a 50 nm–by–50 nm–by–50 nm box using the oligomeric interface observed in the CENP-C cupin crystal structure (Protein Data Bank ID: 7X85). Eight CCAN unit models with different initial orientations were placed at radial distances of 160 to 180 Å around this central cupin assembly. Missing Mini-CENP-C IDRs were generated using MODELLER through ChimeraX (53). This initial configuration was designed to assess orientational relaxation and structural stability rather than de novo cupin oligomerization or spontaneous shell-like formation.


### MD simulations
  CG simulations were performed using the GROMACS (version 2022) (54) and the Martini 2.2 model (33, 34). The initial protein structures were generated using AF3 (32). In the CG models, the tertiary structures of protein regions other than the Mini-CENP-C IDRs were maintained using an elastic network. Proteins and DNA were solvated in a 70 nm–by–70 nm–by–70 nm box containing CG water and 0.15 M NaCl. Initial configurations were generated using the Martini Maker module of CHARMM-GUI (55). Each system was energy minimized and equilibrated using the Berendsen thermostat and barostat (56), followed by a 1-μs production run with a 20-fs time step. During production, the temperature and pressure were maintained at 303.15 K and 1 atm using the velocity-rescaling thermostat and the isotropic Parrinello-Rahman barostat (57), respectively.

  All-atom simulations were performed using GENESIS (58) and the CHARMM36m force field (59). Initial configurations were generated using the Solution Builder module of CHARMM-GUI (55). Energy minimization was performed for 1000 steps using the steepest-descent algorithm, followed by conjugate-gradient minimization. Solvent was equilibrated for 250 ps in the NVT ensemble at 303.15 K, followed by 1.6 ns of NPT equilibration at 1 atm using the Langevin thermostat and barostat (60). Production MD simulations were performed for 100 ns using a 2-fs time step and the Langevin thermostat and barostat. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald method (61, 62). A 12-Å cutoff was used for short-range electrostatic and van der Waals interactions. All bonds were constrained using the SHAKE/RATTLE algorithm (63, 64).

  The relative orientation of two CCAN units was quantified as the direction cosine between their DNA-axis vectors, with the direction from each CCAN unit toward the Mini-CENP-C cupin domain defined as positive. In simulations without DNA, corresponding protein-based vectors were defined using K232 of CENP-L and K261 of CENP-I, which were located at distal DNA binding positions in the DNA-containing models. A value of −1 corresponds to an antiparallel, face-to-face orientation, 0 to a perpendicular orientation, and +1 to a parallel orientation.

  The cupin-directed alignment of each CCAN unit in the shell-like assembly was quantified as the direction cosine between its DNA-axis vector and the vector from D208 on the CCAN side to S324 on the cupin side of the associated Mini-CENP-C chain. A value of +1 indicates that the DNA axis is aligned toward the cupin core, whereas 0 indicates a perpendicular orientation.

  Simulation trajectories were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (http://pymol.org/pymol).


### DNA construction
  mScarlet–HA–SNAP–chicken CENP-T DNA sequence was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and cloned into a pBluescript KS (+) backbone and flanked by ∼1-kb homology arms surrounding the CENP-T start codon, using the In-Fusion system (Clontech, catalog no. 638948) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

  To generate chicken GFP–Mini-CENP-C3E mutant, GFP–Mini-CENP-C plasmid (31) was inversely amplified using primers, which introduced the 3E mutation (K216E/R223E/R226E), and the PCR fragments were circularized using the In-Fusion system. To generate FLAG–CENP-Cwt, FLAG–Mini-CENP-Cwt, and FLAG–CENP-C3E, each CENP-C sequence was amplified by PCR, and the PCR fragments were cloned into the p3xFLAG-CMV-10 vector (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. E7658) using the In-Fusion system. To generate CENP-Cwt–FLAG, the CENP-C sequence was amplified by PCR, and the PCR fragment was cloned into the p3xFLAG-CMV-14 vector (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. E7908) using the In-Fusion system.

  To express chicken CENP-L/N complex in Escherichia coli, the coding sequences of full-length chicken CENP-L and CENP-N were amplified by PCR and inserted into the MCS1 and MCS2, respectively, of a modified pETDuet-1 vector (Novagen, catalog no. 71146) using In-Fusion system. The resulting plasmid encodes maltose-binding protein (MBP)–tagged CENP-L and His6-SUMO–tagged CENP-N.

  For bacterial expression of the 3E mutant of GST-tagged chicken Mini-CENP-C and CENP-C CBD, the corresponding DNA sequences were amplified from the GFP–Mini-CENP-C3E plasmid by PCR and introduced into the Eco RI– and Eag I–digested pGEX-6P-1 vector using the In-Fusion system.


### Preparation of double-stranded oligo DNA
  The 40-nucleotide (nt) oligonucleotides used for in vitro binding assays were purchased from Life Technologies Japan Ltd. as 100 μM solutions in TE buffer. Their sequences are listed in table S3. They were mixed at the final concentration of 40 μM in 50 mM NaCl and annealed by heating the solution at 98°C and gradually lowering the temperature to 4°C over a total duration of 140 min.


### Recombinant proteins
  The chicken CENP-L/N complex was expressed in BL21 star (DE3) (Invitrogen, catalog no. C601003) carrying the pRARE2 plasmid. The transformed cells were grown in LB medium containing ampicillin (100 μg/ml) and chloramphenicol (40 μg/ml) to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of ∼0.6. Expression of the complex was then induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 16 hours at 17°C. The harvested cells were resuspended in LN lysis buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 500 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 10 mM imidazole] supplemented with cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck, catalog no. 04693116001). The suspension was sonicated on ice, and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 40,000g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was loaded onto a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva, catalog no. 17524802) equilibrated with LN lysis buffer. After washing the column with LN lysis buffer containing 40 mM imidazole, bound proteins were eluted with a linear imidazole gradient from 40 to 500 mM. The fractions containing the CENP-L/N complex were incubated with HRV3C protease (in-house) at 4°C overnight to remove the tags. The sample was then loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva, catalog no. 17040703) and purified in LN heparin buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.4) and 0.5 mM TCEP] with a NaCl gradient from 300 mM to 1 M. The purified CENP-LN complex was concentrated, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until use.

  GST-tagged CENP-C fragments were expressed in Rosetta 2(DE3) (Novagen, catalog no. 71397) as described previously (31). Cells were sonicated in CC lysis buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 1 M NaCl (for Mini-CENP-C) or 500 mM NaCl (for CENP-C CBD), 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)] on ice. After clarification by centrifugation, the lysate was loaded to a Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow column (Cytiva, catalog no. 17513202). The column was extensively washed with CC lysis buffer, and the bound proteins were eluted from the column with CC lysis buffer containing 20 mM glutathione instead of DTT. The eluate was loaded onto a HiTrap SP HP column (Cytiva, catalog no. 17115201) and purified in CC SP buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.5 mM TCEP] with a linear NaCl gradient. To remove nucleic acid contamination, the peak fractions containing GST–Mini-CENP-C were pooled and passed through a HiTrap Q HP column (Cytiva, catalog no. 17115401). GST–CENP-C CBD after cation exchange chromatography was further purified by gel filtration using a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva, catalog no. 28989335) in CC GF buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 500 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP]. The purified CENP-C samples were concentrated, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until use.


### GST pull-down assay
  Binding reactions were prepared in 50 μl of pull-down buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 0.05% (v/v) NP-40 alternative (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, catalog no. sc-281108)] and contained 1 μM GST (for negative control) or GST–Mini-CENP-C (50 pmol), 2 μM CENP-LN (100 pmol), and 2 μM 40-bp DNA (100 pmol). Mixtures were incubated with 10 μl of Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow beads for 1 hour on ice with gentle agitation every 10 min. Beads were washed three times with 200 μl of pull-down buffer. After removing the supernatant completely, the proteins were eluted from the beads by incubating at 37°C in 20 μl of Laemmli sample buffer [62.5 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 2% SDS, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 5% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol, and 0.01% bromophenol blue] for 5 min. The supernatant was then boiled at 96°C for 5 min. For SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis, 25 pmol of each protein sample and 10 μl of the supernatant were loaded as the input and pull-down samples, respectively, on a 10–20% SuperSep Ace gel (Wako, catalog no. 191-15031). After visualizing the protein bands by CBB staining, gel images were acquired using the ChemiDoc Touch MP imaging system (Bio-Rad) and analyzed using the Image Lab software v6.1.0. For quantitative analysis, the band intensity of pulled down CENP-N was normalized by that of GST–Mini-CENP-C. Data are shown as mean ± SD from three technical replicates and generated using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1.


### Electrophoretic mobility shift assay
  Reactions for EMSA were prepared in 10 μl of GSA buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM DTT] and contained 1 μM 40-bp DNA. For evaluation of the DNA binding of GST–CENP-C CBD, 2 μM GST (for negative control) or 0.5, 1, 2, or 4 μM GST–CENP-C CBD was added to the sample. For analysis of the cooperative DNA binding of CENP-C and CENP-L/N, 2 μM GST–Mini-CENP-C and 1 μM CENP-L/N were included in the mixture. After incubation for 30 min on ice, 5 μl of each reaction was applied onto a 5–20% SuperSep Ace gel (Wako, catalog no. 197-15011) preequilibrated in 25 mM tris-borate buffer. Electrophoresis was performed at 150 V for 150 min in a cold room. The DNA bands were visualized by ethidium bromide staining. Gel images were acquired using ChemiDoc Touch MP or GelDoc Go imaging systems (Bio-Rad) and analyzed using the Image Lab software v6.1.0. For quantification of the DNA binding affinity of CENP-C CBD, the band intensity for the DNA–CENP-C CBD complex was divided by that for DNA in the first protein-free sample. Data are shown as mean ± SD from three technical replicates and plotted using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1.


### Chicken DT40 cells
  A chicken DT40 cell line CL18 was used as the WT cell (65). DT40 cells were cultured at 38.5°C in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich), 1% chicken serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 15140122) (DT40 culture medium).

  The chicken CENP-C conditional knockout (cKO–CENP-C) DT40 cell line was described before (37). The cKO–CENP-C cells expressing GFP–Mini-CENP-C3E, FLAG–CENP-Cwt, CENP-Cwt–FLAG, FLAG–Mini-CENP-Cwt, and FLAG–CENP-C3E were generated by random integration of the linear plasmid construct. The cKO–CENP-C cell line expressing GFP–Mini-CENP-C (29) or Mini-CENP-C3E were used. To conditionally knockout CENP-C, the cells were cultured in DT40 culture medium containing Tet (2 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. 87128) or Dox (Takara, catalog no. 631311) for 48 to 72 hours.

  DT40 cells in which endogenous CENP-T or CENP-N was replaced with mScarlet–HA–SNAP–CENP-T or CENP-N–FLAG, respectively, were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Guide RNA and homology arm sequences for them have been previously reported (31).


### Cell cycle synchronization
  To synchronize the cell cycle of DT40 cells, we used analog-sensitive CDK1 (CDK1-as) DT40 mutant cells (41). Cells were treated with 2 μM 1-NM-PP1 (Calbiochem, catalog no. 529581) for 10 hours to arrest them in the G2 phase and then released from the arrest by washing with fresh medium. The cells were collected every hour after release for immunostaining. Before each 20-min collection, 10 μM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) was added to label S-phase cells. EdU-positive cells were detected using the Click-iT EdU Imaging Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. C10329) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.


### Immunostaining
  DT40 cells were attached to coverslips using Cytospin 3 at 800 rpm for 5 min. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Electron Microscopy Sciences, catalog no. 15710) in 250 mM Hepes-NaOH (pH 7.4) for 10 min at room temperature (RT; 25° to 28°C), except for CENP-A staining. For CENP-A, cells were fixed with 4% PFA in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 min at RT, followed by methanol fixation at −30°C for 20 min. After blocking with blocking buffer [10% Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, catalog no. 03953-95) and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS] for 30 min, cells were incubated with primary antibodies for 2 hours at RT: anti–ggCENP-T (11) (1:1000), anti–ggCENP-A (11) (1:500), anti-GFP (MBL, catalog no. 598; 1:1000), anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. F3165; 1:1000), anti-HA (Roche, catalog no. 12158167001; 3F10, 1:1000), anti–α-tubulin (DM1A, Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. T6199; 1:1000), and GFP-Booster Alexa Fluor 488 (ChromoTek, catalog no. gb2AF488; 1:200). After three washes, cells were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies together with DAPI (100 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. D9542): anti-rabbit IgG–Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A-11034, 1:250), anti-rabbit IgG–Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A-11036, 1:250), anti-mouse IgG–Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A-11029, 1:250), anti-mouse IgG–Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A-11031, 1:250), and anti-rat IgG–Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A-11077, 1:200). Following washing, cells were directly subjected to ExM.


### Cell counting
  To count DT40 cell numbers, the culture was mixed with the same volume of 0.4% (w/v) Trypan Blue Solution (FUJIFILM, catalog no. 594-31481) and the cell numbers were counted by Countess II (Thermo Fisher Scientific).


### Quantification of mitotic index
  The cKO–CENP-C cells expressing GFP–Mini-CENP-Cwt or GFP–Mini-CENP-C3E were cultured in DT40 culture medium in the presence or absence of Tet (2 μg/ml) for 72 hours at 38.5°C and then immunostained with an anti–α-tubulin antibody as described above. The images were acquired every 0.2-μm intervals of z-slice using a Fusion BT sCMOS camera (HAMAMATSU Photonics) mounted on a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope with an oil-immersion objective lens [Nikon; Plan Apo lambda 100x/1.45 numerical aperture (NA)] with a spinning disk confocal scanner unit (CSU-W1; Yokogawa) controlled with NIS-elements (Nikon; version 5.42.01). The images in the figures are the maximum intensity projection of the Z-stack generated with Fiji (66). The mitotic index (mitotic cell numbers/total cell numbers) was manually counted. Statistical analysis of the measurements was performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.1 or later).


### Expansion microscopy
  We used the ExM method as reported by Norman et al. (38), unless otherwise noted, with slight modifications. Briefly, immunostained cells were postfixed with 2% glutaraldehyde (GA; EM grade, Electron Microscopy Sciences, catalog no. 16220) in PBS overnight at RT. After repeating GA fixation for 15 min, cells were embedded in hydrogel containing N,N-dimethylacrylamide (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. 274135) and sodium acrylate (SA; AK Scientific, catalog no. R624-25g), digested with 30 U of proteinase K (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. P4850) in digestion buffer (0.5 M SDS and 0.5% Triton X-100 in tris-acetate-EDTA) together with DAPI (100 ng/ml) at 37°C for 3 hours and expanded in Milli-Q water. This method uses potassium persulfate (KPS) as the oxidizing agent, whereas the expansion factor can be reduced to ∼6.3 times by using ammonium persulfate (APS) instead of KPS (for Fig. 5C), resulting in reduced hydrogel drift during imaging. The variation in expansion factors for both methods, measured using nuclear size, was within ±1.1. The expanded gel was trimmed to an appropriate size and mounted on a poly-l-lysine–coated 35-mm glass-bottom dish (Matsunami, catalog no. D11141H). Images were acquired using an AxioObserver.Z1 system (Carl Zeiss) equipped with a water-immersion 63× C-Apochromat objective lens (NA = 1.2) and a Prime BSI sCMOS camera (Teledyne) or DeltaVision Elite system (GE Healthcare) equipped with a silicone-immersion 60× UPlanSApo objective lens (Olympus, NA = 1.3) and a pco.edge 4.2 sCMOS camera (PCO). In the AxioObserver.Z1 system, fluorescence excitation was performed with a light-emitting diode (LED) light source (X-cite Xylis2), and emission was separated using appropriate dichroic mirrors and filters: FT 395, BP 445/50 for blue; FT 495, BP 525/50 for green; and FT 570, BP 605/70 for red fluorescence. The microscope was controlled with the ZEN blue 3.6 software (version 3.6.5). Raw data were deconvolved using ZEN, and when sample drift was prominent, drift correction was applied before deconvolution using the 3D-Aligner (67). In the DeltaVision Elite system, fluorescence excitation was performed with an LED light source (Seven-color Solid State Illuminator), and emission was separated using appropriate multiband dichroic mirrors (DAPI/FITC/TRITC/Cy5) and filters: BP 435/48 for blue, BP 525/48 for green, and BP 597/45 for red fluorescence. The microscope was controlled with the SoftWoRx software (version 7.0.0). Raw data were deconvolved using SoftWoRx.

  To confirm the shell-like structure of CENP-T, we used U-ExM (39) in combination with super-resolution microscopy. Immunostained cells were postfixed with fixation buffer [1.4% PFA and 2% acrylamide (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. A4058)] overnight at RT. Cells were then embedded in monomer solution [10% acrylamide, 0.1% methylenebisacrylamide (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. M1533), and 19% SA in PBS], denatured in denaturation buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 9.0), 200 mM NaCl, and 200 mM SDS] together with DAPI (100 ng/ml) at 95°C for 3 hours, and subsequently expanded in Milli-Q water. The expanded gel was mounted on a poly-l-lysine–coated 35-mm glass-bottom dish. Imaging was performed using either an LSM880 with Airyscan (Carl Zeiss) equipped with a 63× C-Apochromat objective lens or a DeltaVision Elite system (GE Healthcare) equipped with a silicone-immersion 60× UPlanSApo objective lens (NA = 1.3; Olympus). Both microscopes were operated with their default control software (ZEN black 2.3 SP1 FP2, version 14.0.0.0; SoftWoRx, version 7.0.0). Airyscan processing was performed using default settings. Enhanced ratio deconvolution was applied in SoftWoRx using default settings with a custom optical transfer function of the 60× UPlanSApo objective lens.

  Image contrast was adjusted in Fiji/ImageJ without altering gamma values for optimal presentation.


### Super-resolution microscopy
  For super-resolution observations, we used LSM990 with AiryScan2 (Carl Zeiis) and STELLARIS STED (Leica Microsystems) systems. For LSM990 with AiryScan2 observation, DT40 cells were immunostained with an anti–CENP-T antibody followed by anti-rabbit-IgG–Alexa Fluor 488 as described above. This system was equipped with oil-immersion 63× PlanApochromat objective lens and controlled using the ZEN blue software (version 3.12). The imaging conditions were as follows: main beam splitter, 405 and 488/561/639; detection wavelengths, 420 to 480 nm and 495 to 550 nm; pinhole, 1.25 A.U.; scan zoom, 10; pixel time, 6.68 μs; and pixel size, 0.042 μm × 0.042 μm × 0.15 μm. Images were deconvolved using the joint deconvolution function in ZEN Blue with default settings except for 40 iterations.

  For STELLARIS STED observation, DT40 cells were immunostained with an anti–CENP-T antibody followed by anti-rabbit-IgG–Alexa Fluor 594 as described above. This system was equipped with oil-immersion HC PL APO CS2 100x/1.40 objective lens and controlled using the LAS X software (version 4.8.2.29567). The sample was observed by TauSTED mode with the following setup: 590-nm white light laser for excitation; 775-nm laser for STED; 601 to 758 nm for spectral position; 0.5- to 8-ns time gate for photon collection. Images were deconvolved by the Richardson-Lucy algorithm using the LAS X software with default settings.


### Quantification of the spatial relationship between CCAN components
  The spatial relationship between the CCAN components was quantified using the Fiji/ImageJ software. Images around each kinetochore were duplicated. Then, they were Gaussian filtered to reduce noise and thresholded to segment the kinetochore region. After determining the kinetochore centroid, the original image was cropped so that the centroid was positioned at the image center. The resulting image was rotated 360° around the z axis using the three-dimensional (3D) projection function and then averaged to generate a rotationally averaged image.

  Line profiles of the averaged images were measured and fitted with a single Gaussian to determine the center and full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the shell. Because shell sizes varied among kinetochores, each profile was normalized by dividing spatial axis by its FWHM. Profile height (intensity) was also normalized using the minimum and maximum intensity values. The over 10 normalized profiles were then averaged and plotted as mean ± 95% confidence intervals.


### Quantification of shell size
  The size of the CENP-T shell-like structure was quantified using Fiji/ImageJ. The major axis of each shell was manually selected and measured. Statistical analysis of the measurements was performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.1 or later).


### Protein purification and Western blotting
  GST-fused CENP-T fragments were expressed in BL21 E. coli cells. Cells harboring the plasmids were cultured in Luria broth medium at 37°C until an OD600 of ∼0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM IPTG, and the cultures were incubated for an additional 3 hours at 25°C.

  Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, catalog no. 4693116001). After sonication, the extract was clarified by centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was incubated with Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads for 1 hour at 4°C. After three washes with PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100, the purified GST–CENP-T fragments were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and CBB staining using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a standard. Five hundred or 50 ng of GST–CENP-T fragments was used for CBB staining and Western blotting, respectively. Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane, probed with anti–ggCENP-T (1:2000) and anti-GST (Nacalai Tesque, catalog no. 04435-84, 1:5000) antibodies and detected by chemiluminescence (ImmunoStar Zeta, Fujifilm Wako, catalog no. 297-72403).
