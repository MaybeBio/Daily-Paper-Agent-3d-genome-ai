# Recent advances in coarse-grained simulations of nucleic acid condensation


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates, formed through phase separation of multivalent biomolecules, play essential roles in cellular processes such as stress response, RNA processing, transcriptional control, and genome organization. Nucleic acids are increasingly recognized as important drivers and regulators of condensate formation and dynamics. This review examines recent advances in coarse-grained molecular simulations of dynamic nucleic acids and their phase transitions. We survey established computational frameworks validated against experimental data and discuss emerging applications that address increasingly complex systems. Existing studies reveal that base-mediated interactions emerge as dominant drivers in homotypic nucleic acid condensation and that simulation resolution fundamentally determines which physical phenomena are accessible and observable. We underscore that different modeling approaches at various resolutions provide complementary insights into nucleic acid condensation mechanisms and the driving forces. We emphasize the opportunities and challenges in extending these frameworks to capture the complexity of nucleic acid-mediated phase separation in biological contexts, while identifying important gaps in current modeling capabilities. Overall, molecular simulations at multiple scales have proven to be powerful tools for elucidating the mechanistic principles underlying nucleic acid condensates and their cellular functions.


## Introduction
  Biomolecular condensates, also known as membraneless organelles, are an important mechanism of subcellular organization and have a wide range of physiological functions [1–4]. Their broad relevance to both fundamental biology and human disease has made them a subject of intense interdisciplinary research interest in recent years [5–7]. They are formed through spontaneous phase transitions of dynamic multivalent molecules such as proteins, DNAs, and RNAs, driven by a wide range of weak intermolecular interactions, including electrostatics, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, aromatic stacking, and π–cation interactions [2,8–11]. Biomolecular condensates display rich material properties ranging from liquid-like to gel and solid-like [12,13], which have important consequences in function [14–16] and have also been implicated in the pathology of neurodegenerative and other human diseases [17,18].

  While protein-driven condensation has received much attention, nucleic acids are key components of many biomolecular condensates, including ribonucleoprotein granules, stress granules, and chromatin-associated compartments. Phase separation provides a fundamental mechanism to organize and regulate nucleic acids across genomic organization [19], support transcription regulation [20] and stress response [21,22]. This mechanism has also been implicated in some neurodegeneration, where disease manifests only beyond a critical number of trinucleotide repeat expansions within specific genes [23–25]. Beyond biological systems, nucleic acid phase separation has emerged as a promising avenue for synthetic biology and drug delivery applications [26,27].

  Nucleic acids exhibit phase behaviors that are physically distinct from proteins [13]. First, RNAs typically undergo lower critical solution temperature (LCST) phase transitions (Figure 1A), entropically driven by dehydration and counterion release [28]. Reentrant transitions at high temperature have also been reported, revealing both upper critical solution temperature (UCST)- and LCST-type behaviors [28,29]. Furthermore, these transitions can be reversible or irreversible, depending on RNA sequence and concentrations, and Mg2+ level, with reversibility likely determined by propensity to percolate and form intra-condensate networks (Figure 1A) [28]. Second, homotypic nucleic acid phase separation generally requires crowding agents, divalent cations, or polycationic species, underscoring the central role of ion-mediated interactions in charge neutralization and inter-chain bridging (Figure 1B) [13,30]. Third, RNA trinucleotide repeats phase separate more readily than their DNA counterparts, a disparity hypothesized to stem from the additional 2′-OH on the ribose ring, which facilitates inner-sphere coordination with Mg2+ and promotes water release [30,31]. In heterotypic systems, nucleic acids introduce additional regulatory complexity: RNA can act as both a promoter and inhibitor of condensation through reentrant phase transitions, where excess RNA overcharges condensate droplets and induces dissolution [13,29]. Together, these observations emphasize that nucleic acid condensation is governed by a delicate balance of electrostatic interactions, base-mediated contacts (stacking and pairing), backbone chemistry, and ion coordination (Figure 1A–C), modulated by sequence, composition, and solution conditions.

  Elucidating these molecular driving forces experimentally remains challenging. Techniques such as turbidity assays, fluorescence microscopy, and SAXS can characterize macroscopic phase boundaries and global structural parameters, but they cannot directly resolve the interplay of competing interactions at the molecular level. For example, how Mg2+ coordination modes shift with temperature, how base stacking competes with electrostatic bridging within condensates, or how individual chain conformations reorganize upon entering the dense phase. Molecular dynamics simulations offer a uniquely powerful complement by providing direct access to these microscopic details [32] and have provided valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms and driving forces [33–35]. However, phase separation involves length and timescales that are largely inaccessible to all-atom simulations. Coarse-grained (CG) models have therefore become essential to bridge this gap in a computationally efficient manner. Models spanning different resolutions, ranging from minimal models to one-bead-per-nucleotide and multi-bead-per-nucleotide representations, have been developed in conjunction with experiments and theory to elucidate the mechanisms and regulation of biomolecular condensates [13,33,34,36].

  In this review, we examine recent developments in molecular modeling of flexible RNAs and DNAs and their phase transitions, with particular emphasis on efficient CG models. We begin by discussing insights from atomistic simulations that reveal the key driving forces and establish physical constraints for CG model development. We then survey CG models organized by resolution, from minimal and patchy-particle representations to one-bead, two-bead, and intermediate multi-bead models, discussing their parameterization strategies, validated capabilities, and application to both homotypic nucleic acid condensation and heterotypic protein–nucleic acid systems. We highlight emerging applications to structurally complex systems, including G-quadruplexes and nucleic acid nanostars, and conclude by identifying key gaps and future directions. Models developed primarily for nucleic acid structure prediction [37], chromatin organization [36,38,39], and earlier CG frameworks for nucleic acid simulations [36,39–42] have been reviewed elsewhere and are beyond the scope of this work.


## Atomistic insights into nucleic acid phase transitions
  Atomistic simulations using empirical physics-based force fields arguably provide the most detailed description of the molecular interactions involved in biomolecular and particularly nucleic acid condensation. Although direct simulation of macroscopic phase separation remains mostly beyond computational reach at fully atomic resolution, targeted studies of small oligonucleotide systems have proven valuable for dissecting the relative contributions of specific driving forces, including ion coordination, base-mediated contacts, hydration, and backbone chemistry, under conditions relevant to condensation. These simulations highlight interaction features that existing CG models may inadequately capture, providing guidance for CG model refinement.

  Distinct force fields have been developed for RNA (e.g. AMBER χOL3 [43], CHARMM36 [44]) and DNA (e.g. AMBER BSC1 [45], AMBER OL24 [46], and CHARMM36 [44]); both face similar challenges in accurately capturing the balance of electrostatic, stacking, and solvent-mediated interactions relevant to condensation. For nucleic acid phase separation specifically, the force field must simultaneously describe electrostatic interactions between negatively charged phosphate groups and cations, base-mediated interactions, including base pairing and stacking, and the thermodynamics of hydration and ion coordination (Figure 1A–C). Achieving this balance has proven challenging, as shown in ongoing reparameterization efforts [35] and systematic benchmarking studies [47–51]. For comprehensive evaluation of nucleic acid force fields and associated water models, we refer readers to recent reviews [35,52] and benchmarking studies against experimental data [47–50]. Despite these challenges, carefully selected force field combinations have yielded important mechanistic insights into nucleic acid condensation, as highlighted below.

  The polyphosphate (poly(P)) system offers a minimal model to isolate the role of backbone-ion interactions in condensation, independent of base-mediated contributions. Banerjee and coworkers demonstrated experimentally that poly(P) readily undergoes LCST-type phase separation in the presence of Mg2+ ions [28], where atomistic simulations using microMg [53] and TIP3P water model [54] were also performed to reveal the underlying mechanism: elevated temperature promotes entropically favorable desolvation of both phosphate groups and Mg2+ ions, which in turn enhances inter-chain cross-linking and drives chain compaction and network formation characteristic of phase separation. This establishes a clear physical picture in which thermally activated dehydration of ion-phosphate contacts provides the entropic driving force for LCST behavior. Interestingly, poly(P) can undergo phase separation with monovalent ions experimentally [55]. That is, divalent cross-linking is not strictly required, and charge neutralization alone is sufficient. However, neither DNA nor RNA undergoes phase separation with monovalent ions alone; they require additional divalent ions [28,56,57] or polycation species [58,59] to condense. This distinction indicates that for nucleic acids containing bases, backbone charge neutralization is necessary but not sufficient—additional interactions mediated by the base and/or the sugar are required.

  Building on this foundation, Ramachandran and Potoyan applied a carefully selected combination of AMBER RNA force field parameters (parmBSC0χOL3) [46,60,61], microMg [53], and the OPC water model [62] to dissect the molecular determinants of phase separation in small RNA and DNA oligonucleotides. Their simulations revealed that three factors collectively tune condensate stability: Mg2+ coordination, nucleobase chemistry, and 2′-hydroxyl group (2′-OH) (Figure 1D–G) [30]. Beyond heat-driven Mg2+ binding and water-released mechanism identified in polyphosphate, the study uncovered a thermal stability hierarchy among RNA homopolymers: poly(G) > poly(A) > poly(C) > poly(U) that predicts their relative propensity to phase separate through coupled ion and hydration effects (Figure 1D). This hierarchy reflects differences in base-mediated interactions: guanine and adenine engage in more stacking and hydrogen bonding networks that provide additional cohesive forces beyond electrostatic bridging. The 2′-OH group on the RNA ribose ring further enhances condensation propensity by enabling hydrogen bonding interactions with Mg2+ and intramolecular base contacts (Figure 1F,G). This leads to a higher propensity for RNA phase separation compared with DNA, consistent with experimental observation [31].

  Together, these atomistic studies reveal that nucleic acid condensation arises from a delicate interplay of ion coordination, base-mediated contacts, backbone hydration, and sugar chemistry. The relative importance of these contributions varies with sequence, temperature, and ionic conditions, giving rise to the rich phase behaviors observed experimentally. These findings provide a useful physical context for assessing and developing CG models, which we survey in the following section.


## CG models for nucleic acid phase separation
  While atomistic simulations reveal detailed dynamics, they cannot access the length and time scales required to directly simulate phase separation. CG models bridge this gap by reducing the degrees of freedom while retaining essential physical features. Many CG nucleic acid models have been developed, spanning resolutions from minimal representations to intermediate multi-bead frameworks (Table 1). These models employ diverse parameterization strategies, drawing on atomistic simulations, experimental structural data on dynamic nucleic acids, thermodynamic measurements, and statistical potentials, and have been applied to both homotypic nucleic acid condensation and heterotypic protein–nucleic acid systems. However, there is a lack of a systematic benchmark to assess these models against different experimental observables for single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), phase separation properties, and computing resources.

  In this section, we will first focus on CG models designed for homotypic nucleic acid phase separations, where we highlight two models, namely SIS and iConRNA, that together build a coherent picture of how base-mediated interactions emerge as the dominant driving force of condensation.


### Single-site and triple-site interaction models (SIS/TIS)
  SIS is a model pioneered by Thirumalai’s group [68], where each nucleotide is represented as a single interaction center. The model has been employed to investigate how systems like riboswitches respond to mechanical force [80,81]. SIS incorporates bonded interactions (bond and angle terms), excluded volume, and base pairing interactions, but no explicit electrostatic forces. Instead, it is calibrated for conditions where high concentrations of monovalent and divalent ions effectively screen phosphate charges [82,83], thereby enabling phase separation. The only adjustable parameter is the energy scale for base pairing interaction, and it was calibrated to reproduce the crystal structures of short (CAG)2 duplex [68]. This minimalist parameterization—a single energy scale targeting a single structural observable—makes the model’s subsequent successes in capturing emergent phase behavior particularly noteworthy.

  Multichain simulations using the calibrated SIS successfully recapitulated the length and concentration dependence of CAG repeat phase separation observed experimentally [25]. The results suggest that, within the condensed phase, intramolecular base pairings are substantially reduced, and dynamic intermolecular base pairings become dominant and allow the formation of a dynamic, soft network composed of stretched and unfolded ssRNA. This condensate-induced structural reorganization, where the dense phase environment reshapes the balance between intra- and intermolecular contacts, has been observed in other contexts experimentally [84]. The model further revealed heterogeneous condensate architecture and identified reptation-like chain dynamics, associating RNA secondary structure reorganization with condensate dynamics. These results established a critical insight: base pairing, even when limited to canonical Watson–Crick contacts, is sufficient to drive phase separation and determine condensate internal organization when electrostatic screening is high.

  However, because the SIS model captured only base pairing without electrostatics and ion coordination, several important questions remain unaddressed. What is the relative contribution of electrostatic binding versus base-mediated contacts? Is base pairing alone sufficient, or do other base interactions—particularly stacking—play an equally important role? How does Mg2+ contribute beyond simple charge screening? These questions require a model that explicitly incorporates both electrostatic and base-mediated interactions at sufficient resolution to distinguish between them.

  Extensions of the SIS model have incorporated electrostatic interactions through the Debye–Hückel (DH) approximation to describe implicit monovalent ions [85,86] with explicit divalent ions [87]. Further developments include the sequence-dependent three-interaction site (TIS) model [88] and its derivative TIS-CAT [89], which employs reference interaction site model (RISM)-based potential mean forces of divalent cations-phosphate interactions featuring distinct inner-sphere and outer-sphere coordination minima. These models incorporate explicit representation of monovalent salts and/or divalent cations and have been employed to study diverse RNA and DNA folding [90]. While TIS-CAT has not yet been applied to phase separation directly, its explicit treatment of Mg2+ coordination makes it a promising framework for future studies of ion-dependent condensation.


### Intermediate resolution model for condensates of RNAs (iConRNA)
  To explicitly capture the balance of diverse interactions of RNA backbone, side chain base, and ions, Li and Chen developed the iConRNA model with intermediate representation of each nucleotide and explicit Mg2+ ions [77]. Each nucleotide is represented by 6–7 beads: one bead for the phosphate, two beads for the sugar ring, and three to four beads for the pyrimidine or purine base, respectively (Figure 2A). This resolution enables the model to incorporate explicit electrostatic, base stacking, and base pairing interactions, such that one could carefully tune the balance of their relative contributions to structure dynamics and condensation. Mg2+ ions are represented as spheres corresponding to the fully hydrated state and interact nonspecifically with the base and sugar groups and electrostatically with the phosphate beads. Since Mg2+ binding with RNA depends strongly on monovalent salt concentration, sequence, structure, and temperature [91], the model employs an empirical scaling factor λ for the phosphate-Mg2+ electrostatic interaction strength, calibrated using excess magnesium per phosphate (Δnmg) [91–94] determined through atomistic simulations, theory, machine learning approaches, or experiments for a given RNA sequence.

  iConRNA has been carefully balanced to reproduce local and long-range structures of flexible ssRNAs, including backbone orientation correlation function (OCF), radius of gyration (Rg), and end-to-end distance (Re) under different salt and Mg2+ concentrations (Figure 2B), as well as folding and melting temperature of a set of small RNAs. This multi-target parameterization ensures the balance between interactions is reasonable and transferable for phase separation studies. The model was shown to capture the experimentally observed distinction between rA30 and rU30, where rA30 can readily undergo phase separation with 50 mM MgCl2 at elevated temperature, while poly(rU) remains homogenous under similar conditions [95]. Crucially, the analysis revealed that, although rU30 exhibits stronger Mg2+ bridging interactions than rA30 (Figure 2C), this electrostatic-mediated stabilization alone is not sufficient to drive condensation. The critical difference lies in the base-mediated interactions: interchain Van der Waals interactions and intermolecular base stacking present in rA30 provided the additional driving forces necessary for phase separation (Figure 2C). Examination of rA30 structure within the dense phase revealed reduced structural complexity compared with dilute conditions, reflecting weakening of intramolecular interactions and enhanced intermolecular interactions, consistent with the SIS observations for CAG repeats [68].

  A notable strength of iConRNA is the ability to accurately recapitulate the nontrivial dependence of CAG repeat phase separation on sequence length, ionic strength, temperature, Mg2+, and RNA concentrations [25,28]. This is a major achievement that has not been demonstrated by other CG or atomistic models to date. The model similarly captured the phase diagrams of purine- to uracil-substituted trinucleotide repeats: CAG and CUG repeats undergo phase separation while the CUU repeat does not under comparable conditions. Across all these systems, base–base interactions remain the dominant driving force even at elevated temperatures, outweighing increases in phosphate-Mg2+ interaction strength. This dominance is also observed through the annealing protocol, where the condensate was cooled to 333K from 368K; base-base interactions retain substantial strength, suggesting they serve as the driving force of condensate gelation and arrest (Figure 2D).

  Nonetheless, important questions remain. Can the iConRNA framework be extended to DNA and RNA–DNA hybrid systems? Does the current resolution adequately distinguish their differential phase separation propensities? The iConRNA model currently represents Mg2+ only in the fully hydrated state without distinguishing inner-sphere from outer-sphere coordination modalities identified in atomistic simulations (Figure 1B) and only includes canonical base pairing and neighboring base-stacking. Incorporation of these additional interactions will likely be necessary for further improving the model’s phase separation predictive capabilities. Another important direction is the integration of iConRNA with an appropriate protein model, such as the hybrid resolution (HyRes) model [96–99], towards modeling heterotypic phase transitions.


## Emerging application of CG models to phase separation of complex nucleic acids
  The CG models discussed above have primarily been applied to simple nucleic acid sequences such as homopolymers and trinucleotide repeats, which behave largely as flexible single-stranded polymers with only transient local structures. However, nucleic acids can adopt complex geometries that influence phase separation in biologically and technologically important ways [100,101]. While applications of CG modeling to phase transitions of RNAs with complex structures remain limited, emerging studies have begun to address this gap. We highlight two examples, namely, G-quadruplexes and engineered nucleic acid nanostars. However, CG models have yet to be applied to functional RNAs and long noncoding RNAs, representing an exciting frontier for future research.


### G-quadruplex
  Nucleic acids rich in guanine can fold into four-stranded G-quadruplex (G4) structures stabilized by Hoogsten hydrogen base-pairing and coordinated by central ions [102]. The phase transition of G4C2 RNA repeats is directly implicated in neurodegenerative pathologies, including familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia, where disease manifests only beyond a critical repeat length [25]. Elucidating how G4 formations coupled to condensation is therefore of significant biomedical interest.

  Souza’s group adapted the Martini 3 force field [103] to address this challenge. To capture the hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking driving G4 architecture, they expanded the standard nucleobase resolution from 3–4 beads to 7 beads in guanine and cytosine [79] with explicit ions (including Mg2+) from Martini 3 [103]. The model can capture the spontaneous quadruplex folding without relying on an elastic network. In multi-chain simulations, increasing G4C2 repeat length from 3 to 8 caused a decrease in average cluster count alongside increased chain compactness, mirroring experimental observations that viable condensate formation in vivo required thresholds beyond five G4C2 repeats [25]. The simulations revealed significant enrichment of Mg2+ in the dense phase relative to Na+ and Cl−, consistent with multi-valent Mg2+-phosphate interactions. The present study demonstrated the potential of CG modeling for connecting non-canonical RNA structure formation to phase behavior, even though further validation, including temperature and salt dependence, and condensate material properties remain to be explored.


### Nucleic acid nanostars
  Synthetic DNA and RNA nanostars, multi-armed structures interacting via sequence-dependent kissing loops, have emerged as programmable scaffolds for engineering artificial condensates [104–106]. These structures can undergo phase separation in vitro without magnesium and in vivo. They can be designed with specific valencies, arm lengths, and binding motifs to create orthogonal non-mixing condensates with tunable material properties. Their programmability makes them attractive systems for both synthetic biology and as model systems to test physical theories of valency-dependent phase transitions.

  Many models at different resolutions have been used to study DNA nanostar dynamics [107–109] and phase separation [110–113]. Here, we focus on a recent study that compared the flexibility of DNA and RNA nanostars using two-bead models oxRNA [114,115] and oxDNA [116–120], which represent each nucleotide as a rigid body with anisotropic interaction sites. It investigated how valency, salt, and temperature differentially control nanostar geometry versus flexibility [121]. These models consist of one backbone bead and three interaction sites on the base bead for base repulsion, base stacking, and hydrogen bond/cross-stacking. They have been extensively parameterized to reproduce thermodynamics and mechanistic properties of RNA [114,115] and DNA [116–120]. The study revealed that increasing the number of arms does not alter individual arm flexibility and that DNA nanostars exhibited larger deviations in inter-arm angles compared with their RNA counterparts. This observation supported the ability of these models to capture backbone-dependent differences in RNA and DNA dynamics. Extending these frameworks to multichain phase separation simulations represents an exciting opportunity to connect programmable nanostar design with condensate formation and properties.


## CG models for protein–nucleic acid heterotypic phase transitions
  Beyond homotypic nucleic acid systems, many biomolecular condensates involve protein–nucleic acid co-condensation, where electrostatic complementarity between positively charged protein regions and negatively charged nucleic acid backbone is a primary driving force. These systems display rich phase behaviors such as reentrant phase transition [122,123], charge inversion [124], and multiphasic architectures [123]. Many CG nucleic acid models have been developed to complement existing protein models in studying these phenomena. These models range from minimalist models representing entire proteins and nucleic acids as single particles or patchy particles to models with multiple beads per nucleotide (Table 1). They have been calibrated using different strategies to capture various physical aspects of the protein/nucleic acid phase behaviors. Below we discuss representative models within increasing resolution, with particular attention to the nucleic acid component.


### Minimal models and patchy particles
  At the coarsest level, minimal continuum models, including patchy-particle models and lattice-based approaches (notably “stickers-and-spacers” representations), have been used to study intrinsically disordered protein phase behaviors, revealing concentration-dependent features of multicomponent protein systems [9,125–130]. Similar approaches have also been used to study RNA–protein [65] and DNA–histone interactions [63,131] (Table 1). These studies revealed that complementary electrostatic interactions and relative protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid binding strengths can tune condensate formation and compartmentalization. However, these models lack residue-level details and cannot address sequence-specific effects.


### Nucleotide-based one-bead models
  One-bead per nucleotide models offer a practical compromise, representing each nucleotide with a single interaction site while retaining sequence identity (Figure 3A). Prominent examples include HPS [66], Mpipi/Mpipi-Recharge [69,70], and COCOMO [71]. These one-bead models emphasize the electrostatic contributions: each bead carries some effective negative charge (−1 in HPS and COCOMO, −0.75 in Mpipi), with electrostatic interactions typically described using the DH potential. The latest Mpipi-Recharge model introduced asymmetric charge–charge interactions using a pair-specific Yukawa potential derived from reference potentials of mean force (PMFs) [70], allowing one to differentiate between the electrostatic behaviors of similarly charged residues (e.g. arginine versus lysine) in condensation and providing a better description of the highly charged interfaces of multi-domain proteins and ssRNAs. Cation–π interactions between basic protein residues and aromatic nucleic acid bases are often effectively captured through Lennard-Jones-style or Wang–Frenkel nonbonded interactions, such as in COCOMO and Mpipi, respectively.

  Different parameterization strategies have been designed for optimization of various one-bead models. For example, HPS utilizes atomic-scale partial charges from the OPLS all-atom RNA force field to calculate the hydropathy value that determines interaction strengths, resulting in repulsive nucleotide–nucleotide homotypic interactions, while attraction toward positively charged amino acids is significant [66]. Mpipi model derived nonbonded parameters from atomic PMFs for all nucleobase-nucleobase and nucleobase-amino acid pairs [69,70]. COCOMO incorporated a top-down approach, parameterizing directly against experimental data, including Rg, OCF, Re, and persistence length (Lp) of ssRNA homopolymers [132,133]. COCOMO achieved good agreement across many properties, except for the OCF of homopolymer adenine, which likely requires explicit base stacking absent from the one-bead model.

  Both HPS and Mpipi-Recharge have been successfully applied to study the re-entrant behavior with RRP sequences and RNA [122,124] (Figure 3B). Simulations using HPS showed that with patches of acidic and basic sequences present in the LAF-1 RGG sequence, the RNA partition is preferentially at the surface, where it mainly interacts with the positive patches [66] (Figure 3C). In addition, increasing RNA concentration first reduces Csat of condensate and then increases Csat beyond a certain point, suggesting both promoter and disruptor roles of RNA in condensate formation. Mpipi-Recharge reproduced similar trends across several arginine-rich polypeptide (RRP) sequences with poly(rU) (Figure 3D), demonstrating that asymmetric treatment of electrostatic interactions improves the recapitulation of reentrant phase behavior with gradual increase of poly(rU) concentration [70]. COCOMO similarly produced condensate clusters in several RNA–protein systems [71]. Nonetheless, the one-bead resolution imposes fundamental limitations; it severely restricts the ability to capture transient local structures (e.g. base stacking), the balance of backbone and side chain interactions in protein–nucleic phase transitions, or conformational rearrangement of nucleic acid within condensates.


### Backbone-base two-bead models
  These models represent each nucleotide with one backbone bead carrying −1 charge and one neutral base bead (Figure 3A), allowing explicit separation of backbone electrostatics from base interactions. A representative two-bead model is HPS-Urry DNA [73], which was developed to model duplex DNA interacting with proteins in chromatin contexts [73,134]. It includes stacking of consecutive bases and canonical base pairing, with interaction parameters derived from CHARMM27 [74] partial charges and PMF calculations [135], adjusted to reproduce the melting profile of a 14-bp DNA duplex. The model reproduced dsDNA structural properties, including base rise, helical width, base pairs per turn, and major and minor grooves. Protein–DNA interactions are validated through the dissociation constant (Kd) of Histone H1 C-terminus with a 20 bp dsDNA. HPS-Urry protein and DNA models together can thus provide a framework for modeling phase transitions in chromatin studies.

  CALVADOS-RNA [75] is another representative two-bead model, but designed for flexible RNA polymers to be used alongside the CALVADOS single-bead protein model [136]. The interactions are sequence-independent, with “stickiness” parameters (λ) adjusted to reproduce poly-rU30 and poly-(rA)30 Rg under different salt concentrations. Interestingly, the model also captured the compactness of poly-(dT)30 and poly-(dA)30 from SAXS at low salt concentration (225 mM), though it produces overly compact structures at higher salt conditions (525 mM) [137]. This suggests that CALVADOS-RNA is potentially a general nucleic acid model appropriate for both ssRNAs and ssDNAs under physiological salt conditions (e.g. ∼150 mM). The model can capture the re-entrant behavior observed experimentally, in which the lowest concentration of protein for phase separation is near the neutralization point and returns to one phase when RNA concentration is high [123]. In the FUSRGG3, PLP, and polyU system, the model can also recapitulate the preferential partition of RNA with FUSRGG3 as shown in experiment [123].


### Models with three or more beads per nucleotide
  These models represent each nucleotide with one phosphate bead, one sugar bead, and one to multiple base beads [77,79,138,139] (Figure 3A). While many models have been applied to study nucleic acid folding with explicit divalent ions [90,138,139] and nucleic acid phase separation [68,77,79], most have not been used for heterotypic protein–nucleic acid phase separation. We note that the Martini force field has compatible protein and nucleic acid parameters, providing a ready-made framework for multicomponent condensation studies and a significant opportunity for the field [103,140]. A challenge, though, is to capture transient protein and nucleic structures that likely play important roles in modulating phase transitions and condensate properties.


## Concluding discussion and outlook
  In this review, we have surveyed molecular models developed for modeling nucleic acid phase separation, spanning from all-atom simulations to CG representations at various resolutions. Atomistic simulations have provided critical mechanistic insights into the driving forces of condensation—phosphate-ion interactions, nucleobase-mediated interactions, and solvent reorganization—but remain limited to small systems. CG models aim to bridge this gap and have demonstrated expanding capabilities for capturing experimentally observed phase behaviors, including LCST-type transitions, sequence- and length-dependent condensation, reentrant dissolution in heterotypic systems, and condensate spatial organization.

  A key theme emerging across model resolution is the trade-off between computational efficiency and physical fidelity. Minimal, one-bead and two-bead models can efficiently explore large sequence and composition spaces, and capture phenomena governed primarily by electrostatic interaction, such as reentrant phase behavior, RNA-mediated condensate regulation, and preferential partitioning in protein–RNA condensates, but cannot access the divalent ion- and base-mediated driving forces that atomistic and intermediate-resolution models have identified as major driving forces in homotypic systems. Intermediate-resolution models such as iConRNA can include explicit electrostatic, stacking, and pairing interactions and achieve superior agreement across a range of experimental observables, including sequence, length, temperature, ion, and concentration dependence on phase separation. Together, this growing toolkit of models at complementary resolutions provides the community with flexible approaches suited to different questions of biological, biomedical, and bioengineering interests.

  The physical picture established by atomistic simulation provides a useful lens for assessing the current state of CG modeling. Several features identified as important at the atomistic level remain challenging to capture in most CG frameworks. First, Mg2+ coordination involves distinct inner-sphere and outer-sphere modalities with different thermodynamic signatures. Most CG models treat Mg2+ either implicitly through DH screening or as a single hydrated sphere, without distinguishing these coordination modes. Second, the 2′-OH group on the RNA ribose ring contributes measurably to condensation propensity by enabling additional hydrogen bonding with ions and bases. Third, the LCST characteristic of nucleic acid phase transitions arises from temperature-dependent dehydration, an inherently solvent-mediated effect. Developing strategies to encode this into implicit-solvent CG frameworks would expand their applicability to temperature-dependent studies.

  Looking forward, several directions appear particularly promising. First, a systematic characterization of protein–nucleic acid interactions at the molecular level remains a critical gap. Interactions such as cation–π contacts between basic protein residues and nucleobases, electrostatic complementarity between arginine/lysine-rich regions and phosphate backbones, hydrogen bonding at the protein–RNA interfaces, and counter-ion release collectively govern nucleic acid recruitment and condensate spatial organization. Experimental thermodynamics data for individual protein–nucleic acid interactions remain sparse. As these data become available, they will enable more rigorous parameterization beyond combination rules and PMF calculations, improving the transferability across different protein–nucleic acid systems.

  Second, extending current models to distinguish RNA from DNA phase behavior is underexplored. The differential condensation propensities of RNA and DNA oligomers have been experimentally observed, and atomistic simulations have hypothesized 2′-OH as a key contributor. Incorporating this distinction into CG frameworks, either through explicit representation of sugar chemistry [141] or through differential effective parameterization at lower resolution, would enable the study of RNA–DNA hybrid systems and their biological roles.

  Third, improved treatment of divalent ion interactions would benefit models across different resolutions. The RISM-based approach employed in TIS-CAT, which captures both inner-sphere and outer-sphere coordination, offers one path forward. Alternatively, machine learning approaches trained on atomistic ion coordination data could provide transferable effective potentials.

  Fourth, the connection between molecular interactions and condensate material properties is also an important area. The observation from iConRNA that base-base interactions increased after annealing suggests that these contacts can potentially drive gelation and irreversible droplet formation, indicating that models capturing these interactions can potentially predict not only phase boundaries, but also liquid-to-solid transitions implicated in diseases. Systematic studies connecting CG model parameters to rheological observables would open an exciting bridge between simulation and experiment.

  Finally, the growing availability of experimental data on nucleic acid condensate structure, composition, and dynamics creates opportunities for more rigorous model parametrization and validation. Quantitative measurements, including SAXS-derived structural properties, single-molecule FRET, NMR characterization of ion environments, and rheological data, can serve as multi-target benchmarks for model development, moving the field toward increasingly predictive and transferable frameworks. The convergence of improved experimental characterization, advancing computational resources, and emerging machine learning approaches [72,142,143] provides exciting opportunities for the field to deliver more comprehensive models to study biomolecular phase separation in increasingly cell-like environments.
