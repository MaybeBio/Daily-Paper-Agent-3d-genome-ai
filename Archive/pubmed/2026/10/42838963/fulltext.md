# α7 integrin co-regulates chromatin accessibility and gene expression in HPV-negative head and neck cancer


## Abstract
  Epigenetic regulation and gene transcription are dynamically modulated by extracellular signals, including focal adhesions; however, the role of integrins in these processes remains poorly understood. In this study in HPV-negative head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, (HNSCC), we combined investigations in physiological three-dimensional, matrix-embedded cell models and patient-derived organoids with multi-omics analyses, bioinformatics and artificial intelligence, to unmask the integrin effector signaling network that reshapes chromatin to drive cancer. Among the 12 α/β1 integrin receptors, α7 integrin (ITGA7) emerged as the most potent modulator of histone post-translational modifications, associated with significant changes in gene transcription and chromatin accessibility. Depletion of ITGA7, achieved independently by CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and RNA interference, reduced chromatin accessibility at the AURKA locus and downregulated an AURKA-centered transcriptional program. Mechanistically, phosphoproteomics revealed significant inhibition of MAPK- and PI3K/AKT-dependent signaling with concomitant functional impairment of cell cycling and cell survival upon ITGA7 depletion. Across all analyses, machine-learning-based feature selection consistently nominated AURKA as the dominant downstream node, and tumor ITGA7 expression stratified patient overall survival in a non-linear manner. In summary, these findings establish α7 integrin as a key co-regulator of chromatin accessibility and gene transcription in HPV-negative HNSCC, acting through an AURKA-centered axis that couples adhesion signaling to cell cycle control and patient survival, and nominate AURKA, potentially targetable with clinical Aurora-A inhibitors such as alisertib, as an actionable therapeutic node downstream of the currently non-druggable ITGA7.


## Introduction
  Although epigenetic regulation and gene transcription occur in the nucleus, they are dynamically controlled through complex feedback interactions with extracellular signals in all living organisms.1 Finely tuned in healthy tissue, genetic mutations and epigenetic dysregulations in human malignancies lead to intra- and intertumoral, highly heterogeneous autonomy towards these signals.2 Be it pathological deregulation of, for example, growth factors, oxygen, metabolism or extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling,3–7 extracellular signals adaptively translate into the required phosphoproteomic repertoire to maintain survival via epigenetic regulation and gene transcription. Because extensive ECM remodeling coincides with altered expression of cell adhesion molecules (CAM) such as integrin receptors and their downstream signaling,8,9 this raises the question of to what extent integrins regulate tumor survival through participation in epigenetic regulation and gene transcription.

  Integrins are a family of transmembrane receptors coalescing with receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK), adapter proteins and protein kinases at large adhesion and signaling hubs termed focal adhesions (FA).8,10,11 Twenty-four integrins have been identified composed from 18 α and 8 β subunits, with β1 integrin being central to 12 out of the 24 integrin receptors.12 The promitogenic and prosurvival functions of integrins via FAK/Src, PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways as well as in local invasion, metastasis and therapy resistance are well documented.8,13 Intervention studies have identified integrins as therapeutically actionable targets and key regulators of DNA repair and transcription.14–19

  Cellular function depends on tightly regulated gene expression and protein biosynthesis, which are driven by extracellular signals and mediated through epigenetic and chromatin-related mechanisms. Chromatin is organized into highly condensed, transcriptionally inactive heterochromatin and more open, transcriptionally active euchromatin.2 The smallest functional unit is the nucleosome, defined as a region of histone-wrapped DNA with specific DNA and histone modifications.2,20,21 All DNA-based processes such as transcription, DNA repair and replication are highly dynamic and tightly controlled by chromatin-modifying enzymes. These modifications can alter chromatin structure and enable the recruitment of chromatin remodeling enzymes.22 In cancer, abnormal expression patterns or genomic modifications in chromatin regulators are associated with oncogenesis and cancer progression.2,23,24 Histones, particularly histone H3 and H4, undergo a range of chemical changes known as histone posttranslational modifications (HPM).22 These modifications play a central role in regulating chromatin accessibility thereby controlling processes such as transcription and DNA replication. An exemplary modification is acetylation of lysine residues (e.g., H3K27ac and H3K9ac).22 This process neutralizes the positive charge on histones weakening their interaction with negatively charged DNA. As a result, the chromatin structure becomes more relaxed typically associated with active gene transcription. This is co-regulated by methylation of histones such as H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K36me3.22 In contrast, other HPM like H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 are associated with gene repression and more compact chromatin. Phosphorylation of histones, particularly during cellular signaling and different stages of the cell cycle, can also promote chromatin relaxation.22 This facilitates access to DNA for transcription or replication machinery. Together, these modifications determine whether chromatin adopts an open, transcriptionally active state or a condensed, repressed configuration.22 Such HPM patterns and epigenetic regulation have been investigated for a number of integrins and in the context of mechanotransduction.25–27 For gene transcription, outside-in signaling via β2 integrin family members promote the induction of anti-inflammatory cascades and is mediated by transcription factors (TF) such as AP-1, Mac-1 or IEX-1.28,29 But how integrins affect epigenetic regulation and transcription in general remains to be shown.

  To address this question, we examined HPV-negative head and neck squamous carcinoma (HNSCC) models and patient-derived organoids (PDO) grown under physiological three-dimensional (3D), matrix-embedded conditions as an exemplary solid cancer type. We combined a knockdown screen for 12 α/β1 integrins with computational analyses on RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq), Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) and phosphoproteome data sets. Among all integrins, knockdown of α7 integrin (ITGA7) showed the strongest impact on HPM with significant changes in transcription and chromatin accessibility. These were mainly associated with perturbations in heterochromatin organization, cell cycle progression and cell viability by multi-omics analyses, an observation that was supported by functional assays. Overall, our results suggest that signaling mediated by α7 integrin regulates chromatin accessibility and gene expression profiles, supporting the paradigm that chromatin integrates upstream cues from cell adhesion signaling, to regulate transcriptional output.


## Results

### Integrins are predicted to act as co-regulators of epigenetic mechanisms in HNSCC
  The dual functionality of FA proteins in adhesion and downstream signaling, as well as physical connections between FA and the nuclear membrane, suggests a potential role of FA proteins in epigenetic regulation.8,11,13 Particularly in cancer such as HNSCC, FA signaling and chromatin organization are rewired to propel survival and therapy resistance.30,31 To determine the function of FA components as co-determinants of epigenetic regulation, we began by analyzing 474 focal adhesion (FA)-associated genes from Gene Ontology (GO) database. By comparing matched healthy tissues and keratinocytes from various publicly available databases, we identified 247 differentially expressed genes (DEG) associated with FA in HNSCC (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data S1). As 89% of these FA-related DEG were upregulated relative to healthy tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data S1), we subsequently constructed an interaction network between these FA genes and all genes associated with a specific cellular compartment, i.e. plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, and chromatin, as well as all DEG in the HNSCC The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset (Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data S1). A Cytoscape protein-protein interaction, STRING-based network was built with a 75% confidence score, considering only genes that share at least one interconnection across all analyzed groups (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data S1). Analysis of first neighbor interactions revealed 160 FA genes with integrins as major interactors of chromatin regulatory genes and the HNSCC DEG groups (Supplementary Fig. 1c-g). As expected, integrins were strongly represented in plasma membrane and cytoplasmic interactions; notably, they also showed extensive connectivity with nuclear and chromatin-associated genes (Fig. 1c). Among the 21 integrins analyzed, ITGB1 exhibited the highest degree of connectivity across all groups (Fig. 1c). We next examined DNA methylation data from TCGA HNSCC samples and found distinct patterns among integrins. Some integrins were highly expressed despite showing relatively high levels of methylation across their gene-associated regions (e.g., ITGB1, ITGB4, ITGA4, ITGA6, ITGA8). In contrast, others were also overexpressed but had comparatively low methylation levels (e.g., ITGB2, ITGB6, ITGB8, ITGA2, ITGA2B, ITGA11; Fig. 1d). Importantly, several integrins were associated with poorer patient outcomes regardless of their expression or methylation status. These included ITGA7, ITGB6, ITGA5, ITGA9, ITGB4, ITGA3, ITGAV, ITGB1, ITGA6, ITGB3, and ITGA2B, each associated with an approximately 20% increased risk of death (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Next, we confined our analysis to all subunits of the 12 α/β1 integrin heterodimers and merged them with 196 associated HNSCC DEG (Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data S1) in a subsequent Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) for functional enrichment with a focus on epigenetic regulatory pathways. In addition, 8 chromatin regulatory genes and a significant activation of key canonical, survival-advantaging pathways, including FAK, PAK, PI3K/AKT signaling, as well as inhibition of PTEN and RHOGDI signaling were predicted (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Data S1; and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Subsequent IPA of upstream gene regulators of integrin-associated DEG identified 13 regulatory genes, of which 11 were predicted to be more activated (activation Z-score > 1) and 2 more suppressed (-1 > activation Z-score), including 11 epigenetic regulatory genes (Supplementary Fig. 4c). We also found 237 significantly more activated and 69 more inhibited master regulators, including 42 chromatin regulatory genes (Supplementary Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data S1). Functionally, these epigenetic regulators act in positive regulation of miRNA transcription (reduced activity; 35%), histone modification (increased activity; 13%) and heterochromatin regulation (increased activity; 9%) (Fig. 1g). A general activation of chromatin openness mechanisms appears to exist in HNSCC via canonical histone modification pathways (Fig. 1h). ### Chromatin marker profiles are altered integrin-dependently
  The central role and proximal location of integrins connecting FA and the nucleus prompted us to perform an RNAi screen targeting all subunits of the 12 α/β1 integrin heterodimers and investigate their impact on 6 key HPM (Fig. 2a). Initially, we characterized a panel of 8 HNSCC cell models cultured in a physiological 3D laminin-rich extracellular matrix (lrECM). Super-resolution laser scanning microscopy allowed us to determine nuclear DNA segmentation patterns by Hoechst 33342 staining and to categorize our cell model panel based on this surrogate marker. We observed a spectrum of nuclear DNA organization ranging from homogeneous (Cal-33) to highly heterogeneous (SAS) (Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Videos S1–S8). The subsequent HPM analysis provided clearly diverging profiles (Supplementary Fig. 5b). For further investigation, H3K9me3 was selected, a marker for dense, stably organized heterochromatin, which exhibited the lowest expression in Cal-33 and the highest expression in SAS models (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c; leftmost group of columns). Next, we analyzed publicly available TCGA chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)seq data and observed a similar distribution in euchromatic and heterochromatic markers relative to our data (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Reviewing previously published whole exome sequencing data of our 8 HNSCC cell models revealed 60 protein-altering mutations in chromatin regulatory genes known for their roles in post-translational modifications (Reactome).32 Thirty-five of these 60 genes were uniquely mutated in our cell models (Supplementary Fig. 5e and Supplementary Data S2), found in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) and Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC), with two-thirds of the 60 genes being rarely mutated in various human populations (Supplementary Fig. 5f and Supplementary Data S2).   Based on these observations and our computational analysis of FA proteins, particularly integrins, we silenced 12 α/β1 integrin receptors to assess their impact on nuclear HPM. To maximize the identification of conserved molecular patterns, these experiments were conducted in two HNSCC cell models, Cal-33 and SAS, which exhibit divergent nuclear characteristics. Our data demonstrate that euchromatic (pan-H3Ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K36me3), constitutive heterochromatic (H3K9me3), and facultative heterochromatic markers (H3K27me3) were differentially modified following specific integrin depletion (Fig. 2a–c). K-means clustering analysis identified that α7, and α7β1 integrins were the most influential determinants of HPM, with α7 integrin showing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 2d; FDR < 0.05). The significant increase in the euchromatic markers H3K4me1/me3 and heterochromatic markers H3K27me3/H3K9me3 in both ITGA7-depleted cell models indicated altered transcriptional regulation. Super-resolution laser scanning microscopy of Hoechst 33342-stained, ITGA7-depleted 3D lrECM Cal-33 and SAS cell models uncovered significantly increased spatial distribution and aggregation of nuclear DNA consistent with increased heterochromatin formation compared to controls (Fig. 2e–g). In addition, staining for pan-H3Ac and H3K4me3 confirmed colocalization with low Hoechst 33342 intensity, indicating euchromatic DNA. In contrast, H3K27me3 partially localized to highly dense nuclear regions representing facultative heterochromatin (Supplementary Fig. 6a).

  We proceeded by characterizing nuclear density and α7 integrin expression in HNSCC tissue samples (Fig. 2h; right, Supplementary Fig. 6b) alongside corresponding normal tissue (Fig. 2h; left; Supplementary Fig. 6b). Consistent with the elevated area of high-density nuclear regions in ITGA7-depleted HNSCC models, HNSCC samples showed a similar, significant increase in DNA spatial distribution and aggregation in the nucleus when compared to normal tissue (Fig. 2i–k). Intriguingly, α7 integrin expression (mRNA, protein) was clearly reduced in all HNSCC specimens relative to normal tissue (Fig. 2l, m). Of note, high α7 integrin expression correlates with reduced OS in HNSCC patients (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Analysis of publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets revealed a progressive decrease in mean ITGA7 expression during HNSCC development from normal tissue (NL) to leukoplakia (LP), primary tumors (CA), and lymph node metastases (LN).33 These data also enabled us to examine the cell type-specific distribution of ITGA7 expression within HNSCC tumor biopsies (Fig. 2n–p and Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Beyond HNSCC, ITGA7 mRNA was found to be downregulated in 19 of 27 major cancer types (Fig. 2q). Together, these findings support a regulatory role for integrins, particularly α7 integrin, in chromatin organization in HNSCC.


### ITGA7 co-controls gene transcription in HNSCC models
  Hypothesizing that α7 integrin acts as an epigenetic co-regulator, we anticipated that ITGA7 depletion would alter both the transcriptome and chromatin accessibility. First, we conducted a comparative analysis of the transcriptomic response in ITGA7-depleted 3D lrECM Cal-33 and SAS models (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). We detected 2900 DEG in Cal-33 and 3847 DEG in SAS models (p ≤ 0.05; FDR < 0.05) using DESeq2 (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 7c, d; and Supplementary Data S3). IPA of canonical pathways shared between the two ITGA7-depleted cell models predicted significant activation of MAPK, PTEN, and ILK pathways (Supplementary Figs. 7e and 8a, b) as well as significant inhibition of mitotic prophase, EMT, pyrimidine deoxyribonucleotide biosynthesis, and histone modification, which prompted us to further focus on epigenetic pathways. ShinyGO analysis revealed shared, strongly impacted pathways between the two HNSCC models associated with histone methylation/demethylation and heterochromatin organization, with over 60 DEG related to histone modification (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data S3). We next focused on shared DEG) and identified 521 genes that were consistently altered across both Cal-33 and SAS models. These similarly changed DEG (scDEG) were defined as genes that were significantly differentially expressed and showed the same direction of change (either upregulation or downregulation) across datasets or experimental conditions. Among these, 63% were downregulated, and 37% were upregulated compared to controls (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Data S3). Genes such as AURKA, NPM3, and CALCRL were among the most strongly repressed, whereas WNT4, TSPAN2, and CD22 were among the most strongly induced (Fig. 3e). Chromosome enrichment analysis revealed a pronounced accumulation of scDEG on chromosomes 12 (which harbors ITGA7) and 20 (Fig. 3f). In addition, 50 of the 521 scDEG were linked to epigenetic regulation. Of these, 70% including AURKA, NPM3, and NKX1-2 were downregulated following ITGA7 depletion (Fig. 3g). The most strongly induced epigenetic regulators were SDR16C5, HMGA2 and MXD4 (Fig. 3g). ClueGO analysis further indicated that these identified genes are involved in heterochromatin regulation (Fig. 3h). We next performed IPA upstream regulator analysis on ITGA7-dependent transcriptional changes and identified a set of shared regulators that were either predicted to be activated (e.g., TNF, FOXO3, RELA) or inhibited (e.g., AREG, GLI1, AKT1). These findings suggest that such transcriptional regulators may play a role in mediating the epigenetic effects observed following ITGA7 knockdown (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Subsequently, the predicted master regulatory network identified increased activity in TGM2, JUNB, and MAP2K6, and a decrease in NUPR1, GLI1, and AREG (Fig. 3j and Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). ### ITGA7 depletion elicits profound changes in chromatin accessibility
  To directly link transcriptomic changes to modifications in chromatin accessibility, we performed ATAC-seq in tandem with the above RNA-seq (see workflow in Fig. 3a). Our analysis revealed 20,660 differentially accessible chromatin regions (DAR) in Cal-33 and 17,926 DAR in SAS models after ITGA7 depletion relative to controls (p ≤ 0.05; Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 9a, b; and Supplementary Data S4). The overall distribution of ATAC-seq peaks under control conditions was similar for both cell models, with over 70% of peaks located in non-coding regions, such as introns and intergenic regions, while the remaining 29% were found in promoters, transcription start sites (TSS), and exons (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Upon ITGA7 knockdown, we observed a general increase in peak numbers across all regions in the Cal-33 model, whereas the SAS model showed a global decrease in peaks (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Interestingly, we detected reduced DNA accessibility at TSS in both cell models, further indicating a general and corresponding loss of accessibility following ITGA7 knockdown (Fig. 4b). Comparable to our transcriptome analysis, we focused on shared DAR to identify regions of consistent chromatin change. We found 2892 shared DAR, of which 2146 (74%) represent similarly changed DAR (scDAR) in the two cell models (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Data S4). The vast majority of these scDAR localized to intronic regions (27%) and promoter TSS (49%) (Supplementary Fig. 9e), with 64% showing decreased promoter TSS accessibility (Fig. 4d). In contrast, introns and transcription termination sites (TTS), exhibited increased accessibility (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 9e). Consistent with the accumulation of scDEG to chromosomes 12 and 20, chromosomal enrichment of scDAR demonstrated similar and significant clustering on chromosomes 3, 12, and 20 (Fig. 4f). To further underline the functional involvement of α7 integrin, we searched for scDAR involved in epigenetic regulation and found peaks associated with 125 genes (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Data S4), 80% of these genes showed reduced accessibility, for example for NRDE2, RFX5 and NKX2-5, in contrast to those with increased accessibility such as ARID1A, SOX6 and SETX.   Next, we asked whether there is a mechanistic link between shared DEG and DAR by comparing ATAC-seq peaks and transcription (Fig. 4i). We observed a clear enrichment of ATAC-seq signals in TSS regions of the top 300 highly expressed genes, while the top 300 lowly expressed genes showed a loss of peak signals under all conditions and cell models. Likewise, an increased accessibility in upregulated genes and decreased accessibility in downregulated genes was found after ITGA7 depletion, indicating a direct relationship between chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity (Fig. 4j). We identified 47 peaks in shared DEG and DAR, including 34 mirroring genes (genes that show the same vector of change across different conditions or models), of which 9 were upregulated with increased accessibility and 23 downregulated with reduced accessibility (Fig. 4k). Given the consistent impact of promoter accessibility on gene expression, we employed functional enrichment analysis on all shared DEG and DAR, which revealed 57% of these genes to be engaged in negative regulation of protein kinase activity, primarily through the regulation of protein phosphorylation (Fig. 4l). Narrowing down to shared DEG and DAR in epigenetic regulation provided 7 genes (NKX1-2, BRPF3, SOX9, CBX6, RFX5, MXD4, DYRK1A), of which 28% function in histone modification, 9% in chromatin organization and 5% in chromatin remodeling (Fig. 4m, n and Supplementary Fig. 9f). To search for potential TF that bind to these differentially accessible chromatin regions, we conducted HOMER de novo motif discovery and known motif scanning in more and less accessible regions across both cell models. Fourteen common TF were identified in regions with increased accessibility, including FOSL2, BATF, and FRA2, while 20 TF were associated with decreased accessibility, including ELF4, KLF15, and NFY (Supplementary Fig. 9g, h and Supplementary Data S4). By merging predicted transcriptome regulatory gene datasets deduced from IPA with ATAC-seq motif analyses, we discovered 11 overlapping TF, with JUNB, FOSL1, and RELA being activated and only SPDEF being inhibited (Fig. 4o). Overall, integration of transcriptomic and ATAC-seq data indicates that ITGA7 knockdown leads to increased heterochromatin formation paralleled by reduced gene expression based on alterations in DNA accessibility and epigenetic regulation.


### ITGA7 depletion modulates protein phosphorylation and expression, particularly of cell cycle- and epigenetic-associated signaling mediators
  Next, we investigated how α7 integrin mechanistically regulates the observed regulation of transcriptome and chromatin accessibility and examined 2010 proteins and their phosphorylation events by Kinex™ antibody microarray in whole cell lysates from 3D lrECM, ITGA7-depleted Cal-33 and SAS models (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 10a). We found 290 significantly altered, differentially phosphorylated proteins (DPP) upon ITGA7 depletion relative to controls (p ≤ 0.05; Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 10b, c; and Supplementary Data S5). Canonical pathway analysis revealed similar activation of mTOR, EGF, STAT, and telomerase signaling pathways in the two cell models (Supplementary Fig. 10d and Supplementary Data S5). Decreased activities were seen for PPAR, ATM, PTEN, and CLEAR pathways. Shared upstream and master regulators included TP53, VEGFA, MUS81, and the mTORC1 complex (Supplementary Fig. 10e, f). Among the 210 similar changed DPP (scDPP) of both cell models, 136 (65%) showed increased and 74 (35%) decreased phosphorylation (Fig. 5b, c and Supplementary Data S5). IPA revealed similar changes in both cell models in activated (PI3K, RAC, cell cycle) and inhibited (BMP, PXR, GDNF) signaling axes (Supplementary Fig. 10l and Supplementary Data S5). Among more than 40 master regulators from scDEP, SALL4 and CDC37 were the only upstream regulators predicted to be significantly relevant (Supplementary Fig. 10p, q and Supplementary Data S5).

  Building on our earlier findings that ITGA7 influences chromatin accessibility, we next examined epigenetic regulators within the (phospho)proteomic datasets. Analysis of scDPP identified 59 proteins linked to epigenetic regulation. Of these, 31 (53%) showed increased phosphorylation, while 28 (47%) showed decreased phosphorylation, with prominent examples including JUN, FOXO1A, and RORA (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Data S5). Overall, phosphorylation changes affected several transcriptional and chromatin-associated regulators, such as JUN, FOXO1A, RORA, AML2, and TP53. In parallel, analysis of scDEP identified 34 epigenetic regulators. Among these, 20 (59%; top examples: GADD153, STAT6, NPM1) were upregulated, while 14 (41%; top examples: JUN, ARRB1, HIPK2) were downregulated (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Data S5).

  Pathway analysis of both scDPP and scDEP revealed strong enrichment for cell cycle regulation and histone modification processes (Fig. 5i, j). Notably, several chromatin-regulating proteins exhibited differential protein phosphorylation, indicating direct modulation of both chromatin-activating and chromatin-repressive mechanisms following ITGA7 depletion. For example, phosphorylation of the histone acetyltransferase CREBBP/CBP was reduced at S121/S124 by 24%, and phosphorylation of the MAPK-dependent kinase MSK1 (RPS6KA5) was reduced at S376 by 33%. CREBBP acts as a key transcriptional co-activator that acetylates proteins including histones, depositing acetylation marks such as H3K27ac, while MSK1 phosphorylates histone H3 (e.g., H3S10 and H3S28) during transcriptional activation. Reduced phosphorylation of these proteins indicates impaired enhancer activation and diminished histone modification.

  Conversely, components of the chromatin-repressive machinery were also affected. Phosphorylation of HDAC4 at S632 was reduced by 19%, together with a 15% increase in phosphorylation of the SIN3A scaffold protein at S832, pointing toward enhanced HDAC-mediated chromatin silencing. Additional phosphorylation changes were observed in epigenetic enzymes such as KMT2C (MLL3) at S4267 and PRMT5 at T634. Further alterations were detected in chromatin-associated signaling pathways. A 25% decreased phosphorylation of FOXO1 at the canonical AKT target sites S256 and a 32% decrease at S319 indicates perturbed PI3K/AKT signaling downstream of ITGA7. Additional phosphorylation changes were observed in AMPKα1 (T183/S184) and WEE1 (S642). In contrast, expression of the histone chaperone NPM1 was significantly upregulated by 51%, while its phosphorylation changed (11% increase at S4; 16% reduction at T199), suggesting altered functional activity.

  Integrating transcriptomic and (phospho)proteomic data revealed an overall suppression of chromatin-opening mechanisms in HNSCC following ITGA7 silencing, particularly through canonical histone modification pathways (Fig. 5k). Together, these findings identify chromatin-associated signaling mediators and epigenetic regulators that may underlie ITGA7-dependent transcriptional programs and changes in chromatin accessibility.

### α7 integrin defines a clinically relevant signaling axis controlling HNSCC cell viability
  To define the signaling network underlying the observed functional effects of ITGA7 depletion, we combined random forest (RF) feature selection with network-based correlation clustering (Fig. 6), identifying 63 candidate genes associated with the ITGA7 signaling axis (Supplementary Data S7). Experimental validation using a targeted RNAi screen in 3D-grown Cal-33 models (Fig. 7a) assessed single and combinatorial knockdowns with ITGA7 based on delta (Δ) cell viability scores, reflecting similarity to ITGA7 depletion. Using a <15% similarity threshold, 42 of 63 candidates phenocopied ITGA7 knockdown. Among these, seven genes (AURKA, EHMT2, SEPTIN8, SPTY2D1, LSM14B, BDKRB2, SOX9) showed no additional effect upon co-silencing, indicating their involvement in a shared signaling axis. To further refine pathway contribution, we calculated a ΔPathScore (Supplementary Methods), identifying 15 genes with ~50% probability of belonging to ITGA7 axis. Across all analytical approaches, AURKA consistently ranked highest, showing 90–99% similarity to ITGA7 depletion (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Data S7). Independent linear regression analysis identified 24 genes closely aligned (<15% deviation) with ITGA7-dependent effects, further supporting AURKA as a central node in α7 integrin signaling (Supplementary Fig. 12c).

  We next examined the clinical relevance of this signaling axis. Given the complex distribution of ITGA7 expression in tumors (Fig. 2), we avoided predefined high/low cutoffs and instead applied a non-linear rank-based survival analysis. TCGA patient data were processed and ranked according to tumor ITGA7 log2 fold change (tumor vs normal), and overall survival (OS) was evaluated using overlapping sliding windows (80 patients per window, step size 10; Fig. 7d, Supplementary Fig. 12d). Within each window, the restricted mean survival time (RMST) was calculated up to a window-specific maximum follow-up time (τ). RMST showed a pronounced non-linear distribution across ITGA7 expression, with patients in the intermediate low expression range (~−0.7 to −0.07) showing significantly improved survival compared to adjacent groups and permutation-derived null models (2000 permutations). The observed RMST peak exceeded the upper bounds of the permutation null bands, indicating that this localized survival enrichment is unlikely to arise by random chance. To evaluate stability, we performed bootstrap-based dominance probability analysis (2000 resamples). For each window, ΔRMST (RMSTwindow - RMSTrest) was recalculated across bootstrap iterations. The bootstrap-based dominance probability analysis confirmed the robustness of this survival advantage, with probabilities approaching 100%, whereas both expression extremes showed no consistent benefit.

  To translate this continuous survival pattern into discrete patient groups, we performed systematic Kaplan-Meier analyses for all possible 4-bin splits (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f). This revealed non-random clustering of optimal cut points at specific ITGA7 expression, confirming stable survival transition zones (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f). The majority of hits recapitulated the non-linear survival structure observed in Fig. 7d. Most significant combinations of Kaplan–Meier survival probability demonstrated that survival is not increased steadily with decreasing or increasing ITGA7 levels. Notably, the intermediate low ITGA7 group (−0.7 to −0.07) exhibited the most favorable survival, whereas both lower (−3.0 to −0.7) and higher (0.5–4.4) expression extremes were associated with poorer outcomes. Pairwise log-rank testing confirmed significant separation (p = 0.03; strongest p = 0.01), indicating that survival differences are confined to defined expression intervals.

  Given this interval-specific survival pattern, we next tested whether predicted ITGA7-axis genes (Fig. 7c) support survival differences within ITGA7-defined patient groups. Patients were subdivided within each ITGA7-defined cohort according to the median expression of individual signature genes, and 5 year Kaplan–Meier OS probabilities were calculated. Genes reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05) were clustered to evaluate combinatorial survival effects (Fig. 7f). To account for subgroup size and avoid overinterpreting small subsets with high survival probabilities, we implemented a weighted survival score (KM probability × subgroup size). Reduced ITGA7 expression consistently associated with improved survival. Within the low ITGA7 cohort, the gene signatures with MARCKS, PIM2, SP4, and AURKB showed the highest survival (100%), whereas in the high ITGA7 group, MET, EHMT2, MAPK1, PDPK1, and NEK2 were associated with >80% survival. NKX1-2, CRADD, and RPS6KA5 demonstrated consistent survival associations across groups, suggesting context-independent effects.

  To mechanistically validate this clinically supported axis, we profiled AKT/MAPK, cell cycle, and Aurora signaling in Cal-33 and SAS models following ITGA7 knockdown (Fig. 7g, Supplementary Fig. 13a-b and Supplementary Data S7). Both models showed concordant reductions in Aurora A, CDC25C, Ki-67, cyclins, and α7 integrin, along with decreased AKT and ERK1/2 signaling. Phosphorylation of AKT (Ser473), FOXO1 (Ser256), and Aurora A (Thr288) was markedly reduced. In contrast, stress-response pathways were activated, including increased JNK1/2 expression, enhanced c-JUN and ATF2 phosphorylation, and activation of p38 MAPK signaling. Moreover, GSK-3β activity increased, consistent with a shift toward anti- mitogenic signaling. Across all datasets, AURKA emerged as the most consistent and dominant node within the ITGA7-dependent network.

  Consistent with this, ATAC-seq revealed reduced chromatin accessibility at the AURKA promoter following ITGA7 silencing (−23% in Cal-33; −39% in SAS; Fig. 7h), with independent assays showing stronger reductions (−60% and −70%, respectively; Fig. 7j). Correspondingly, AURKA mRNA expression decreased by 54% (Cal-33) and 74% (SAS; Fig. 7i). In contrast, WNT4 expression increased markedly (~400% in Cal-33; ~900% in SAS), confirming selective regulatory effects.

  Finally, integration of transcriptomic, epigenetic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic and functional data enabled reconstruction of a coherent α7 integrin signaling axis (Fig. 7a, k and Supplementary Fig. 14a). This integrated model links adhesion signaling to coordinated regulation of AKT/MAPK, cell cycle, and Aurora kinase pathways, positioning AURKA as a central mediator connecting ITGA7 to chromatin state and cell viability. Notably, genes functionally aligned with ITGA7 depletion also stratified patient survival, demonstrating direct convergence between mechanistic biology and clinical outcome. Together, these findings establish α7 integrin as a determinant of biologically distinct tumor states and identify the ITGA7-AURKA axis as a clinically relevant and potentially targetable vulnerability in HNSCC.


## Discussion
  Understanding how extracellular signals directly influence chromatin organization, accessibility, and transcriptional competence remains a central challenge in cancer biology. Importantly, despite their known autonomous properties, cancer cells strongly depend on environmental cues such as extracellular matrix, nutrients, hormones, and hypoxia, regulating processes including cancer stem cell niche organization, immune cell infiltration, and sustained angiogenesis during tumor progression.7,9,13,34 While integrins are well-known determinants of cell survival, mechanotransduction, and therapy resistance, their role in epigenetic control has remained largely indirect and to be defined. Here, we demonstrate α7 integrin (ITGA7) as a previously unrecognized regulator of chromatin accessibility and transcription, establishing a direct mechanistic link between cell adhesion signaling and epigenetic regulation in HPV-negative HNSCC. These findings position cell adhesion receptors not merely as upstream signaling components but as active determinants of the epigenetic landscape.

  A central advance of our study is the demonstration that ITGA7 functionally drives chromatin state transitions. Multi-omics analyses revealed that ITGA7 depletion induces a general shift toward a repressive chromatin configuration, characterized by increased heterochromatin organization, reduced promoter accessibility, and transcriptional downregulation. These molecular changes translated into consistent phenotypic effects, including decreased viability, reduced clonogenic survival, and impaired proliferation across multiple 3D HNSCC models and patient-derived organoids, establishing ITGA7 as a regulator of tumor cell fitness in a physiologically relevant microenvironment. Importantly, ITGA7 was perturbed by two mechanistically independent modalities, i.e., RNA interference and CRISPR/Cas9 knockout, which produced convergent chromatin, transcriptional, and phenotypic outcomes across multiple genetically, epigenetically, and phenotypically diverse models and patient-derived organoids. This concordance across orthogonal reagents controls for reagent-specific off-target effects and indicates that the observed phenotypes reflect on-target loss of ITGA7.35

  Genetic and pharmacological perturbation of transmembrane receptors has demonstrated that growth factor receptors and their downstream signaling mediators, mechanotransduction-associated cytoskeletal components, and ion channels contribute to genome organization, chromatin accessibility, and gene transcription.26,34,36,37 In line with this, extracellular cues are integrated through signaling networks that converge on chromatin regulation. In addition to transcription factors, histone post-translational modifications, and chromatin-modifying enzymes, survival, proliferation, differentiation, and migration critically depend on the integration of extra- and intracellular biochemical as well as mechanical signals. Epigenetic plasticity makes dynamic adaptation of transcriptional programs in response to these inputs feasible, underpinning the fact that genetic and epigenetic alterations cannot be fully understood in isolation but must take into account the environmental context. Prominent examples include cell-ECM interactions and growth in three-dimensional extracellular matrix environments, both of which profoundly influence chromatin organization, gene expression, DNA repair, and responsiveness to anticancer therapies.7,19,30,38–41 However, a direct epigenetic regulatory function of cell adhesion molecules remains largely to be clarified. Our findings extend the existing body of literature by identifying α7 integrin as a key upstream regulator linking adhesion signaling to epigenetic regulation and gene transcription.

  To mechanistically resolve these observations, we identified a signaling-chromatin axis centered on AURKA, a master regulator of mitotic progression integrating signals controlling centrosome dynamics, spindle assembly, and cell proliferation.42,43 ITGA7 depletion reduced chromatin accessibility at the AURKA locus, resulting in decreased transcription and protein expression paralleled by diminished cell cycling and survival. This multi-layer validation uncovered a direct link between adhesion signaling, chromatin remodeling, and cellular phenotype, positioning AURKA as a key downstream effector of integrin-dependent epigenetic regulation.

  At the molecular level, ITGA7 signaling converges on the chromatin-modifying machinery. Multiple epigenetic regulators were altered upon ITGA7 silencing, including HMGA2 and MXD4,44,45 as well as chromatin-associated enzymes such as EHMT2, CBX6, PHC1, BRPF3, and DYRK1A46–54. The global reduction in chromatin accessibility at transcription start sites and concordant transcriptional repression indicate a shift toward a more compact chromatin status.

  In parallel, transcription factor networks were affected showing an enrichment of RELA, SPDEF, ATF4, and AP-1 components (JUN, FOSL1, JUNB),28,55 thereby suggesting disrupted transcription factor-mediated chromatin regulation. Both cell models investigated demonstrated consistently reduced phosphorylation of CREBBP and MSK1, indicating confined activation of chromatin-opening mechanisms, whereas altered phosphorylation of HDAC4 and SIN3A collectively supports enhanced chromatin-repressive activity.56–61 In addition, decreased FOXO1 phosphorylation points to attenuated PI3K/AKT signaling, further linking adhesion signaling to epigenetic pathways. Together, these findings indicate that ITGA7 signaling coordinates multiple chromatin-regulatory complexes including histone methyltransferases, Polycomb components, and acetylation regulators to control the balance between chromatin accessibility and compaction.

  Notably, ITGA7 exhibits a context-dependent relationship with tumor progression and patient outcome. Although its expression decreases during the dedifferentiation of normal tissue to carcinoma, functional analyses demonstrate that ITGA7 is inevitable for tumor cell survival and proliferation in certain contexts, which need to be defined in detail. Consequently, we speculate that ITGA7 acts as a tumor suppressor in HPV-negative HNSCC. ITGA7 expression progressively decreases with increasing pressure of malignant transformation, similar to a Red Queen-like dynamic, with higher malignancy associated with lower ITGA7 levels. Future studies are needed to investigate this hypothesis in detail. The fact that ITGA7 expression and patient survival are in a non-linear relationship is also reflected in the patient data. These data demonstrate a non-linear relationship between ITGA7 expression and overall survival. Intermediate expression levels suggest that these are associated with a more favorable prognosis, while both low and high levels correlate with a poorer prognosis. Our findings suggest that ITGA7 defines different tumor stages rather than functioning as a linear biomarker.

  The present study was deliberately restricted to HPV-negative HNSCC. In HPV-positive disease, the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 dominate epigenetic regulation through upregulating DNA methyltransferases and driving a globally distinct, hypermethylated landscape, and their expression is sufficient to reprogram the DNA-methylation profile of HPV-negative cells.31,62 Including HPV-positive models in the present study would therefore superimpose a strong, virus-driven epigenetic program that would likely mask the more specific chromatin adhesion signal under investigation; thus, isolating the HPV-negative subtype is a prerequisite for specifically attributing chromatin changes to ITGA7. HPV-negative HNSCC is, moreover, the subtype of greatest unmet clinical need, being more therapy-refractory and carrying a worse prognosis than HPV-positive oropharyngeal carcinoma.63 Whether HPV-driven tumors retain or bypass ITGA7-dependent chromatin regulation is an important question that we will address in a dedicated follow-up study.

  From a translational perspective, the ITGA7-AURKA axis may present a strategically elegant, therapeutically actionable signaling module as it integrates adhesion cues, cell cycling, and chromatin regulation. The convergence of our multi-omics data, functional validation, and patient analyses underscores its biological and clinical relevance. These results provide an excellent basis for therapeutic strategies targeting the disruption of adhesion-dependent chromatin regulation. Given the complexity, feedback mechanisms, and evasion possibilities of ITGA7-AURKA signaling, future studies must elucidate in detail the functional effectiveness and hierarchical vulnerability of the numerous potential targets of this signaling cascade using genetic and pharmacological intervention approaches. In this regard, no selective pharmacological inhibitor of α7β1 integrin currently exists, and clinically available integrin-directed agents target distinct heterodimers; pan-integrin inhibition would engage multiple receptors simultaneously and preclude clean attribution to ITGA7. The most immediately actionable node emerging from this mechanism is therefore the downstream effector AURKA, for which Aurora-A inhibitors such as alisertib are in clinical development, whereas selective targeting of the ITGA7 pathway itself remains contingent on the development of selective compounds.

  Although in vivo validation and modulation of upstream mechanical cues remain important next steps, the use of 3D matrix-based models and patient-derived organoids provides a physiologically highly relevant and clinically translatable system capturing key aspects of tumor microenvironment signaling.64,65 According to our observations, ITGA7 depletion alone is sufficient to reprogram chromatin accessibility and tumor cell behavior, demonstrating a direct causal link between adhesion signaling and epigenetic regulation. Integrin-ECM engagement in three-dimensional laminin-rich matrix has been shown to govern tumor cell survival and the malignant phenotype as well as to predict outcomes subsequently confirmed in vivo. Hence, this system was historically validated precisely based on its capacity to recapitulate integrin biology in tissue.66–68 In HNSCC specifically, patient-derived organoid drug and radiotherapy responses mirror the clinical outcomes of the matched patients.64,69,70 We nonetheless acknowledge, as a limitation, the absence of orthotopic and immune-competent in vivo models. These represent the logical next step toward therapeutic development but are not required to support the mechanistic conclusions established here across three complementary human systems and corroborated by patient survival data.

  In summary, this study establishes α7 integrin (ITGA7) as a causal co-regulator of chromatin accessibility and gene expression in HPV-negative HNSCC. Loss of ITGA7, achieved independently by CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and RNA interference across multiple 3D matrix-embedded HNSCC models and patient-derived organoids, suppresses MAPK- and PI3K/AKT-dependent signaling, reduces chromatin accessibility at the AURKA locus, downregulates the AURKA-centered transcriptional program, and impairs cell cycle progression and survival. Because tumor ITGA7 expression stratifies patient overall survival, yet ITGA7 itself is not currently druggable, its downstream effector AURKA, targetable with clinical Aurora-A inhibitors such as alisertib, constitutes an immediately actionable therapeutic entry point in this therapy-refractory subtype. More broadly, these findings demonstrate that integrin-mediated adhesion directly co-determines the epigenetic and transcriptional landscape of cancer cells.
