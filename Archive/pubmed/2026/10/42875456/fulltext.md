# Molecular dynamics guided all-atom reconstruction of cryo-ET maps reveals mechanisms of histone tail mediated chromatin compaction


## Abstract
  Dynamics and physical state of chromatin are crucial in regulating gene expression, DNA replication, and repair. Intrinsically disordered histone tails were previously recognized as key modulators of chromatin states. However, detailed atomistic mechanisms by which histone tail dynamics are associated with chromatin compaction and higher-order chromatin organization remain poorly understood. In this work, we combine extensive all-atom molecular dynamics simulations of tri-nucleosomes with varying linker lengths and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) of native nucleosome arrays. Our approach offers distinct advantages as it elucidates realistic inter-nucleosomal interactions and tri-nucleosome orientations derived from physics-based MD simulations, enabling a more accurate and physically grounded interpretation of cryo-ET data. The results reveal that tri-nucleosome models can be successfully used for fitting and the near-atomistic interpretation of cryo-ET density maps of native human chromatin. Moreover, histone tails were shown to promote chromatin compaction via three major patterns: through histone-DNA interactions, histone H2A-H4 and H3-H4 tail-tail interactions. Notably, the distributions of MD-generated structural parameters of tri-nucleosomes were found to be in strong agreement with those of experimental condensed chromatin arrays.


## Introduction
  Chromatin, a nucleoprotein complex in which genomic DNA is packaged with histones and diverse non-histone proteins and associated RNAs, plays a central role in regulating cellular processes, such as gene expression, DNA replication, transcription, recombination, and DNA repair. The dynamic folding and unfolding of chromatin are essential for maintaining its functional state, with histone N- and C-terminal tails playing a central role in these processes1,2. Histone tails, which are intrinsically disordered protein regions, participate in various interactions that facilitate chromatin compaction, the higher-order organization of chromatin fiber and stability3–5.

  Understanding the physical principles governing chromatin folding remains a key challenge in chromatin biology6–8. A number of experimental techniques, including cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), X-ray scattering, super-resolution fluorescence microscopy, NMR and chromosome conformation capture methods, have been employed to explore the structural organization of chromatin both in vitro and in vivo9–16. While these methods have provided crucial insights into chromatin architecture, achieving atomic-level resolution remains difficult, particularly for histone tails in condensed chromatin regions. This limitation arises from the intrinsic flexibility of chromatin and the transient, heterogeneous nature of histone tail interactions with both nucleosomal and linker DNA17–19. Earlier structural models of chromatin posited that histone tails may mediate the inter-nucleosomal interactions predominantly through nucleosome face-to-face stacking across very short distances9,20–22, while recent cryo-EM studies suggest that nucleosomes may interact through their tails at larger distances and various orientations12,23,24. A full mechanistic understanding of how different histone tails contribute to chromatin compaction remains incomplete25.

  Another major challenge involves reconstructing realistic 3D chromatin models from cryo-EM or ET maps. Traditionally, nucleosome organization has been inferred by rigidly fitting mono-nucleosome crystal structures into high-density volumes obtained from cryo-ET or EM data9,11,12. While these approaches provide valuable structural insights, they suffer from some limitations: they rely on arbitrary decisions about nucleosome chain direction, often connect nucleosomes using straight DNA linkers, do not take into account histone tail-mediated interactions and ignore the geometric and torsional constraints of DNA. These issues are exacerbated in highly condensed chromatin regions, where ambiguous fits and poor DNA resolution complicate the generation of reliable models. Recent studies have tried to address these limitations by incorporating more realistic representations of linker DNA. For example, linker DNA models have been reconstructed from cryo-ET densities using polymer-based models, such as worm-like chains26. In addition, fragment-based approaches combined with hierarchical chain growth have been employed to generate conformational ensembles of linker DNA at atomistic resolution27. Despite these advances, a consistent and physically rigorous framework that integrates linker DNA geometry, nucleosome orientation, and array-level organization remains lacking.

  To overcome these limitations and provide mechanistic insight into nucleosomal array folding, we employed all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of different tri-nucleosome models with various linker lengths. Tri-nucleosomes represent the minimal chromatin unit capable of capturing inter-nucleosomal geometry, linker DNA conformations, and histone tail interactions. We demonstrated that these tri-nucleosome models allow for near-atomistic interpretation of cryo-ET density maps of native human chromatin. Namely, these models specify the relative orientation of consecutive nucleosomes, while simultaneously and inherently incorporating DNA and protein geometric and torsional constraints, even in partially resolved maps. This tri-nucleosome-based fitting framework provides a practically superior strategy for building biologically meaningful chromatin models. Additionally, we present a systematic analysis of tri-nucleosome dynamics in systems with and without histone tails and show that histone tails are critical for driving nucleosome array compaction and mediating inter-nucleosomal interactions. Importantly, we find that the tri-nucleosome conformations generated in our simulations reproduce key stereological features of experimental native chromatin. Moreover, we reveal previously uncharacterized modes of histone tail interaction that may provide mechanistic insights into higher-order chromatin structure.


## Results

### Tri-nucleosome fitting into experimental cryo-ET maps
  Previous efforts to model chromatin and nucleosome arrays predominantly relied on fitting of a mono-nucleosome core particle X-ray crystal structure into the high-density regions of cryo-ET/EM maps9,11,12,28,29. However, mono-nucleosome-based fitting strategies have several inherent limitations. One major drawback is the arbitrary selection of the nucleosome connection path, which often leads to ambiguous or inconsistent definitions of consecutive nucleosome arrangement (Fig. 1a and see Fig. S1 for strong contrast with a black background and volume rendering of cryo-ET density maps. The corresponding XZ views of the cryo-ET density maps are shown in Fig. S2). Additionally, straight-line DNA segments are typically used to connect nucleosomes and ignore the torsional mechanics and geometric constraints of DNA, such as twist, rise, and bend, that are essential for maintaining the structural integrity and topological regulation of chromatin23,30. Another major limitation of mono-nucleosome fitting occurs in condensed chromatin regions within the cryo-ET map, where multiple mono-nucleosomes can be erroneously fitted into the same high electron density volume area, often in implausible mutual orientations (Fig. 1b).   Our approach utilizes various tri-nucleosome conformations derived from MD simulations for fitting to the cryo-ET maps. These conformations inherently define the relative spatial organization and order of three consecutive nucleosomes (Nuc1, Nuc2, and Nuc3), providing information about nucleosome arrangement and a more meaningful representation of local regions of chromatin and nucleosome clutches (Fig. 1c). Importantly, since these tri-nucleosome conformations are sampled from physically plausible MD trajectories, the associated linker DNA segments naturally embody torsional strain and geometric constraints, possibly offering a more accurate and/or plausible structural conformation and interpretation for modeling of nucleosome arrays. Moreover, when tri-nucleosome core structures are aligned with appropriate high-density regions in the map, the linker DNA segments connecting the three nucleosomes can be positioned in alignment with the corresponding DNA density, even when the linker DNA densities are partially missing or poorly resolved in the cryo-ET map (Fig. 1d). As such, tri-nucleosome fitting can provide insight into the geometry and connectivity of nucleosome arrays within chromatin.

## Discussion
  Recent super-resolution microscopy studies showed that nucleosomes are often arranged in discrete and irregular clusters (“clutches”) containing anywhere from a few to a couple of dozen nucleosomes in mammalian cells47–49. In our study, using all-atom MD simulations of tri-nucleosomes with different linker lengths representing structural units of nucleosome clutches, we provide an integrative structural framework that connects atomistic simulations with cryo-ET map-based chromatin reconstructions. Our results establish atomistic tri-nucleosome fitting as a paradigm for understanding native chromatin plasticity at the level of nucleosome clutches. Traditional approaches for cryo-ET/EM map interpretation typically involve fitting individual mono-nucleosome structures into local high-density regions. However, this strategy often results in nucleosomes that remain disconnected or artificially linked by straight DNA linkers, disregarding the geometric and torsional constraints of DNA. These limitations become even more pronounced in highly condensed chromatin regions. Moreover, in such cases, the atomistic-level modeling is limited because histone tail conformations are typically not resolved in mono-nucleosome PDB structures and are therefore omitted in conventional modeling approaches.

  Our study demonstrates that fitting tri-nucleosome conformations, sampled from physically realistic MD trajectories, into cryo-ET maps in some cases may resolve these limitations. The MD conformations not only maintain the relative positioning and orientation of three consecutive nucleosomes but also incorporate histone tails and linker DNA that reflect the intrinsic torsional mechanics and geometric constraints of DNA and proteins in chromatin50–52. We show that tri-nucleosome-based modeling may improve the fitting accuracy and provide atomistic structural interpretation of nucleosomal arrays. The proposed approach also reduces manual intervention during the model construction by embedding disjointed nucleosomal relationships and linker DNA geometry directly into the fitting template, especially valuable for regions where the linker DNA density is missing or partially resolved. We provide a library of successfully fitted conformations that can be used in the future by other researchers. Despite these advantages, the accuracy of the resulting models is constrained by the quality and completeness of the cryo-ET density maps. In particular, map denoising, routinely used by experimentalists, cannot recover information that is missing because of the limited tomographic tilt range. The resulting missing wedge may cause anisotropic resolution and elongation artifacts and increase uncertainty in density regions that are unfavorably oriented relative to the tilt axis. Moreover, no denoising method can fully distinguish weak biological density from noise.

  Beyond structural fitting, our tri-nucleosome simulations reveal distinct structural and dynamic features of nucleosomal arrays. Histone tails have long been recognized as essential contributors to higher-order chromatin folding, self-association and compaction. It has been shown that even high concentrations of divalent cations, like magnesium, are unable to induce self-association of nucleosomal arrays without H3 and H4 histone tails53,54. Indeed, the intrinsically disordered nature of histone tails and their enrichment in positively charged residues enable them to partially neutralize the negative charge of DNA, thereby facilitating regular chromatin fiber compaction and inter-nucleosomal interactions23,54–57. Our simulations reveal that the presence of histone tails is associated with the compaction of tri-nucleosomes, as evidenced by low inter-nucleosomal distances, a narrow radius of gyration distribution and the conformational free-energy landscapes with narrow energy wells. All results have been confirmed in different independent runs. To complement previous studies, we provide an atomistic analysis of tail-mediated compaction of nucleosome arrays, which are mostly mediated by tail–DNA interactions but also involve tail–tail interactions. It has been observed that most tail–tail interactions involve lysines from H2A-H4 and H3-H4 tail pairs, forming salt bridges and hydrogen bonds, maintained in some cases on a microsecond time scale.

  Previous studies have shown that the H3 tail predominantly stabilizes inter-nucleosomal interactions58–60 and the absence of histone H3 tails leads to more extended chromatin structures22,36,40,61,62. Our data show that H3 tails can span the linker DNA regions to form extensive networks of tail–tail and tail–DNA interactions with consecutive nucleosomes. Interestingly, important post-translational modification sites, like H3K9, have been found to be involved in a substantial fraction of these interactions, and, as was shown previously, its methylation could increase the interaction with neighboring nucleosomes and facilitate chromatin compaction63. Our results also indicate that H2A and H2B tails might play a relatively minor role in compacting the nucleosome arrays. However, it should be mentioned that these results do not necessarily apply to histone variant nucleosomes. For example, H2A.Z C-terminal tails may extend to the neighboring nucleosomes, form interactions with their acidic patch and mediate higher-order compaction64,65.

All these observations confirm previous works42,66–68 and extend them by showing that tail–tail and tail–DNA interactions may mediate inter-nucleosome interactions, their spacing and dynamics. Notably, the tail-mediated inter-nucleosomal interactions are transient and allow for local dynamical behavior even in the condensed state. We should acknowledge that sampling of disordered histone tails in such large systems remains limited due to the computational cost of all-atom simulations. In addition, limitations in force-field accuracy and the omission of chromatin-associated factors, such as linker histones and other binding proteins, constrain the representativeness of the sampled histone tail conformations in the native chromatin. As a complementary approach, coarse-grained (CG) methods can offer improved sampling efficiency and may potentially capture linker DNA flexibility, large-scale chromatin heterogeneity and histone tail interactions, even though without high atomistic resolution65,69–72.

  Several cryo-ET studies of native chromatin vitrified in vitro12 and in situ23 showed a multiplex heterogeneous nucleosome arrangement with nucleosome disks contacting each other in face-to-face, face-to-side, and side-to-side orientations. We show that there is a noticeable difference between the structure and dynamics of tri-nucleosome systems with different linker lengths.   Our all-atom MD simulations reveal a mechanism for interaction between two disjointed nucleosomes (Fig. 4).   In conclusion, our study introduces a strategy for interpreting the cryo-ET maps and reconstructing nucleosome array structure at atomistic resolution with inherently realistic DNA torsional constraints and inter-nucleosomal relationships. This approach may help to resolve ambiguities in cryo-ET maps, particularly in condensed chromatin regions, and ultimately capture biologically meaningful nucleosome connectivity.

### Molecular dynamics simulation protocol
  All MD simulations were performed with the package GROMACS version 2022.381, using the AMBER 14SB force field for protein parameters and OL15 for nucleic acids82,83, along with the Optimal Point Charge (OPC) water model. This water model has been shown to accurately reproduce bulk water properties and improve the accuracy of simulations involving nucleic acids and intrinsically disordered proteins84. In each simulation, the initial tri-nucleosome model was solvated in a box with a minimum distance of 20 Å between the nucleosome atoms and the box edges. Sodium chloride (NaCl) was added to the system to achieve a concentration of 150 mM.

  Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 10,000 steps. The system was then gradually heated to 300 K over 800 ps, with restraints applied to the atoms, followed by 1 ns of equilibration (we will refer to these models as “initial models” throughout the text). Production simulations were conducted in the isobaric-isothermal (NPT) ensemble for up to 4 µs. Temperature was maintained at 300 K using the Bussi thermostat, and pressure was controlled at 1 atm using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. A cutoff of 10 Å was applied to short-range non-bonded vdW interactions. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cut-off of 10 Å was used to calculate all long-range electrostatic interactions. Periodic boundary conditions were applied, and covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm85, allowing for a 2 fs integration time step. The coordinates of each solute were recorded every 100 ps, and a total of 40,000 frames in each simulation run were collected for analysis. Three independent simulation runs were performed for each system, 4 µs each. A summary of the simulation setup and details is provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.


### Cryo-electron tomographic maps
  Cryo-ET maps of chromatin from human K562 cells under two experimental conditions (0 mM Mg²⁺ and 0.75 mM Mg²⁺) were obtained from previous work12. To aid 3D visualization and to improve the resolution of individual nucleosome orientations, we processed the tomograms using a deep learning-based denoising method32. The chromatin samples, vitrification conditions, and resulting cryotomograms are listed in the Supplementary Table 1 of ref. 12 and the raw tilt series are available from the Dryad data repository (https://datadryad.orghttps://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.ttdz08m21). Samples analyzed in this work correspond to the tilt series TS21_1 (0 mM MgCl2) and TS22_1 (0.75 mM MgCl2).


### Fitting of MD simulation conformations to experimental cryo-ET maps
  To construct three-dimensional (3D) chromatin models at near-atomic resolution, we fitted the atomic coordinates of the mono-nucleosome structure (PDB: 1KX5) or tri-nucleosome models into each denoised cryo-ET density map (7 maps for chromatin array vitrified at 0 mM Mg2+, 13 maps vitrified at 0.75 mM Mg2+) using the fitmap tool in ChimeraX 1.7, which performs rigid-body local optimization86. The voxel size was set to 8.4 Å for the 0 mM Mg²⁺ map (EM magnification 42,000) and 5.51 Å for the 0.75 mM Mg²⁺ map (EM magnification 64,000). The parameter settings in the fitmap tool were the same as in the previous study12. Specifically, the fitmap tool was configured with a resolution of 16 Å, a cluster angle of 200°, and a cluster shift of 24 Å. Initial random placements of the structural model into the 3D density map were performed, and then the optimal orientation and position were searched and saved that maximized the cross-correlation between the model’s density and the experimental map.

  In detail, the atomistic model was converted into a density map by representing every atom by a scattering potential approximated by a Gaussian distribution. A global search comprising 1000 random initial placements was performed to explore the full map volume, followed by local rigid-body optimization to maximize the map-map correlation coefficient. Distinct fitting conformations were identified by clustering the resulting fits using angular and translational thresholds of 200° and 24 Å, respectively. Based on this protocol, we performed the fitting of tri-nucleosome conformations from the MD simulations to native chromatin cryo-ET maps. Due to the high computational cost of the fitting procedure (a large number of conformations, 40,000 frames per simulation × 3 runs × 4 tri-nucleosome systems), we selected 200 MD frames per run uniformly spaced at 20 ns intervals of tri-nucleosome system. This resulted in a total of 600 (MD frames) × 4 (systems) × 20 (cryo-ET maps) = 48,000 fitting operations, where each fitting operation corresponds to a single execution of the fitmap command on one conformation structure from the MD simulation and one cryo-ET map. The MD conformation with the highest goodness of fit correlation coefficient value was retained. ### Analysis of MD simulations
  For each MD simulation system, the initial 200 ns of each simulation trajectory were discarded as equilibration. The resulting trajectories were visualized using VMD 1.9.3 and analyzed with a set of VMD and GROMACS utilities. Histone-histone and histone-DNA contacts were defined as interactions between any two non-hydrogen atoms within 4.0 Å of each other. The radius of gyration of the DNA segments was calculated using the GROMACS tool gmx_gyrate81. The multi-dimensional free energy landscapes were computed using another GROMACS tool, gmx_sham, which estimates the Gibbs free energy by Boltzmann inversion of frequency histograms. The nucleosome center-center distances were used as collective variables. The interaction energies between the inter-nucleosomal histone tail and DNA were determined using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) method87. The salt concentration was set to 0.15 M, and the generalized Born model OBC2 was used with parameters igb = 5 and radii = mbondi2. The fast LCPO algorithm was applied to compute an analytical approximation to the solvent accessible area of the molecule (molsurf = 0). The calculations were performed based on every 1 ns frame.
