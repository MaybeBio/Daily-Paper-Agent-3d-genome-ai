# Berberine alleviates radiation-induced lung injury by promoting microbiota-derived inosine via the gut–lung axis


## Abstract
  Radiation-induced lung injury (RILI) is a common complication of radiotherapy. Although berberine (BBR) has been suggested to be associated with reduced RILI incidence, the underlying mechanisms remain unknown. Here, we investigated whether the gut microbiota mediates the radioprotective effects of BBR using a C57BL/6 RILI mouse model with 20 Gy thoracic irradiation (n = 6 per group). BBR (100 mg/kg) and inosine (INO, 300 mg/kg) were administered orally in vivo. Antibiotic depletion and fecal microbiota transplantation were performed to assess microbiota dependence. Lung injury was assessed by histology, pulmonary function, and cytokine levels. Gut microbiota was analyzed by 16S rRNA sequencing, and metabolites were profiled using LC-MS/MS. Transcriptomic and epigenomic alterations were assessed by RNA sequencing, ATAC sequencing, and CUT&Tag analysis. Molecular docking and surface plasmon resonance were used to assess metabolite–protein interactions. We demonstrated that BBR alleviated RILI in a microbiota-dependent manner. BBR increased Akkermansia muciniphila abundance and metabolite INO levels. Mechanistically, INO was associated with reduced neuron navigator 3 (NAV3) expression, accompanied by decreased chromatin accessibility and increased histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) at the NAV3 locus. Together, these findings reveal a gut microbiota–mediated mechanism underlying BBR-mediated protection against RILI, and suggest microbiota-informed biomarkers for risk stratification.


## Introduction
  Lung cancer is the most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer death [1]. Radiotherapy plays a key role in all stages of lung cancer and can be used as either definitive or palliative therapy [2]. However, the lung is highly sensitive to ionizing radiation and readily sustains injury [3]. Radiation-induced lung injury (RILI) is a common and serious complication characterized by acute pneumonitis and subsequent lung fibrosis. RILI, along with local recurrence and distant metastasis, poses significant challenges for the effective treatment of lung cancer. About one-third of patients receiving thoracic radiotherapy develop grade ≥ 2 radiation pneumonitis (RP) with dry cough, dyspnea, and low-grade fever [4]. Effective intervention during the acute inflammatory phase is essential for mitigating pulmonary fibrosis [5]. In clinical practice, management relies largely on glucocorticoids, which carry notable adverse effects and tolerability issues [6]. Therefore, there is a pressing need for more effective and safer therapeutic strategies.

  Emerging evidence implicates communication between the gut and the lung in respiratory diseases, including asthma, respiratory infections, pulmonary hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and lung cancer [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Disruption of the gut homeostasis may allow microbial translocation into the bloodstream, triggering a chronic inflammatory response that contributes to lung injury, pulmonary fibrosis, and carcinogenesis [[11], [12], [13]]. In addition, gut-derived metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and bile acids can circulate to distant organs and act in peripheral tissues [14,15]. Early studies suggest that the gut microbiota may modulate susceptibility to RILI, although the mechanistic links remain unclear [[16], [17], [18]].

  Berberine (BBR), a natural plant alkaloid extracted from Berberis aristata and Coptis chinensis, exhibits broad pharmacological activities in cancer, metabolic, and inflammatory diseases [19,20]. Despite very low oral bioavailability [21], BBR has demonstrated diverse pharmacological effects, increasingly attributed to modulation of the gut microbiota [20,22]. Several studies indicate that BBR can reshape microbial composition and metabolites.

  A clinical study has suggested that BBR may reduce the incidence of RILI during thoracic radiotherapy, but the underlying biology is not defined [23]. Whether its radioprotective effect is mediated through microbiota modulation has not been investigated. Here, we hypothesized that BBR may mitigate RILI through the gut–lung axis, and aimed to elucidate the role of microbiota-derived metabolites in this process.


## Results

Additionally, BBR pre-administration provided greater protection than BBR-post treatment (Figure S2A–S2C, Table S2).   As shown in Figure 1B, 20 Gy of thoracic irradiation induced significant body weight loss, which was alleviated by BBR treatment (Table S3). In addition, the lung coefficient, which primarily reflects the degree of pulmonary edema [24], was elevated following irradiation but attenuated by BBR (Figure 1C, Table S4). Histological analysis showed that irradiation led to alveolar septal thickening, inflammatory infiltration, and collagen deposition, whereas BBR mitigated these pathological changes (Figure 1D, Table S5). Consistently, assessment of pulmonary function further confirmed BBR-associated improvements (Figure 1E, Table S6). Moreover, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-2, IL-6, IL-17, IL-22, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), were markedly elevated in the IR group but suppressed by BBR treatment (Figure 1F, Table S7). Collectively, these findings indicate that BBR effectively alleviates RILI in mice.


### The Gut Microbiota Mediates the Protective Effects of BBR on RILI
  To explore the potential link between the gut microbiota and the protective effect of BBR on RILI, we first treated mice with a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail (ABX) to deplete intestinal commensal bacteria (Figure S3A). To confirm the effectiveness of microbiota depletion, 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequencing was performed. Rarefaction curves and alpha diversity indices, including Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson (Table S8), all showed markedly decreased richness and diversity in ABX-treated mice compared with controls (Figure S3B–S3C). Taxonomic profiling at the order level further confirmed the near-complete loss of dominant microbial populations in the ABX group (Figure S3D), validating the establishment of a microbiota-depleted model.

  Next, we evaluated the efficacy of BBR in microbiota-depleted mice by administering BBR following ABX treatment (Figure 2A). Notably, in the absence of gut microbiota, BBR failed to exert its protective effects, as evidenced by persistent weight loss (Figure 2B, Table S9), elevated lung coefficient (Figure 2C, Table S10), aggravated lung histopathology (Figure 2D, Table S11), impaired pulmonary function (Figure 2E, Table S12), and nonsignificant changes in inflammatory cytokines (Figure 2F, Table S13).   To further determine whether BBR-modulated gut microbiota plays a causal role or merely reflects secondary changes to BBR treatment, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from BBR-treated or saline-treated donor mice into recipient mice (Figure 2G). The transfer of the BBR-modulated microbiota partially reversed irradiation-induced body weight loss (Figure 2H, Table S14) and lung edema (Figure 2I, Table S15), and alleviated histopathological damage (Figure 2J, Table S16). Improvements in pulmonary function and reductions in several proinflammatory cytokines were also observed (Figure 2K–2L, Tables S17–S18). Together, these findings suggest that the gut microbiota modified by BBR may play a causal role in the alleviation of RILI.


### BBR Enriches the Gut Akkermansia muciniphila
  To better understand the impact of BBR on the gut microbiota in RILI, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed on fecal samples from control, IR, and IR + BBR groups. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) demonstrated distinct clustering of microbial communities across the three groups (Figure 3A–3B, Tables S19–S20), indicating significant compositional changes. In contrast, α diversity indices (Table S21), reflecting within-sample species richness and evenness [25], did not significantly differ among groups (Figure 3C). Bacteroidia and Verrucomicrobiae were restored by BBR treatment, whereas Bacilli and Deferribacteres were reduced following BBR administration (Figure 3D, Table S22).   To identify key bacterial taxa altered by BBR in RILI mice, we applied linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) and a random forest model. Both approaches consistently identified Akkermansia as the most enriched genus in the IR + BBR group (Figure 3E). A. muciniphila, the predominant species within this genus, is found in human feces and has been associated with multiple health benefits [26]. We further validated its abundance by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), which confirmed a decrease after irradiation and an increase following BBR treatment (Figure S4, Table S23). These findings indicate that BBR promotes the expansion of A. muciniphila in RILI mice.

  In addition, we also assessed the microbiota-modulating effects of BBR in non-irradiated mice (Figure S5A). 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that BBR administration, in the absence of irradiation, significantly altered the microbial composition (Figure S5B–S5C, Tables S24–S25). A reduction in α diversity was observed (Figure S5D, Table S26), likely due to the antibiotic-like properties of BBR. LEfSe and random forest analyses again consistently identified Akkermansia as the most enriched genus in the BBR-treated group (Figure S5E–S5F), and qPCR confirmed increased A. muciniphila abundance (Figure S5G, Table S27). Collectively, these findings further confirm that BBR promotes A. muciniphila enrichment.


### A. muciniphila-derived INO Ameliorates RILI
  Given the close relationship between gut microbiota and host metabolite profiles, we hypothesized that microbiota-derived metabolites might mediate the protective effects of BBR against RILI. To investigate this, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of fecal samples from control, IR, and IR + BBR mice using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed distinct metabolite profiles among the three groups, with the IR + BBR group clustering closer to the control group (Figure 4A). A total of 147 metabolites were significantly altered following irradiation, and 38 were modulated by BBR treatment (Figure 4B, Tables S28–S29). To elucidate the biological relevance of these significantly altered metabolites, we performed pathway enrichment analysis based on the small molecule pathway database (SMPDB), which revealed significant enrichment in purine metabolism (Figure 4C). Notably, among the 11 shared metabolites, inosine (INO), a key metabolite in this pathway, was markedly reduced in irradiated mice but restored following BBR administration (Figure 4D). Importantly, correlation analysis revealed a positive association between fecal INO levels and the abundance of Akkermansia (Figure 4E), suggesting a potential microbial origin. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that INO is a metabolite secreted by A. muciniphila [27].   In accordance with the gut–lung axis, gut microbiota-derived metabolites can be released into the circulation and exert their functions in peripheral tissues [15]. To investigate this, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of lung tissues from control, IR, and IR + BBR groups (Tables S30–S31). PLS-DA analysis revealed distinct clustering of metabolites across the three groups (Figure 4F). Notably, INO was significantly elevated in lung tissues following BBR treatment (Figure 4G, Table S32), supporting its distribution in lung tissues. Furthermore, serum INO levels were also increased in the IR + BBR group (Figure 4H, Table S33), suggesting that INO may exert biological effects on lung tissue via the gut–lung axis.

  To assess whether INO could protect against RILI in vivo, INO was administered to RILI mice (Figure 5A). Notably, INO treatment significantly alleviated body weight loss (Figure 5B, Table S34), reduced lung edema (Figure 5C, Table S35), improved histopathological damage (Figure 5D, Table S36), enhanced pulmonary function (Figure 5E, Table S37), and suppressed inflammatory cytokines in lung tissues (Figure 5F, Table S38). ### INO Regulated NAV3 Expression Through Modulating Chromatin Accessibility and H3K27me3 Modification

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are depicted in a volcano plot (Figure 6A). Compared with the control group, the IR group showed the upregulation of 1032 genes and the downregulation of 610 genes, whereas in the IR + INO group, 111 genes were upregulated and 361 were downregulated relative to those in the IR group (Tables S42–S43). The functional annotation and enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed several enriched pathways and Gene Ontology categories, including the p53-mediated DNA damage response and apoptosis signaling pathways (Figure 6B).   Growing evidence indicates that microbiota-derived metabolites regulate gene expression by modulating epigenetic modifications in specific genomic regions [28]. INO has been shown to modulate cell function by inducing changes in chromatin accessibility [29]. To assess whether the radioprotective effect of INO is related to epigenetic reprogramming, we further performed the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) on BEAS-2B cells. A heatmap of ATAC-seq data revealed significant differences in the expression patterns among the control, IR, and IR + INO groups (Figure 6C). Compared with those in the control group, 755 nucleosome-free regions presented changes in signals in the IR group, whereas 1362 alterations were observed in the IR + INO group compared with the IR group (Tables S44–S45). These data suggest that INO treatment alters chromatin accessibility under irradiation in BEAS-2B cells.

  Subsequently, cross-analysis of the RNA-seq data with ATAC-seq data revealed two genes whose expression was regulated by both irradiation and INO (Figure 6D). Previous studies have shown that neuron navigator 3 (NAV3) is involved in the DNA damage response and is upregulated in response to DNA damage [30]. Our RNA-seq data indicated that NAV3 expression was significantly elevated by IR and suppressed by INO treatment, which was also confirmed by ATAC-seq. Furthermore, qPCR and western blotting confirmed that irradiation-induced upregulation of NAV3 expression was inhibited by INO treatment (Figure 6E–6G, Tables S46–S47). These data suggest that INO may inhibit NAV3 expression by modulating chromatin accessibility.

  Histone modifications are well known to be involved in the regulation of gene transcription [31]. Trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me3) is crucial for gene repression [32]. Next, we investigated whether INO regulated gene expression by promoting H3K27 trimethylation. Western blot analysis revealed that H3K27me3 expression was increased following INO supplementation (Figure S6A–S6B, Table S48). Therefore, we next performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) assays for H3K27me3 to further investigate whether INO regulated gene expression through H3K27me3 modification. Interestingly, 151 DEGs identified by RNA-seq exhibited reduced chromatin accessibility in the IR + INO group compared with the IR group but increased accessibility in the IR group relative to the control group (Figure 6H). In contrast, these genes presented increased H3K27me3 signals in the IR + INO group and decreased signals in the IR group (Figure 6I), suggesting that INO is associated with the chromatin accessibility state and histone modification. Notably, ATAC-seq and CUT&Tag profiles were consistent with increased H3K27me3 and reduced chromatin accessibility at the NAV3 locus in association with lower NAV3 expression in the IR + INO group compared with the IR group (Figure 6J). These findings support a model in which increased H3K27me3 at the NAV3 locus contributes to reduced NAV3 expression.

  The removal of H3K27me3 marks is catalyzed by the lysine demethylase 6 (KDM6) family of histone demethylases, comprising KDM6A and KDM6B [33,34]. To explore whether the changes in H3K27me3 levels observed after INO treatment were associated with altered expression of demethylases, we assessed the mRNA levels of KDM6A and KDM6B. No significant differences were detected between the INO-treated and control groups (Figure S6C–S6D, Tables S49–S50), suggesting that INO might regulate H3K27me3 levels by affecting demethylase activity rather than transcription. To further test this hypothesis, we directly measured the demethylase activity of KDM6A/KDM6B in BEAS-2B cells and found that INO treatment significantly reduced their activity (Figure 6K, Table S51).

  Hence, we investigated whether INO inhibited demethylase enzyme activity by directly binding to KDM6A/KDM6B. First, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the direct binding between INO and KDM6A (PDB ID code 6FUL) or KDM6B (PDB ID code 4ASK). KDM6A and KDM6B are JmjC domain-containing enzymes [[35], [36], [37]]. The docking structure revealed that INO binds with high affinity to KDM6A and KDM6B, with Gibbs free energies of −8.3 and −7.6 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 7A–7B). Further analysis revealed that the binding sites were located primarily within their respective JmjC catalytic domains (residues 1095–1258 in KDM6A and residues 1339–1502 in KDM6B). These results suggest that INO may interfere with substrate recognition by occupying the catalytic pocket, thereby suppressing demethylase activity.Figure 7KDM6A and KDM6B are direct binding targets of INO(A–B) Molecular docking analysis of INO with KDM6A (A) and KDM6B (B). (C–D) Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensorgrams demonstrating direct binding of INO to KDM6A (C) and KDM6B (D) at the indicated concentrations. Abbreviations: KDM6A, lysine demethylase 6A; KDM6B, lysine demethylase 6B; INO, inosine; SPR, surface plasmon resonance.Figure 7

  To verify the interaction of INO with KDM6A and KDM6B determined by molecular docking, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays. INO displayed dose-dependent binding to both KDM6A and KDM6B, with dissociation constant (KD) values of 2.24 × 10−6 M and 4.65 × 10−6 M, respectively (Figures 7C–7D and S7). These data confirmed a specific interaction between INO and both demethylases. Together, these findings suggest that INO likely inhibits KDM6A and KDM6B by directly targeting their catalytic domains and interfering with their histone demethylation activity.


### Knockdown of NAV3 Alleviates RILI
  On the basis of the above findings, we speculated that the NAV3 gene may play a role in RILI. To this end, we generated a RILI mouse model with lung-specific NAV3 knockdown through intranasal (i.n.) administration of adeno-associated virus (AAV-lung) vectors carrying either NAV3-targeting short hairpin RNA (AAV-shNAV3) or a scrambled control (AAV-shNC) (Figure 8A–8B, Table S52). NAV3 knockdown mitigated irradiation-induced body weight loss (Figure 8C, Table S53) and the degree of lung edema (Figure 8D, Table S54). Histologically, NAV3 knockdown alleviated structural lung damage induced by irradiation (Figure 8E–8F, Table S55). Pulmonary function assessments revealed corresponding results (Figure 8G, Table S56). Furthermore, the mice in the IR group presented high levels of inflammatory cytokines in the lungs, whereas NAV3 knockdown reduced the IR-induced increase in cytokine levels (Figure 8H, Table S57). ### BBR and INO Do Not Protect Malignant Tissues Against Radiation
  To further assess the radioprotective effects of INO and BBR on malignant tissues, a lung cancer xenograft mouse model was established using the subcutaneous injection of A549 cells (Figure S8A–S8B). Mice with palpable subcutaneous tumors were irradiated with 8 Gy local radiation and subsequently treated with BBR or INO. While radiation significantly inhibited tumor growth compared with that in the model group, no significant differences in tumor growth were observed between the IR and IR + BBR groups or between the IR and IR + INO groups (Figure S8C–S8D, Tables S58–S59). These results suggest that neither BBR nor INO confers radioprotection in malignant tissues.


## Discussion
  RILI can occur following exposure to high doses of ionizing radiation following accidental or deliberate nuclear incidents [38], and it is a common and challenging complication of radiotherapy, which is administered to ∼60% of cancer patients [39,40]. Currently, no safe and effective drugs are available for its clinical management. The concept of the gut–lung axis has recently emerged, and increasing evidence supports a role for gut microbiota-derived metabolites in lung diseases. Here, we demonstrated the protective effect of BBR against RILI in mice and identified the regulation of the gut microbiota as a critical mediator.

  We found that pre-irradiation BBR treatment provided better protection than post-irradiation treatment, suggesting that its efficacy may be largely attributable to its impact on gut function. Prior studies have shown that pre- and post-IR interventions can exert distinct effects on microbiota composition [18], possibly due to early colonization of beneficial bacteria enhancing tissue resilience [41,42]. Consistent with previous studies [16], we observed that thoracic irradiation itself disrupted gut microbiota structure. Therefore, the modulatory effects of post-irradiation BBR treatment on the gut microbiota were likely influenced by IR-induced dysbiosis, leading to a diminished effect compared with pre-IR administration. Additionally, our finding that higher BBR doses provided enhanced protection further supports the pivotal role of the microbiota [20,22].

  The ability of the microbiota to mediate the treatment efficacy of compounds has been demonstrated previously. We found that BBR consistently increased the abundance of A. muciniphila in both RILI and healthy mice. Previous studies have also linked BBR’s therapeutic effects in diabetes, atherosclerosis, and alcoholic liver disease to A. muciniphila [[43], [44], [45]]. A. muciniphila is a mucin-degrading bacterium and the sole genus of the phylum Verrucomicrobia, which is found in human stool [46]. A. muciniphila is essential for maintaining gut barrier integrity and may activate immunosuppressive cell populations [44]. It has also been shown to alleviate radiation-induced damage by promoting intestinal regeneration [47], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic bacterium in radiation toxicity [46].

  Lung irradiation triggers DNA damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, leading to epithelial cell death and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [3,48]. These in turn recruit immune cells and propagate an inflammatory cascade, ultimately promoting tissue remodeling and fibrosis [3,48]. Studies have shown that the progression of pulmonary fibrosis is related to ongoing inflammation, which is related mainly to increased interleukins and tumor necrosis factor [18,49], and the inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 can help alleviate RILI [50,51]. In our study, BBR reduced levels of certain pro-inflammatory cytokines, indicating that its anti-fibrotic effects may be related to the reduced inflammation levels.

  INO, a bacterial metabolite derived from Bifidobacterium pseudolongum and A. muciniphila [27], emerged as a key mediator of BBR’s effects. As Bifidobacterium abundance was unchanged in our dataset, we propose that the enriched A. muciniphila was the primary source of INO. Prior studies have shown that INO protects against oxidative DNA damage [52], which is consistent with our results in RILI. Moreover, INO also exhibits protective effects in endotoxin shock [53], inflammatory lung injury [54], neurological disorders [55], and ischemic kidney disease [56], although its biological functions appear to be context-dependent [53,57]. INO has also been shown to promote T helper 1 (Th1) cell differentiation and enhance checkpoint blockade immunotherapy efficacy in tumors [27].

  NAV3, a member of the neuronal navigator family, is involved in protein degradation, signal transduction, and gene expression regulation [58]. Recent research has emphasized the role of NAV3 in the response to genotoxic stress induced by chemotherapy [30]. In addition, NAV3 has also been identified as a factor that drives myofibroblast function in kidney fibrosis [59,60]. Prior studies identified NAV3 as a transcriptional target of p73 [30], a member of the p53 family [61], further suggesting its involvement in stress-related signaling pathways.

  Gut microbiota-derived metabolites are known to regulate multiple epigenetic pathways, such as DNA methylation, miRNA, and histone modification [62,63]. However, the precise mechanisms and roles of the interplay between the gut microbiota and epigenetic mechanisms remain poorly understood. Smith et al. demonstrated that short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), metabolites derived from the gut microbiota, can enhance the immunosuppressive functions of regulatory T cells through their histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitory activity [64]. Zhang et al. found that, unlike SCFAs derived from intestinal microbes, L. plantarum-derived ILA might recruit pioneer transcription factors to mediate epigenetic changes instead of inhibiting the enzymatic activity of HDACs [28]. Our findings indicate an association between INO treatment, increased H3K27me3, and reduced NAV3 expression; however, causality is not established, and further experiments are required. Moreover, although molecular docking and SPR analysis suggested specific binding of INO to KDM6B, further loss-of-function studies with KDM6A/B-knockout cell models are needed to elucidate their role in the effect of INO.

  Interestingly, neither BBR nor INO impaired the radiosensitivity of lung cancer cells. This raises the possibility that oral BBR administration could protect normal tissue in gastrointestinal cancer patients receiving thoracic radiotherapy without compromising anti-tumor efficacy. Further investigation is needed to understand differential responses in normal versus malignant cells.

  Mouse models employing thoracic IR using various strains have been developed to model clinical outcomes of RILI in humans [65]. The C57BL/6J mouse strain is commonly applied to evaluate therapeutic agents to treat RILI [66]. Similar to RILI in humans, elevated inflammatory signaling in the mice leads to recruitment of activated neutrophils and macrophages into lung tissue following irradiation [3]. In this context, BBR represents a well-characterized natural compound with an established safety profile, and its oral administration supports its feasibility as a preventive or adjunctive strategy for patients undergoing thoracic radiotherapy. Moreover, the identification of microbiota-derived INO highlights candidate biomarkers that may aid in patient stratification and treatment monitoring. Further clinical studies are required to validate its safety, optimal dosing, and biomarker relevance in humans. Our study has several limitations. First, the absence of human samples limits clinical extrapolation. Second, although we observed an association between A. muciniphila enrichment and INO levels, and prior studies have reported INO to be secreted by A. muciniphila, we cannot exclude contributions from other members of the microbiota or host metabolism. Third, our study focused on the epithelium; the potential effects of INO on other cell types, such as immune cells, remain to be explored. Fourth, the specific biological function of NAV3 in RILI, beyond its consideration as a downstream target of INO, requires further mechanistic elucidation. Finally, whether INO affects three-dimensional (3D) chromatin structure remains unknown and could be investigated using high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C).

  In summary, our findings support a model in which BBR enriches gut A. muciniphila, leading to higher INO levels via the gut–lung axis. INO is associated with reduced KDM6A/B activity and increased H3K27me3, together with lower NAV3 expression. Overall, these findings provide mechanistic insight into a microbiota, metabolite, and epigenetic pathway in the context of RILI. These observations may guide future therapeutic exploration and support the evaluation of related readouts in risk assessment. In addition, systematic evaluation of candidate biomarkers, including microbial composition, circulating metabolites, and specific chromatin marks, could assist risk stratification in RILI.


## Materials and methods

### Cell Culture and Irradiation
  Human pulmonary epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) and murine pulmonary epithelial cells (MLE-12) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Virginia, USA). A549 cells were obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China). BEAS-2B cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; MeilunBio, Liaoning, China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, New York, USA). MLE-12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (MeilunBio, Liaoning, China), and A549 cells in 1640 medium (MeilunBio, Liaoning, China), both supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Irradiation was performed using a 60Co γ-ray irradiator (Beijing Institute of Radiation, Beijing, China). The control cells received sham irradiation.


### Bacteria
  A. muciniphila (BAA-835) was obtained from ATCC (Virginia, USA) and cultivated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C in brain-heart infusion (BHI) medium (Hope Bio, Shandong, China) supplemented with 5% type III mucin (Sigma, Missouri, USA) and 0.05% l-cysteine (Sigma, Missouri, USA). After incubation, the bacterial culture mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; MeilunBio, Liaoning, China) before gavage.


### Animal Experiments
  C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w) were purchased from Huafukang Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and BALB/c-nude female mice (4–5 w) were purchased from GemPharmatech Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). The mice were housed in a standard environment with a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle, temperatures ranging from 20 to 24 °C, and continuous access to a standard diet and water. Prior to the experiments, the mice were acclimated for one week and provided with sufficient food and water. The experimental unit was a single mouse in all in vivo experiments. For each experiment, mice were randomly allocated to the indicated groups using a computer-generated random number list. Mice were monitored daily, and predefined humane endpoints (e.g., > 20% body-weight loss, severe respiratory distress, or moribund state) were used as exclusion criteria. Group sizes were based on our previous experience with RILI and tumor xenograft models and published studies, and were considered sufficient to detect biologically meaningful differences while minimizing animal use. All animals that completed the experimental protocol were included in the final analysis, and no animals were excluded. To minimize potential confounders, cages from different groups were distributed across different shelves, and treatments and measurements were performed in a rotating order. Investigators performing histological scoring and data analysis were blinded to group allocation. No formal protocol registration was performed for this study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) guidelines and applicable local regulatory requirements and laws. The study was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (approval numbers IACUC-DWZX-2024-P588 and IACUC-DWZX-2025-507).

  The RILI mouse model was established as previously reported [67,68]. C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w) (total n = 18) were randomly assigned to three groups: sham-irradiated control (Control, n = 6), irradiated without treatment (IR, n = 6), and irradiated with 100 mg/kg berberine treatment (IR + BBR, n = 6). Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium prior to a single dose of 0 or 20 Gy of 60Co γ-ray thoracic irradiation. Apart from the thoracic region, other parts of the body were shielded with 10 cm-thick lead bricks. At 28 days post-irradiation, pulmonary function was assessed prior to euthanasia, after which mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and feces, blood, and lung tissues were harvested for subsequent analyses. The mice in the non-irradiated group underwent the same procedure but were not exposed to irradiation. Histopathological change, pulmonary function, and inflammatory cytokine levels were measured at the endpoint. Investigators performing histological scoring and data analysis were blinded to group allocation.

  For the BBR (MCE, New Jersey, USA) treatment, preliminary experiments were conducted to determine the optimal dose and administration strategy (BBR-pre or BBR-post). In the dose-comparison experiment (total n = 15), C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w) were randomly allocated to five groups (n = 3 per group): control, IR, IR + 30 mg/kg BBR, IR + 50 mg/kg BBR, and IR + 100 mg/kg BBR. In the schedule-comparison experiment (total n = 21), mice were randomly allocated to control (n = 3), IR (n = 6), IR + BBR-pre (n = 6), and IR + BBR-post (n = 6) groups. The BBR-pre regimen consisted of oral gavage with 100 mg/kg BBR for 7 days before IR, followed by saline post-IR, whereas the BBR-post regimen consisted of saline before IR and 100 mg/kg BBR administration for 10 days after IR. Following these evaluations, a regimen of 100 mg/kg BBR administered daily for 7 days prior to IR was selected for further investigation.

  For microbiota depletion, an antibiotic-induced microbiota-depleted mouse model was established according to a previously published protocol [69]. For the microbiota-depletion model validation, C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w, total n = 12) were randomly allocated to the following groups (n = 6 per group): control and antibiotic cocktail-treated groups. Mice received either saline (control) or the antibiotic cocktail (100 mg/kg vancomycin, 200 mg/kg neomycin sulfate, 200 mg/kg metronidazole, 200 mg/kg ampicillin; Aladdin, Shanghai, China) via daily oral gavage for 7 days. C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w, total n = 12) were randomly allocated to the following groups: antibiotic cocktail plus vehicle-treated control (IR + ABX, n = 6) and antibiotic cocktail plus BBR-treated group (IR + ABX + BBR, n = 6). Mice were treated with either saline or the antibiotic cocktail by oral gavage once a day for 7 days before 100 mg/kg BBR administration.

  For the FMT experiment, donor mice (6–8 w C57BL/6 male mice, total n = 12) were randomly assigned to two groups: saline-treated donors (control donors, n = 6) and berberine-treated donors (BBR donors, n = 6), and were treated with saline or 100 mg/kg BBR for 7 days. Feces from BBR- or saline-treated donor mice of the same group were pooled and resuspended in PBS (100 mg of feces in 1 mL of PBS). The mixture was vigorously vortexed for 3 min and allowed to settle for 2 min. The supernatant was then filtered with a 70 μm sterile filter membrane. Fresh fecal supernatant was prepared within 20 min before gavage to prevent changes in bacterial composition. Before the FMT intervention, recipient mice (6–8 w C57BL/6 male mice, total n = 12) were orally pretreated with ABX for 7 days to deplete the gut microbiota, and were then randomly assigned to the indicated FMT groups (saline-derived feces vs BBR-derived feces, n = 6 per group). At 72 h before gut flora reconstitution, ABX treatment was discontinued [70]. Then, the mice were gavaged with fresh supernatant (200 μL for each animal) for 7 days before irradiation.

  For the INO (MCE, New Jersey, USA) treatment, C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w, total n = 18) were randomly allocated to the following groups: control (n = 6), irradiation alone (IR, n = 6), and irradiation with INO treatment (IR + INO, n = 6). Mice received oral gavage of INO (300 mg/kg) once daily, starting one day before irradiation and continuing for 10 consecutive days post-irradiation [54].

  To evaluate the role of NAV3 in RILI in vivo, the model was established according to a published protocol [71]. C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 w) were randomly allocated to the following groups (total n = 18): sham-irradiated shNC (control, n = 6), irradiated shNC (IR + AAV-shNC, n = 6), and irradiated shNAV3 (IR + AAV-shNAV3, n = 6), and were intranasally administered AAV-shNAV3 or AAV-shNC (AAV-Lung serotype, 1 × 1011 vector genomes per mouse) 10 days prior to irradiation.

  For tumor models, 5 × 106 A549 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice (4–5 w). Once the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly allocated to the following groups (total n = 24): tumor sham-irradiated group (model, n = 6), tumor irradiated group (IR, n = 6), tumor irradiated with INO treatment group (IR + INO, n = 6), and tumor irradiated with BBR treatment group (IR + BBR, n = 6), and then subjected to 0 or 8 Gy of local irradiation followed by saline, INO (300 mg/kg), or BBR (100 mg/kg) by gavage for 10 days. The tumor volume was measured every 3 days using Vernier calipers and calculated by the following formula: V = a∗b2/2 (a, the longest diameter; b, the shortest diameter). At 28 days after irradiation, the tumor tissues were excised and weighed.


### qPCR Analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from cells using a Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiFiScript RT Kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instruction. qPCR was performed using SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix (CWBIO, Beijing, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, California, USA) according to the manufacturer's instruction. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference. For A. muciniphila abundance, DNA was extracted from feces using a fecal genomic DNA extraction kit (Solarbio, Hubei, China). 16S rRNA was used as the internal reference. Relative expression levels were calculated by the 2–ΔΔCT method with normalization to the internal reference gene. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S60.


### Western Blotting Analysis
  Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Mei5 Bio, Beijing, China) and phosphatase inhibitors (CWBIO, Beijing, China). Protein samples for γ-H2AX were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to nitrocellulose transfer membranes (Cytiva, Massachusetts, USA). Protein samples for NAV3 were separated on 4%–12% Bis-Tris gels (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γ-H2AX (Cat#GB111841, rabbit, 1:1000; Servicebio, Hubei, China), NAV3 (Cat#bs-12151R, rabbit, 1:1000; Bioss, Beijing, China), GAPDH (Cat#60004-1, mouse, 1:2000; Proteintech, Illinois, USA), H3 (Cat#bs-33042M, mouse, 1:2000; Bioss, Beijing, China), and H3K27me3 (Cat#ab192985, rabbit, 1:2000; Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK) in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Cat#A70828; ABCone, Shanghai, China). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat#ab205719, goat anti-mouse; Cat#ab205718, goat anti-rabbit; 1:10000; Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK) were subsequently applied for 1 h at room temperature, and enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) reagent was then used to visualize protein expression. The blots were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54p).


### Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining
  Mouse lung samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h. After fixation, the samples were dehydrated using a series of ethanol concentrations and embedded in paraffin. Then, 5 μm sections were heated at 60 °C for 2–3 h to ensure firm adhesion to the slides, dewaxed in xylene I and xylene II (10 min each), and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100% ethanol I, 100% ethanol II, 95% ethanol I, 95% ethanol II, 90%, 80%, and 70% ethanol; 5–10 min at each step) to distilled water. Slides were then stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water, differentiated briefly in 1% acid alcohol (1–3 s), and blued under running tap water for 3 min. After counterstaining with eosin for 3 min, sections were dehydrated through 95% ethanol I, 95% ethanol II, 100% ethanol I, and 100% ethanol II (1–2 min each), cleared in xylene I and xylene II again (10 min each), and mounted with neutral resin. Histopathological changes in the lungs were observed under a light microscope (CX40, Sunny Optical Technology, China). Histopathological changes in the lungs were assessed under an optical microscope. The severity of lung injury was scored using the Ashcroft scale as follows [72]: 0, normal; 1–2, mild alveolar septal thickening with fewer than 10 inflammatory cells per high-power field; 3–4, moderate thickening of the alveolar septum with 10–20 inflammatory cells; 5–6, marked alveolar septal thickening with extensive inflammatory cell infiltration; and 7–8, severe inflammation with full infiltration of the alveolar septum and pronounced alveolar collapse. The average score for each group was calculated.


### Masson’s Trichrome Staining
  Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. After dewaxing and rehydration, the sections were stained using a commercial Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.


### Pulmonary Function Assessment
  The mice were positioned within the body plethysmography chamber of the EMKA Small Animal Pulmonary Function Test System (EKMA TECHNOLOGIES, Paris, France) at 28 days post-irradiation, and pulmonary function was measured via noninvasive whole-body plethysmography [73,74]. Following an initial acclimatization period, pulmonary function parameters, including tidal volume, expiration time, and minute ventilation volume, were collected from conscious mice for 3 min per mouse.


### Cytokine Measurement
  Lung tissues were weighed and homogenized in cold PBS solution, and then centrifuged at 8000 g at 4 °C for 10 min to obtain a clear homogenate as the supernatant, which was used for further analysis. Cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-17, IL-22, and TNF-α) in the supernatant were then measured using the Mouse Magnetic Multiplex Assay Kit (Cat#IS135; Cloud-Clone, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, and blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then incubated with a γ-H2AX primary antibody (1:250; Servicebio, Beijing, China) overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the cells were incubated with a HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibody (Cat#ab150077, 1:400; Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (fluorescent mounting medium with DAPI; ZSGB Bio, Beijing, China), and images were captured using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).


### Colony Formation Assay
  600 BEAS-2B or 800 MLE-12 cells per well were plated in 6-well plates and exposed to 0, 4, or 8 Gy of irradiation with or without treatment. The medium containing the drug was then immediately replaced with fresh medium, and the cells were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 7–10 days to form colonies. The colonies were fixed with anhydrous methanol for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 10 min, rinsed with PBS, and then counted.


### Cell Viability Assay
  Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Cat# 40203ES80; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). 3000 BEAS-2B or 5000 MLE-12 cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. At 48 h after irradiation, the medium in each well was replaced with medium supplemented with 10% CCK-8 solution, and the cells were incubated for 1–2 h at 37 °C. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the optical density (OD) of each well at 450 nm using a microplate reader (MB-580, Huisong Technology, Shanghai, China).


### Measurement of INO
  The levels of INO were determined using an inosine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Hengyuan Bio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The protein concentrations of the lung homogenate supernatant and fecal supernatant were measured using the BCA assay, and the levels of INO were normalized to the total protein concentration of the sample.


### Molecular Docking
  The 3D structure of inosine (CAS: 58-63-9) was built using Chem3D (version 20.0) and energy-minimized with the MM2 force field [75]. The optimized conformation was exported in PDB format for molecular docking. The crystal structures of KDM6A (PDB ID: 6FUL) and KDM6B (PDB ID: 4ASK) were selected for docking on the basis of resolution and cofactor availability [76]. Cocrystallized ligands and water molecules were removed. Protein and ligand structures were prepared using AutoDock tools (version 1.5.7) and converted to PDBQT format, and docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2) with a 40 Å3 search box centered on the catalytic domain [77]. Binding interactions were analyzed using PyMOL (version 2.5.4, https://www.pymol.org/) and Discovery Studio (version 2023, https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download) to visualize key residues and interaction modes.


### SPR Analysis
  The interaction between KDM6A/KDM6B and INO was verified by SPR using a BIAcore S200 system (Cytiva, Massachusetts, USA). A CM5 sensor chip was employed to activate the sensor surface. The target protein KDM6B was diluted to 30 μg/mL in 10 mM acetate, while the target protein KDM6A was diluted to 50 μg/mL. Both were immobilized on the CM5 chip surface at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. The reaction was halted with 1 M ethanolamine. Various concentrations of INO were introduced through double gradient dilution, and the equilibrium dissociation constants for the interactions were determined using BIAcore S200.


### 16S rRNA Sequencing
  Fresh fecal samples were collected, rapidly frozen, and stored at –80 °C until analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (Cat#DP328; TianGen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′) and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, California, USA) with paired-end 250 bp reads. The raw data were processed using QIIME 2 (version QIIME2-202202), including quality filtering, denoising, and chimera removal using the DADA2 pipeline (version 1.16), to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [28,78]. Taxonomic classification was performed with the Silva v138.1 database [79,80]. Downstream analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.2). PCoA and NMDS were performed to assess sample-species complexity (β diversity), with the use of the R package ade4 and vegan. Differentially abundant genera were identified by LEfSe with the R package microeco, and by the random forest model with the R package RandomForest.


### LC-MS/MS Analysis
  Fecal (100 mg) and lung tissue (100 mg) samples were collected, ground in liquid nitrogen, and extracted in ice-cold 80% methanol. After vortexing and incubation on ice for 5 min, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were diluted to 53% methanol with LC-MS-grade water, centrifuged again, and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis [81]. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) coupled to an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer, using a Hypersil Gold column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) with a 12-min linear gradient at 0.2 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water and methanol. The instrument was operated in both positive and negative ion modes with standard source settings. The raw data were processed with Compound Discoverer 3.3 for peak detection, alignment, and quantification. Metabolites were annotated using the mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList databases. Downstream analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.2). PLS-DA was performed using the R package mixOmics. VIP values were extracted from the orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA) results, which were generated using the R package MetaboAnalystR. Volcano, heatmap, and Venn plots were conducted using R packages ggrepel, pheatmap, and ggVennDiagram, respectively.


### RNA-seq
  Total RNA was extracted from BEAS-2B cells using TRIzol (Cat#15596018; Invitrogen, California, USA), and RNA-seq libraries were prepared with the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Cat#NR605; Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturers’ protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with Trim Galore (version 0.6.10). The reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values. Subsequent data analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), with some shown above. Differentially expressed genes were then identified by the R package DESeq2. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed on DEGs using the R package ClusterProfiler.


### Assay for ATAC-seq
  ATAC-seq libraries were prepared from 5 × 104 BEAS-2B cells using the TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 (Cat#TD501; Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were amplified with the TruePrep Index Kit V2 (Cat#TD202; Vazyme, Jiangsu, China), purified with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Cat#N411; Vazyme, Jiangsu, China), and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. The raw data were processed through a standard pipeline, including quality assessment with fastp (version 0.11.9), adapter and low-quality read trimming with Trim Galore (version 0.6.10), alignment to the hg38 reference genome, and sorting and duplicate removal with samtools (version 1.3.1). Peaks were identified with MACS2 (version 2.1.1.20160309) and annotated using Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment (HOMER) (version 4.1.5). A heatmap and profile of peak coverage were generated with DeepTools (version 3.5.2). Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) (version 2.17.4) was used to visualize the BW files. Other downstream statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) with packages shown above.


### CUT&Tag Assay
  CUT&Tag sequencing was performed on 5 × 104 BEAS-2B cells using the CUT&Tag Assay Kit (pAG-Tn5) for Illumina (Cat#RK20265; ABclonal, Hubei, China) and the Dual DNA Adapter 96 Kit (Cat#RK20290; ABclonal, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the cells were incubated with ConA beads and a mouse anti-H3K27me3 antibody (Cat#6002, 1:2000; Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK) overnight at 4 °C, and then with a unconjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Cat#ab46540; Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK). Tagmentation was carried out at 37 °C for 1 h using the pAG-Tn5 transposome. DNA was extracted, amplified, and purified for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. The reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome. Data processing followed a pipeline similar to that of ATAC-seq, as described above.


### Measurement of KDM6A/KDM6B Enzyme Activity
  The total nucleoprotein of BEAS-2B cells was extracted (Cat#P0027; Beyotime, Beijing, China) with or without treatment with INO. The demethylase activity of KDM6A/KDM6B was measured with a JMJD3/UTX Demethylase Assay Kit (Cat#P-3084; EpigenTek, New York, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The demethylation products are detected by specific antibodies, and the signal intensity, proportional to the enzyme activity, is measured using a microplate reader (Huisong Technology, Shanghai, China) at 450 nm using a standard curve. KDM6A/KDM6B activity was calculated as the amount of demethylated product (ng) divided by the incubation time (min) and input nuclear extract (mg).


### Statistical Analysis
  The data in this study are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was evaluated using Student’s t-test for two groups and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The assumptions of the statistical tests were checked, and when these assumptions were not met, appropriate data transformation or non-parametric tests were applied. Unless otherwise specified, all data analyses and plots were carried out using R (version 4.2.2). Results with P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical results are provided in Table S61.
