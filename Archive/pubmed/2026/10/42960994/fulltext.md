# Cross-task benchmarking reveals variable transferability of deep learning models across molecular quantitative trait loci


## Abstract
  Most genetic variants associated with complex traits reside in non-coding regions of the genome, complicating their functional interpretation. Recent deep learning models have advanced the prediction of expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), but whether these models generalize across diverse molecular phenotypes is unknown. Here, we assess the cross-task transferability of pretrained regulatory features from six representative sequence- and annotation-based models across nine classes of molecular quantitative trait loci (xQTLs). We observe that while all models perform well in eQTL prediction, their performance varies substantially across other xQTL predictions. Chromatin accessibility, transcription factor binding, and histone QTLs are generally well predicted, whereas chromatin looping and microRNA expression QTLs remain challenging. Foundation models, particularly AlphaGenome and Borzoi, consistently outperform models based on local sequence context and exhibit improved robustness across class-imbalance settings. Tree-based ensemble classifiers further enhance predictive performance, suggesting that nonlinear interactions among regulatory features contribute substantially to variant prioritization. Applying AlphaGenome and Borzoi to coronary artery disease-associated genome-wide association studies loci identifies 337 high-confidence candidate chromatin accessibility QTLs, including 11 previously reported and 326 candidates that display significantly larger allele-specific differences in predicted chromatin accessibility signals. Collectively, our results reveal substantial variability in the cross-task performance of current eQTL-centric models, highlight the advantages of long-range genomic representations and inform the prioritization of candidate regulatory variants contributing to complex diseases.


## Introduction
  With the rapid advancement of high-throughput genotyping and sequencing technologies, the amount of publicly available genetic variation data has increased exponentially [1, 2]. These technological developments have paved the way for large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which have successfully identified numerous genetic variants significantly associated with complex traits and diseases [3]. However, >90% of these variants reside in non-coding regions of the genome, far from protein-coding sequences, making their biological functions difficult to interpret using conventional protein-based annotation strategies [4, 5]. This distribution highlights the central role of gene regulation—the precise spatial and temporal control of gene expression—in shaping phenotypic variation and disease susceptibility.

  Accumulating evidence indicates that these non-coding regions frequently harbor functional regulatory elements and are significantly enriched for multiple molecular quantitative trait loci (xQTLs) [1, 6–12]. These variations affect diverse aspects of gene regulation—transcription, splicing, methylation, translation, and polyadenylation—and play a central role in shaping phenotypic diversity across complex traits [12]. As such, deciphering the functional consequences of non-coding variants is critical for translating GWAS signals into mechanistic insights.

  Experimental identification of xQTLs, however, remains technically and practically challenging, as it typically requires large cohort sizes, tissue- or cell-type-specific molecular profiling, and extensive high-throughput functional assays, all of which are costly and labor-intensive [13, 14]. Moreover, many regulatory effects are highly context-dependent, further complicating systematic experimental characterization across diverse biological conditions [15]. In recent years, machine learning, particularly deep learning, has become a powerful approach for modeling the regulatory effects of non-coding variants [16]. Early methods, such as Genome Wide Annotation of VAriants (GWAVA), relied on curated functional annotations to prioritize likely functional variants, but their performance is constrained by annotation availability [17]. Moreover, they do not directly learn from DNA sequences, limiting their ability to capture context-dependent and long-range regulatory effects encoded in genomic sequences. Subsequent sequence-based models, including DeepSEA and ExPecto, enabled direct learning from raw DNA sequences to predict diverse regulatory activities, facilitating the interpretation of eQTLs [18, 19]. However, most of these approaches are limited to short sequence contexts, restricting their ability to capture long-range regulatory interactions that are critical for many xQTL mechanisms. To address this limitation, genomic foundation models have recently been developed to integrate extended genomic context. By modeling long-range dependencies in a self-supervised manner, these approaches provide a unified framework for capturing both local sequence determinants and distal regulatory interactions across diverse cellular contexts [20–25].

  Despite rapid methodological advances, significant limitations remain in the computational prediction of molecular quantitative trait loci. The computational efforts have remained disproportionately focused on predicting eQTLs and have shown limited generalizability to other regulatory phenotypes [10, 11, 18, 19, 21, 22]. While eQTLs are crucial, this narrow focus has come at the expense of developing robust predictors for other equally important regulatory variants. This limited focus restricts the understanding of the broader regulatory architecture of the genome [26–29].

  In this study, we systematically evaluate the cross-task generalizability of widely used eQTL prediction models across a diverse spectrum of xQTLs. By comparing representative prediction frameworks across multiple regulatory phenotypes, we characterize the transferability of eQTL-centric models to other regulatory variants and highlight potential factors that influence their transferability. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to independent disease-associated variant datasets, demonstrating that genomic foundation model-based predictions can prioritize candidate regulatory variants. Our results reveal fundamental limitations of current approaches and provide a conceptual foundation for developing unified models to interpret the effects of non-coding variants across regulatory mechanisms.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection and processing
  We collected a comprehensive set of nine types of xQTLs from GTEx (version 10) and previous publications to conduct subsequent analysis [1, 30–33]. Specifically, the datasets included alternative polyadenylation QTL (apaQTL) in whole blood, transcription factor binding QTL (bQTL) in human coronary artery smooth muscle cells (HCASMC) [30], chromatin accessibility QTL (caQTL) in HCASMCs [30], chromosomal looping QTL (clQTL) in HCASMCs [30], gene expression QTL (eQTL) in whole blood, histone QTL (hQTL) in lymphoblastoid cell lines [31], microRNA expression QTL (miQTL) in whole blood [32], protein expression QTL (pQTL) in whole blood [33], and mRNA splicing QTL (sQTL) in whole blood. For each xQTL dataset, negative control variants were randomly selected from the 1000 Genomes Project reference panel at a 1:10 ratio relative to the number of significant xQTLs [34]. Detailed information regarding xQTLs is summarized in Table S1. Using the same approach, additional control sets were constructed at 1:5 and 1:1 ratios to further evaluate the impact of class imbalance on model performance. The sampling was performed independently for each xQTL dataset, and the resulting control variants were used consistently across the evaluated approaches. The minor allele frequency (MAF) was calculated based on the 1000 Genomes Project data [34], and only variants with MAF ≥ 0.01 were retained for subsequent analyses. In addition, a more stringently matched negative-control set was constructed for sensitivity analysis. Candidate control variants were first restricted to those located at least 500 kb from the xQTLs to minimize the influence of linkage disequilibrium, and all known xQTLs were excluded. For each xQTL variant, a control variant was then selected by matching MAF (difference < 0.05) and genomic context annotation (e.g. promoter, untranslated region (UTR), exon, intron, and intergenic regions).

  To identify novel disease-associated xQTLs, we additionally obtained 2492 coronary artery disorder-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (CAD-SNPs) located on the autosomes from the GWAS Catalog under the trait terms ‘coronary artery disorder’, including data from child traits (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/efotraits/MONDO_0005090).


### Framework for cross-xQTL prediction
  We selected six representative computational approaches for regulatory variant effect prediction to assess their performance in cross-xQTL functional variant prediction tasks, including GWAVA [17], DeepSEA [18], ExPecto [19], Enformer [21], Borzoi [20], and AlphaGenome [25]. Among these methods, GWAVA was specifically designed for eQTL prediction, whereas DeepSEA, ExPecto, Enformer, and Borzoi were primarily developed for predicting epigenomic features and are commonly applied to eQTL prediction tasks. AlphaGenome, similarly, focuses on epigenomic feature prediction but has been extended to a broader range of xQTL analyses, including apaQTL, caQTL, bQTL, eQTL, and sQTL prediction. A statistical summary of the variant-level features generated by each model is provided in Table S2.

  To ensure fair and consistent comparison across models with distinct models and feature representations, all benchmarking analyses were conducted using a unified preprocessing and evaluation pipeline (Fig. 1). For each xQTL dataset, chromosome-based partitioning was applied to generate non-overlapping training and testing datasets. The test chromosomes were randomly selected for each xQTL dataset to retain ~20% of variants for independent evaluation (Table S1). The same train-test partition was then applied to all six models within each xQTL dataset. For each model, we extracted variant-level chromatin features and used them to train XGBoost classifiers for xQTL prediction. Within the training set, hyperparameter optimization was performed using 5-fold cross-validation. Random Forest (RF), L1 regularization, and Elastic Net (Enet) logistic regression were additionally evaluated to assess the impact of classifier selection on predictive performance, using a chromosome-based partitioning strategy in which independent chromosomes were reserved exclusively for model evaluation for each xQTL category (Table S1). All models were trained using a fixed random seed (seed = 0).


### Model performance evaluation
  To systematically assess the predictive performance of the models, we first computed the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) and the Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUPRC). These metrics provide a comprehensive measure of a model’s discriminative ability, independent of any decision threshold.

  For threshold-dependent evaluation, continuous scores were binarized using classification thresholds. Variants with scores greater than or equal to the threshold were classified as functional, whereas variants with scores below the threshold were considered non-functional. ### Analysis of feature importance
  We assessed the contribution of each chromatin feature to xQTL prediction using model-derived feature importance scores. Hyperparameter optimization was performed within the training set using 5-fold cross-validation, and the optimized parameters were subsequently used to train the final model on the complete training partition. For XGBoost models, feature importance was quantified using gain values from the optimized final model, and features were ranked according to their relative contributions to prediction performance.

  To further interpret the biological relevance of predictive features, each feature was mapped to its corresponding tissue or cell type using a feature-to-tissue annotation file. Features were ranked by their average importance scores, and the top 10% were designated as highly important features. For each tissue, we calculated the observed fraction of highly important features annotated to that tissue. To assess statistical significance, we performed 10 000 permutation tests by repeatedly sampling the same number of features from the non-top 10% set and computing the fraction of sampled features in each tissue. Empirical P-values were calculated as the proportion of permutations in which the sampled fraction exceeded the observed fraction, and false discovery rates (FDRs) were computed to correct for multiple testing.


### Enrichment analysis
  To further assess the biological relevance of the predicted CAD-associated caQTLs, enrichment analyses were performed using g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/) [35]. The CAD-associated caQTLs variants were annotated using the GENCODE v38 (Ensembl release 104) gene annotation [36] based on their genomic coordinates. The resulting annotated genes were subsequently used for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Human Phenotype Ontology (HP) enrichment analyses. Multiple testing was controlled using the false discovery rate (FDR), and terms with FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.


## Results

### Comparative evaluation of model prediction performance for eQTL identification
  We first evaluated the prediction performance of six models on the eQTL dataset (Fig. 2 and Table 1). The dataset was constructed using 14,811 whole-blood expression quantitative trait loci identified in the GTEx project [1], representing a widely used and well-characterized reference resource for studying genetic regulation of gene expression. To better reflect a realistic positive-to-negative ratio, non-eQTL variants were sampled at a 10:1 ratio relative to positive eQTLs, resulting in a deliberately imbalanced class distribution (Table S1). Among these models, AlphaGenome achieved the best overall performance (AUROC = 0.810, AUPRC = 0.389, F1 = 0.343, MCC = 0.292), and also exhibited the lowest Brier score (Fig. 2A and B and Table 1). Borzoi and Enformer also demonstrated strong predictive performance, achieving AUROC values of 0.804 and 0.799, respectively (Fig. 2A). Notably, Borzoi achieved a higher AUPRC than Enformer (Fig. 2B), suggesting enhanced sensitivity for identifying functional regulatory variants associated with transcriptional activity. In contrast, models based on local context showed comparatively weaker performance, with ExPecto and DeepSEA achieving AUROC values of 0.724 and 0.688, respectively (Fig. 2A and B). Although GWAVA relies solely on functional annotations, it outperformed DeepSEA and ExPecto (AUROC = 0.775, AUPRC = 0.319) (Fig. 2A and B).

  Model bias regarding allele frequency was further evaluated by comparing the MAF distributions of predicted eQTLs with those of all analyzed variants. Across all models, the MAF distributions of predicted eQTLs were highly consistent with the background variant set, indicating that none of the models exhibited systematic bias toward either common or rare variants in eQTL prediction (Fig. 2C). Although xQTL prediction was formulated as a binary classification task, significant positive correlations were observed between predicted probabilities and absolute effect sizes across all models (Fig. 2F), indicating that higher predicted probabilities were generally associated with variants exerting larger regulatory effects on gene expression.

  To interpret the biological relevance of predictive features, we further performed chromatin feature enrichment analysis based on model-derived feature importance scores. The most important features derived from AlphaGenome and Borzoi were predominantly enriched in RNA-seq signals, while Enformer demonstrated strong enrichment for CAGE-derived transcriptional activity signals (Fig. 2D and Fig. S1). The strong enrichment of RNA-associated signals in AlphaGenome and Borzoi is consistent with their superior performance in eQTL prediction, suggesting that direct modeling of transcriptional activity may improve the identification of functional regulatory variants influencing gene expression. In addition, DeepSEA showed enrichment for RNA polymerase II (Pol2)-associated chromatin features, whereas ExPecto exhibited enrichment for H3K36me3 and Pol2-related signals (Fig. S1). At the tissue level, the most important features in DeepSEA, ExPecto, and Enformer were enriched in blood-related tissues, consistent with the cellular origin of the eQTL dataset used in this study (Fig. 2E and Fig. S2). In contrast, AlphaGenome and Borzoi exhibited broader tissue enrichment patterns, with multiple tissues showing comparable enrichment levels rather than strong enrichment in a single dominant tissue category (Fig. S2). Collectively, the concordance between enriched feature categories and downstream xQTL performance indicates that model transferability is strongly influenced by the extent to which pretrained regulatory representations align with the molecular phenotype being predicted.


### Cross-xQTL prediction performance
  To evaluate whether representative eQTL prediction frameworks can generalize to other regulatory variant types, all models were then used to predict multiple xQTL types, including apaQTL, bQTL, caQTL, clQTL, hQTL, miQTL, pQTL, and sQTL (Fig. 3, Fig. S3, and Table 1). Overall, predictive performance varied substantially across xQTL types, while consistent performance hierarchies were observed across models. Among all models, Borzoi and AlphaGenome showed the most stable performance across datasets, frequently achieving the best prediction performance across ROC, F1, MCC, and Brier scores (Fig. 3A, Fig. S3, and Table 1).

  Performance was highest for caQTLs, bQTLs, and hQTLs, where all models achieved markedly high discrimination. In caQTL prediction, AlphaGenome achieved the best overall performance (AUROC = 0.954, AUPRC = 0.757), followed closely by Borzoi and GWAVA, indicating strong detectability of chromatin accessibility-associated regulatory variants (Fig. 3A and Fig. S3). Notably, we observed a bias toward classifying variants with higher MAF as caQTLs across all models (Fig. S4). Furthermore, feature enrichment analysis revealed that all models showed strong enrichment for DNase-associated chromatin accessibility signals (Fig. S5). Similarly, for bQTLs, AlphaGenome and Borzoi consistently performed best, achieving both high discriminative power and well-calibrated probability estimates (Fig. 3A, Fig. S3, and Table 1). Compared with bQTL prediction, hQTL prediction was more strongly influenced by MAF, as reflected by differences in the consistency between MAF distributions of predicted xQTLs and all tested variants (Fig. S4). Specifically, DeepSEA showed the highest consistency, followed by ExPecto and Enformer, whereas GWAVA showed the lowest concordance despite achieving the best predictive performance (Fig. 3A and Fig. S4).

  Intermediate performance was observed for apaQTLs, pQTLs, and sQTLs, where foundation models maintained a consistent advantage over local-context and annotation-based models. In pQTL prediction, Borzoi achieved the highest AUROC (0.794) and AUPRC (0.442), while Enformer also showed competitive performance (Fig. 3A and Fig. S3). For apaQTLs and sQTLs, AlphaGenome, Borzoi, and Enformer exhibited broadly comparable performance (Fig. 3A and Fig. S3). In addition, all models showed minimal bias toward variants with different MAF, indicating that predictive performance was not driven by allele frequency effects (Fig. 3B and C). Moreover, significant positive correlations were observed between predicted probabilities and absolute effect sizes for both apaQTL and sQTL predictions (Fig. 3B and C), suggesting that model outputs capture biologically meaningful effect magnitude information. For sQTL prediction, feature enrichment analysis revealed that only AlphaGenome-derived important features were enriched for splice junction-related signals (Fig. S6), which may partially explain its superior prediction performance.

  In contrast, prediction of clQTLs and miQTLs represented the most challenging tasks, with substantially reduced performance across all models (generally AUROC <0.71 and low MCC values) (Fig. 3A, Fig. S3, and Table 1), highlighting the difficulty of capturing the underlying regulatory mechanisms using current chromatin features. Despite this, AlphaGenome remained among the top-performing models. Except for GWAVA, all other models exhibited only minor prediction bias toward variants with different MAF in clQTL and miQTL prediction (Fig. S4).

  To further assess the robustness of our findings, we performed three complementary sensitivity analyses: First, to evaluate the potential influence of negative-control selection, we repeated the analyses using a more stringently matched control set. Control variants were required to be at least 500 kb away from the corresponding xQTL variants, known xQTLs were excluded, and controls were matched by MAF and regulatory element annotation. The sensitivity analysis largely recapitulated the overall performance trends observed in the primary analysis, with consistently higher performance for caQTL and lower performance for clQTL and miQTL across most models, indicating that our main conclusions were not substantially affected by the negative-control selection strategy (Fig. S7). Second, to evaluate the potential influence of random initialization, we repeated the analyses using two additional random seeds (seed = 42 and seed = 68), in addition to the original seed = 0. The performance metrics across the three runs were summarized as the mean and standard deviation (SD). The results showed highly consistent performance across random seeds, with standard deviations ranging from 0.00012 to 0.00735 for AUROC and from 0.00004 to 0.01106 for AUPRC across all models and evaluation settings (Table S3), indicating that the observed performance was robust to random initialization. Third, to assess the potential influence of chromosome composition on cross-task comparisons, we performed an additional analysis using the same test chromosomes (chromosomes 2, 12, 14, 19, and 21) across all nine xQTL categories and six approaches. These chromosomes were selected to retain ~20% of variants in the test set for each xQTL dataset where possible. The results were largely consistent with those obtained using the original xQTL-specific chromosome partitions, with only minor differences observed between the two results (Table S4), indicating that the main conclusions were not substantially affected by the choice of test chromosomes. Fourth, the fixed classification threshold may affect threshold-dependent metrics under class-imbalanced conditions. To assess the potential effect, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which the optimal threshold was determined based on MCC using the training data and subsequently applied to the test data. As expected, the optimized thresholds led to changes in threshold-dependent metrics such as F1 and MCC, whereas AUROC and AUPRC remained largely unchanged. Importantly, the overall performance patterns and main conclusions were consistent with those obtained using the fixed threshold of 0.5 (Table S5). These sensitivity analyses demonstrate that our main findings are robust to random initialization, chromosome partitioning, and classification threshold selection.

  Collectively, these results reveal a gradient of cross-phenotype predictability, where regulatory phenotypes directly linked to chromatin activity are substantially more learnable from current genomic representations than higher-order or post-transcriptional regulatory traits.


### Factors influencing cross-xQTL prediction performance
  The extent to which predictive performance was influenced by the choice of downstream classifier and dataset composition was systematically evaluated. Firstly, we evaluated the impact of the classifier choice on model performance in terms of discriminative ability and robustness across xQTLs. A pronounced performance gap was observed between tree-based and linear classifiers across all xQTL categories. In particular, XGBoost achieved the highest discriminative performance for caQTL prediction (AUROC = 0.894–0.954, AUPRC = 0.652–0.757), with random forest (RF) showing comparable but slightly reduced performance (AUROC = 0.887–0.946, AUPRC = 0.598–0.733) (Fig. 4 and Fig. S8). Similar trends were consistently observed across other regulatory QTL types, indicating that ensemble tree-based methods provide a stable advantage in ranking causal variants across diverse regulatory contexts (Fig. 4 and Fig. S8). In contrast, linear models exhibited substantially reduced predictive performance, with L1-regularized models frequently yielding AUROC values close to 0.5 and MCC values near zero for multiple xQTL prediction tasks (Fig. 4, Fig. S8, and Table S6). This performance deterioration was particularly evident when using features derived from DeepSEA, ExPecto, and Enformer, suggesting that the regulatory signals these models capture are highly nonlinear and cannot be effectively modeled with sparse linear decision boundaries. Notably, RF occasionally achieved superior lower Brier scores for most xQTLs except eQTL and pQTL prediction (Table S6), indicating greater stability of predicted probabilities despite slightly reduced ranking-based performance compared with XGBoost. However, XGBoost maintained a more consistent balance between discrimination across all xQTLs.

  To further evaluate the robustness of the models under different class imbalances, we compared model performance across different control-to-case ratios (1:1, 1:5, and 1:10). Overall, AUROC remained relatively stable across imbalance settings, whereas AUPRC showed substantial declines as the proportion of control variants increased (Fig. 5 and Fig. S9). In contrast, well-predicted xQTLs, including caQTLs, hQTLs, and bQTLs, maintained comparatively high discriminative performance even under severe imbalance, although AUPRC still exhibited a gradual decline (Fig. 5). Notably, for AlphaGenome-based caQTL prediction, AUROC remained above 0.95 across all ratios, despite a pronounced reduction in AUPRC from 0.954 (1:1) to 0.757 (1:10) (Fig. 5 and Fig. S9).

  Despite the overall decline in performance under imbalance, AlphaGenome and Borzoi consistently demonstrated greater robustness compared with local-context models such as DeepSEA and ExPecto (Fig. 5, Fig. S9, and Table S7). These foundation models maintained higher AUROC and MCC across multiple xQTLs and imbalance ratios (Fig. S9 and Table S7). Notably, AlphaGenome generally achieved the best or near-best overall performance for several xQTLs (Fig. 5, Fig. S9, and Table S7), indicating improved generalizability under increasingly realistic negative sampling scenarios. These results suggest that long-range sequence representations capture more stable and transferable regulatory features than local-context models.

  In summary, these results demonstrate that model choice has a substantial impact on xQTL prediction, with tree-based ensemble methods (particularly XGBoost) providing the most robust and generalizable performance across diverse xQTLs. Besides, increasing the proportion of control variants consistently reduced AUPRC, F1 score, and MCC across nearly all xQTLs, whereas AUROC remained comparatively stable.


### Identification of novel CAD-associated caQTLs
  Given the strong performance of AlphaGenome and Borzoi in caQTL prediction, we next evaluated whether these models could prioritize disease-associated regulatory variants from an independent set of CAD-associated variants downloaded from GWAS datasets (Fig. 6A). Of these, 9 variants overlapped with the positive caQTL samples and 15 overlapped with the negative control samples used for caQTL model training. Borzoi predicted 503 variants as candidate caQTLs, whereas AlphaGenome identified 422 candidate caQTLs (Fig. 6B). To assess the consistency between the two models, we further compared their prediction scores and binary classifications. The AlphaGenome and Borzoi scores showed a strong positive correlation, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.807 and a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.746 (both P < 2 × 10−16; Fig. S10). The two models showed 89.92% overall concordance in their binary classifications, with a Cohen’s κ of 0.6673, indicating substantial agreement. To further increase the stringency of candidate prioritization, we therefore focused on variants jointly predicted as caQTLs by both models. Using this consensus-based strategy, we identified 337 potential CAD-associated caQTLs, including 11 previously reported CAD-associated caQTL variants and 326 unreported candidate caQTLs [30] (Fig. 6B).

  To further characterize the biological relevance of the consensus-predicted caQTLs, we performed functional enrichment analysis of 214 genes associated with these variants. These genes showed significant enrichment in the pathway of cholesterol metabolism (FDR = 5.32 × 10−9) (Fig. S11). Phenotype enrichment further showed strong associations with atherosclerosis (FDR = 8.57 × 10−9), arteriosclerosis (FDR = 2.14 × 10−8), and coronary artery atherosclerosis (FDR = 7.42 × 10−5) (Fig. S12). These enrichments provide independent biological support that the genes associated with the consensus-predicted caQTLs are functionally related to cardiovascular and lipid-related processes relevant to CAD.

  Next, we examined allele-specific DNase-seq signal differences as an additional functional assessment of the consensus-predicted candidates. The consensus-predicted caQTLs exhibited significantly greater differences in DNase-seq signal between reference and alternative alleles compared with non-caQTL variants (Fig. 6C). For example, the known CAD-associated variant rs11670056 exhibited a pronounced difference in DNase-seq signal between reference and alternative alleles, accompanied by strong DNase-seq signal enrichment in the surrounding genomic region (Fig. 6D). In contrast, representative non-caQTL rs33998987 variants showed minimal allelic differences and lacked clear local DNase-seq signal enrichment (Fig. 6E). Notably, rs11670056 is located within the ELL gene, which encodes a component of the transcription elongation factor complex and has previously been associated with coronary artery disease and blood-related traits [37–39]. The big allele-specific differences in DNase-seq signal observed at this locus further support a potential regulatory role of rs11670056 in modulating chromatin accessibility regulation and downstream coronary artery disease risk.

  Together, the concordance between AlphaGenome and Borzoi provides a consensus-based prioritization strategy for identifying candidate regulatory variants associated with CAD, while further evidence from fine-mapping, colocalization, or orthogonal functional assays will be required to establish their causal roles.


## Discussion
  In this study, we performed a systematic evaluation of representative eQTL prediction frameworks across a broad spectrum of xQTLs, providing a unified assessment of their generalizability beyond gene expression traits. While previous studies have primarily focused on model performance within gene expression regulatory [40–42], our cross-xQTL benchmarking reveals substantial variability in predictive transferability, indicating that distinct molecular phenotypes are associated with different levels of regulatory complexity and varying degrees of learnability from genomic and epigenomic features.

  Consistent with prior work [20, 21, 25], models capable of capturing long-range regulatory interactions—such as Enformer, Borzoi, and AlphaGenome—demonstrated superior performance in eQTL prediction. Importantly, our analyses show that these architectures generalize more effectively across multiple xQTL classes. This improved generalizability may partly reflect their ability to capture long-range regulatory dependencies, differences in model architectures, and training objectives. Besides, pretraining datasets may also contribute to the observed performance. In contrast, models relying on local sequence features, including DeepSEA and ExPecto, exhibited substantially lower cross-task transferability across regulatory layers. Interestingly, despite relying only on manually curated annotations, GWAVA consistently outperformed DeepSEA and ExPecto in various xQTL predictions, emphasizing that biologically informed annotations remain highly informative for regulatory variant prioritization. Another potential limitation is that the six models generate regulatory representations with different feature dimensionalities, which may contribute to differences in downstream classification performance. These feature representations are intrinsically different in their biological content and were retained as generated by the original models to preserve their intended regulatory information. Therefore, differences in feature dimensionality may represent a potential confounding factor when comparing model performance.

  Prediction performance varied substantially across xQTL types. caQTLs, bQTLs, and hQTLs were predicted with consistently high accuracy across nearly all models, whereas clQTLs and miQTLs remained challenging. This pattern likely reflects differences in how directly these molecular phenotypes relate to chromatin features represented in current training datasets, including DNase accessibility and histone modification signals. Supporting this interpretation, all models exhibited strong enrichment of DNase-associated features in caQTL prediction. In contrast, the relatively poor performance for clQTLs and miQTLs suggests that their underlying regulatory mechanisms may involve more context-specific or indirect biological processes that are insufficiently represented in existing datasets and model architectures. Intermediate performance was observed for apaQTLs, pQTLs, and sQTLs, foundation models maintained a consistent advantage, although overall performance was lower than that observed for caQTLs. Notably, AlphaGenome-derived features showed enrichment for splice junction-related features in sQTL prediction, potentially explaining its improved ability to capture variants influencing RNA splicing. Moreover, predicted probabilities were positively correlated with effect sizes across several xQTLs, suggesting that models can partially capture biologically meaningful quantitative information beyond simple binary classification. In addition, the degree of tissue and cell-type correspondence between the model pretraining data and the xQTL datasets may influence model performance. Previous studies have suggested that sequence-based regulatory models may perform better when the training or predicted regulatory context more closely matches the target tissue or cell type, whereas performance may be reduced in highly cell-type-specific regulatory regions [43].

  Our analyses also demonstrated that downstream classifier selection substantially influenced predictive performance. Across nearly all xQTLs, tree-based ensemble methods, particularly XGBoost, consistently outperformed linear models such as L1 and Enet. This difference in performance gap was especially evident for DeepSEA-, ExPecto-, and Enformer-derived features, where linear models often showed near-random performance. These findings suggest that the regulatory representations learned by sequence models contain substantial nonlinear interactions that cannot be adequately captured by linear classifiers.

  In addition, increasing class imbalance consistently reduced AUPRC, MCC, and F1 scores across nearly all xQTLs, while AUROC remained relatively stable. This result indicates that AUROC may overestimate predictive utility under realistic genomic settings where functional variants are sparse. Although the models largely retained their ability to rank positive variants above negatives, their capacity to accurately identify true functional variants under threshold-dependent classification settings deteriorated as class imbalance increased. Notably, transformer-based models such as Borzoi and AlphaGenome demonstrated improved robustness under imbalanced conditions. This improved robustness likely reflects their ability to capture long-range regulatory dependencies and the broader genomic context, thereby learning more transferable and biologically meaningful regulatory representations than models that primarily rely on local sequence features.

  Furthermore, we demonstrated the practical utility of genomic foundation model-based xQTL prediction for disease-associated variant prioritization. By applying AlphaGenome and Borzoi to CAD-associated GWAS loci, we identified hundreds of candidate CAD-associated caQTLs, including both previously reported regulatory variants and unreported candidate loci [30]. Functional enrichment analysis of genes associated with these candidates showed significant enrichment in cholesterol metabolism and cardiovascular disease-related phenotypes, including atherosclerosis and coronary artery atherosclerosis, providing orthogonal biological support for their potential relevance to CAD. In addition, these predicted caQTLs exhibited significantly stronger allele-specific differences in DNase-seq signals than non-caQTL variants, supporting the biological relevance of the inferred chromatin perturbations. These findings highlight the potential of genomic foundation model-based xQTL prediction to facilitate functional interpretation of noncoding GWAS loci and improve prioritization of candidate regulatory variants contributing to complex diseases.

  Together, these findings suggest that foundation models perform better in terms of variant prioritization in complex disease genomics. However, their practical application may be constrained by model sizes, computational requirements, and reliance on extensive pretraining datasets. As such, although foundation models offer improved predictive performance, their computational demands should also be considered when selecting models for large-scale or resource-constrained applications. In addition, the rapidly evolving landscape of genomic foundation models is also an important consideration when interpreting the scope of our benchmark. Although our study includes AlphaGenome, a recently developed large-scale sequence-to-function foundation model, several emerging genomic language models, including Caduceus [44], HyenaDNA [45], and Nucleotide Transformer [23], were not evaluated. These models primarily learn general-purpose DNA sequence representations through large-scale pretraining and can subsequently be adapted to diverse downstream tasks, representing a different modeling paradigm from the regulatory sequence-to-function models evaluated here. Future extensions should incorporate these emerging models to determine whether our conclusions generalize across different genomic modeling paradigms.


## Conclusions
  In this work, we provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the strengths and limitations of current regulatory variant prediction models across diverse molecular phenotypes. The substantial variability in transferability across xQTL classes further emphasizes the need for next-generation models that integrate long-range sequence context with cell-type-specific and multi-omics regulatory information. Such approaches will be essential for the mechanistic interpretation of noncoding genetic variation and advancing the identification of functional regulatory variants associated with human disease.
