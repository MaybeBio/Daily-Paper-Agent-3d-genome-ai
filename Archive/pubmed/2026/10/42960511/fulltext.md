# Data analysis of experimentally solved nucleosome arrays reveals heterogeneous features


## Abstract
  Atomic-resolution nucleosome array structures have provided invaluable information on eukaryotic genome packing. Although these arrays are often obtained for regular fibers with designed sequences, such information complements in vivo genome organization data and modeling studies. Here, we analyze such systems with a focus on chromatin organization. We highlight both robust and unique features, as well as suitable average values for coarse-grained modeling, such as the relative orientation and positioning of neighboring nucleosomes, and opening of the linker DNA. Our associated web resource (https://www.biomath.nyu.edu/chromstruct/) shares these collected systems and provides tools for their analysis. The heterogeneous features exhibit a wide range of geometrical properties that depend on linker DNA length, core histone protein variants, auxiliary protein binding, and fiber interdigitation. Yet experimental fiber geometries can be reproduced reasonably using average parameters, as demonstrated here by simulations with our mesoscale model for regular fibers and as reported separately for life-like systems with epigenetic marks. Such analysis may also be useful for modular design of chromatin fibers. The structural characterization of these chromatin building blocks provides new insights into the regulation of chromatin architecture and has implications for the modular design of chromatin fibers.


## Introduction
  The eukaryotic genome is hierarchically and modularly organized to pack ∼2 meters of DNA inside the micrometer sized nucleus, thereby tightly controlling gene expression [1] (Fig. 1). At the largest scale of organization, chromosomes occupy specific regions in the nucleus where they self-interact in a non-random way, forming chromosome territories [2]. At the Mb scale, compartments A and B segregate euchromatin from heterochromatin through phase separation [3, 4]. Inside compartments, DNA organizes into topologically associated domains (TADs) that preferentially interact with each other and are actively formed by the loop extrusion mechanism directed by cohesin and CTCF [3, 5, 6].

  Although at a large scale chromatin organization appears irregular and dynamic [8, 9], on the small nucleosome or sub-kb level, many structural features have also been discovered by experiments and modeling. For example, nucleosomes assemble to form “clutches” [10], whose size and compaction are regulated by the cell developmental stage [11] and epigenetic marks [12]. Interesting features on the sub-nucleosome level have also been suggested. For example, tetranucleosome [13] and trinucleosome conformations [14]. Clearly, chromatin organization begins at the level of small modular units.

  The growing increase in resolution reached by Cryo-EM and X-ray crystallography techniques has allowed researchers to obtain structural information of chromatin arrays at unprecedented levels. Arrays of up to 12 nucleosomes with different linker DNA lengths, auxiliary proteins, and core histone protein variants are now available [15], providing a wealth of information for detailed structural insight.

  Physics-based models of chromatin fibers at nucleosome resolution use structural information from experiments to derive model parameters [16–19]. For example, the radius and width of wound DNA supercoil (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r_0$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\omega _0$\end{document}), and the angle between the entering and exiting points for linker DNA in the nucleosome (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta _0$\end{document}) are defined based on nucleosome crystal structures [20, 21]. Similarly, the DNA on the surface of the nucleosome core can be parametrized based on the bp parameters of nucleosome crystal structures [17]. The inclination angle defining nucleosome rotation around the dyad axis, can be defined based on electric dichroism experiments [22]. The entry–exit DNA geometry has also been parametrized using the crystal structure of a tetranucleosome array [23].

  Crystal structures of nucleosome core particles have been reviewed [24] and recently analyzed [25]. Depending on their composition, nucleosomes display a heterogeneous range of geometric features. Different ways of DNA unwinding and modes of DNA supercoiling are also observed. Experimental structures of stacked nucleosomes without linker DNA connections [26] revealed that a head-to-tail orientation and a shift of 36–44 Å are common features of nucleosome stacking. A recent database of nucleosomes and polynucleosomes also allows analysis of the relative twist and local geometry of DNA, core histone protein geometry, and DNA–protein contacts [25].

  Although these in vitro structures do not represent in vivo conditions due to the regularity of the fibers combined with designed DNA sequences and absence of epigenetic factors, they form valuable references for analysis of in vivo studies and provide robust averages for modeling chromatin systems composed of repeating units. Thus, computational modeling of life-like chromatin arrays with parameters derived from these reference models remains essential for providing detailed biophysical mechanisms underlying chromatin architecture and regulation, just like simulating biomolecules relies on force fields derived from small model systems of protein and nucleic acid constituents.

  As far as we know, a comprehensive analysis of atomic-level chromatin units has not yet been reported. Here, we evaluate structural properties of experimentally solved nucleosome arrays of size 2 to 12 nucleosomes (Fig. 2 and Table 1) to gain insight into chromatin organization extending to the kb level. In particular, we seek to identify unique and robust features that are useful for molecular design, nanotechnology applications, and coarse grained modeling.

  The paper is organized as follows:

  From the experimental nucleosome array analysis, we find that although geometrical properties adopt some preferred values, wide variations are found for the organization of the nucleosomes with respect to one another, and for the location of the linker DNA. These variations depend on the linker DNA length, core histone protein variants, auxiliary protein binding, and fiber interdigitation. This type of analysis on chromatin structures proves useful for defining parameters for coarse-grained chromatin models.

  From the mesoscale modeling [40–42] of 16-nucleosome arrays, we find that, by using reasonable average values extracted from experiments, fibers can be successfully simulated at nucleosome resolution, especially at the 5 bp resolution; geometrical parameters approximate those of experimental arrays. Thus, despite wide variations in the structural properties of small nucleosome arrays, the overall fiber architecture can be captured by using average features.

  Our construction of chromatin fibers using small nucleosome arrays as building blocks shows that dinucleosomes and one trinucleosome with long linkers can be used easily as modular units for constructing larger arrays. Routine combinations of longer modular systems, such as tri, tetra, or hexanucleosomes produce clashes of nucleosomes or linker DNA. These results have implications for DNA engineering nanotechnology.

  Finally, because the above analyses are performed for uniform fibers, we discuss and illustrate how incorporating heterogeneity, as found in vivo, is crucial for dictating genome folding. Still, we emphasize that reference fibers are useful for identifying regulating factors and deriving parameters for coarse-grained models, as well as for understanding chromatin organization at the sub-kb level.

These structures were simulated with both of our linker DNA models, the ∼9 bp [20] and the 5 bp [42] resolution models, and with and without 1 LH per nucleosome.

  Our chromatin mesoscale model treats nucleosomes as disks with 300 charges distributed on their irregular surface and calculated with the DiSCO algorithm [46, 47]. Linker DNA is treated with a combined bead and wormlike-chain model where each bead intersegment can represent ∼9 or 5 bp [20, 42, 48]. N-terminal histone tails of H3, H4, H2A, and H2B core proteins, plus the C-terminal tail of H2A, are modeled with beads composed of five residues where the charges on each bead are equal to the sum of the charges of the residues that create the bead [49, 50]. The globular head and C-terminal domain of the linker histone (LH) protein are similarly described with beads composed of five residues, but each bead charge is calculated in this case with our DiSCO algorithm [51].

## Results
  As described in our web resource (https://www.biomath.nyu.edu/chromstruct/) and in Table 1 and Fig. 2, the experimental library of nucleosome arrays contains systems from 2 to 12 nucleosomes.

  Fourteen different dinucleosome arrays are included. Two arrays do not have auxiliary proteins bound (1ZBB and 6M3V), nine have one or two LHs bound (7XX6, 7XVL, 7COW, 6LA8, 6L9Z, 6LAB, 7XX5, 7XVM, and 8YTI), and three of them have auxiliary proteins bound: repair protein complex PARP2–HPF1 (6X0N), chromatin remodeler complex ISW1a (7X3T), and histone deacetylase complex Rpd3S (8JHO).

  Among the six trinucleosome arrays, four have LH bound and differ by the length of the linker DNA (7PEU, 7PF0, 7PFA, and 7PFT). The other two do not contain LH and differ by the presence of CENP-A versus the canonical H3 core histone protein (6L49 and 6L4A).

  Among the five tetranucleosome arrays, two have LH bound (7PET and 6LA2), two are telomeric arrays with very short linker DNA where one of the arrays is open (7VA4) and one is closed (7V9K), and one (5OY7) has short linker DNA and is arranged in an open configuration.

  The hexanucleosome array (6HKT) has LHs bound, but these are not solved in the crystal structure. The octanucleosome array (8H1) and the dodecanucleosome array (8XJV) both have LH.

  In Table 1, we specify the DNA sequences and origin of the recombinant core histone proteins used to construct the arrays. Most of the arrays use DNA repeats of the 601 Widom sequence and recombinant human or Xenopus laevis core and LHs. Some of the dinucleosomes were generated using “sticky” DNA segments that pair with each other to create the linker DNA.


### Geometry between neighboring and next-neighbor nucleosomes i, i+1, and i, i+2

### Linker DNA geometry
  A major determinant of chromatin architecture is the linker DNA length, as demonstrated by modeling [61–63], in vitro [64, 65], and in vivo studies [66–69]. It can alter the relative rotational position of consecutive nucleosomes and has a profound effect on chromatin architecture, altering the accessibility to the genetic material [18, 22, 42, 70–78]. Here, we analyze the relationship between linker DNA length and the linker entry/exit α and β angles, the distance between both linkers, and the distance, dyad–dyad angle, and plane–plane angle between nucleosomes i and i+1.

  We first examine the opening of the entry/exit linker DNAs by measuring the α and β angles as defined above and in Fig. 3. Recall that α is the angle between each linker DNA and the nucleosome plane and β is the angle between each linker DNA and the dyad axis. The α angle tends to slightly decrease with linker DNA length (Fig. 5A). This could be because longer linkers allow higher DNA bending, whereas short linkers are more straight, as we have shown previously [61]. Similarly, the β angle also tends to decrease with linker DNA length (Fig. 5B).

  As linker DNA length increases, the distance between linkers slightly decreases (Fig. 5C), and the trend follows an asymptotic behavior, with the distance converging to ∼4 nm once the linker DNA length reaches 40 bp.

  An outlier appears in the trinucleosome 6L49. In this array (Fig. 2), the central nucleosome orients perpendicular to the other two nucleosomes, creating a very open linker DNA without crossing. This is due to the presence of the centromeric core histone protein H3 variant CENP-A, which creates an untwisted chromatin architecture [33]. These results highlight the epigenetic regulation of chromatin architecture by core histone protein variants.

  The comparison between the trinucleosome arrays 7PEU and 6L4A (linker DNAs of 30 and 22 bp) shows the compacting effect of LH; 7PEU with LH bound has shorter linker–linker distance, and in agreement, a shorter distance and rise between the stacking nucleosomes.

  Figure 5D shows, as expected, a linear relationship between the distance of nucleosomes i and i+1 and the linker DNA length. Fitting the data to a linear regression provides a slope of 0.28 nm/bp, close to the expected value of 0.34 nm/bp. As outlier appears the dinucleosome 6LA8 that has an open zigzag conformation and has the dyad axes oriented in a distinctive way where interdigitation is favored [27]. A more bent linker DNA results, reducing the distance between nucleosomes i and i+1.

  Figure 5E shows the relationship between the dyad–dyad angle formed by nucleosomes i and i+1 versus the linker DNA length. Interestingly, we see that this property does not significantly depend on the linker DNA length. ### Robust geometrical features of nucleosome arrays are captured by mesoscale modeling
  Given these heterogeneous structural variations, the following question arises: can average values of geometric parameters be used to capture global chromatin architecture? We analyze simulations of a 16-nucleosome array with various linker DNA lengths and with and without 1 LH per nucleosome [42] in light of the experimentally determined nucleosome systems.

  Our mesoscale nucleosome resolution model uses average parameter values listed in Table 2 as detailed in [50]. In particular, average parameters from elastic models of DNA are used to describe the linker DNA, but the linker DNA entering and exiting positions in the nucleosome are fixed based on the nucleosome crystal structure (see blue beads in Fig. 3B). Similarly, the initial geometries (nucleosome–nucleosome distances, rise, shift, and nucleosome–nucleosome plane angles) for ideal solenoid and zigzag fibers for the simulations are defined based on experimental structures. Thus, here we explore how well this set up captures the geometries of the experimental arrays.

  Figure 6A shows the distance between nucleosomes i and i+2 as a function of linker DNA length for systems simulated with the ∼9 and 5 bp resolution linker DNA model, and with and without 1 LH per nucleosome. For the systems without LH, this distance increases with linker DNA length, from around 10 to 20 nm. On the other hand, the distance remains mostly constant when LH is bound due to its strong compacting effect when linker DNAs are long [61, 79]. The results obtained with both methods are consistent. When comparing these values with the experimental data reported in Fig. 4B, we see that the predicted distances from modeling are slightly larger, as most experimental structures show distances 7 nm. The experimental structures are mostly compact zigzag fibers (Fig. 2) that resemble the modeled fibers of up to∼40 bp (Fig. 6G), especially those fibers with 10n linkers, like the 30 bp fiber. For these modeled systems, distances between nucleosomes i and i+2 are around 10 nm or less, similar to the experimental values.

  Next, we calculate the distance between nucleosomes i and i+1 as a function of linker DNA length (Fig. 6B). As expected, and similar to the trend seen for the experimental data (Fig. 5D), this distance increases linearly with linker DNA length, and the increase is slightly larger without LH. The linear fit of the data provides slopes of 0.24 and 0.22 nm/bp (black and blue lines) for the ∼9 bp resolution model without and with LH, and slopes of 0.27 and 0.26 nm/bp (red and green lines) for the 5 bp resolution model without and with LH. These results are in close agreement with the linear fit of the experimental data (Fig. 5D) that gives a slope of 0.28 nm/bp, remarkably similar to the slopes obtained with the 5 bp resolution model.

  Figure 6C shows the angle between the planes of nucleosomes i and i+1. Similarly, both models produce consistent results, with the 9 bp model yielding larger angles. The 5 bp resolution model without LH additionally shows periodicity for short linkers of up to 35 bp with 10n bp linkers smaller than those for 10n+5 bp linkers (i.e., 20 bp linkers produce smaller values than 15 and 25 bp linkers). Such periodicity was also observed in our previous work [42]. This is because the 5 bp resolution model captures the rotational setting of the nucleosomes induced by linker DNA lengths that are multiples or not of the DNA helical repeat (10 bp). This model also shows higher sensitivity to LH binding than the 9 bp model; larger differences in angle values are found and the periodicity is lost upon LH binding. When comparing with the experimental values, we see that our model produces average plane–plane angles that are slightly larger.   Overall, these results show that, although structural details might vary among different experimental nucleosome arrays, the chromatin fiber adopts an overall structure that can be captured by mesoscale models that use average parameters in the energy functions and in the description of the fiber geometry. Although average parameters resemble, in general, experimental arrays, specific parameters in coarse-grained models can be further adapted to reproduce a specific folding observed in the experimental arrays. For example, linker DNA parameters could be modified to reproduce the untwisting seen in the array containing CENP-A (6L49), similar to our modeling of the bending effect of FOXA on linker DNA [80].


### Building chromatin fibers from modular units
  Can these experimental data of small arrays be used to design larger chromatin fibers? Small repeating chromatin structural motifs could serve as regulators of genome function [81]. For example, dinucleosomes are the preferred substrate of chromatin regulator enzymes like HP1 and PRC [82, 83] and appear to be the basic repeating unit of tetranucleosome arrays [36]. “V”-shaped trinucleosomes have been observed in cryo-electron tomography images of human heterochromatin [14]. Similarly, tetranucleosome structural units contribute to the formation of a dodecanucleosomal 30-nm fiber [38]; they also constitute a basic secondary structural motif [13, 84] whose stability can be regulated by chromatin remodelers [85].

  To explore this question, we construct nucleosome arrays of 11–13 nucleosomes by combining multiple units of dinucleosome (6LA8, 6M3V, and 6X0N), trinucleosome (7PEU and 7PFT), tetranucleosome (7PET and 5OY7), or hexanucleosome (6HKT) arrays (see Materials and methods for details and Fig. 7). Specifically, we overlap the terminal nucleosome of unit i with the first nucleosome of unit i+1. Although this is one way to concatenate units, other ways might be possible, for example, by overlapping only the nucleosomal DNA and not the core histones proteins or alignment of central nucleosome rather than last. The constructed arrays are then simulated with our nucleosome resolution mesoscale model and MC sampling approach.

  As shown in Fig. 7, the dinucleosomes 6LA8 and 6M3V and the trinucleosome 7PFT can be used as building blocks as they produce reasonable structures for the ∼12 nucleosome chromatin arrays. While the dinucleosomes with linker DNA 27 and 31 bp produce zigzag ladder-like structures (Fig. 8), the trinucleosome 7PFT with longer linker DNA (60 bp) produces a more heterogeneous and globular structure (Fig. 8), which agrees with previous findings on the role of linker DNA length on global fiber folding [61–63].

  The systems created with the dinucleosmes 6LA8 and 6M3V retain the original structure in terms of i, i+1 distance. However, for the 7PFT construct, the average i, i+1 and i, i+2 distances increase significantly compared to the crystal structure; around 10 nm. This results from the 60 bp linker DNA that creates a very heterogeneous folding in the construct after the MC simulation.

  The other experimental arrays tested by our simple fiber building procedure produce large clashes between nucleosomes and DNA and fiber configurations are not feasible (Figs 7 and 8). These building block motifs will need to be separated by other chromatin segments to prevent steric clashes. Alternatively, topoisomerases or chromatin remodelers could help reduce steric clashes and favor a more relaxed fiber topology [86, 87].

  Similar results were obtained by an MD study that constructed pentanucleosome arrays from trinucleosome units of various linker DNA lengths by superimposing nucleosome 1 of the second unit onto nucleosome 3 of the first unit [73]. There, the authors show that many structures lead to steric collisions, especially when linker DNA is short, around 20 bp. However, other structures lead to reasonable fibers. Note that the constructed pentanucleosome arrays in [73] are smaller than our ∼12 nucleosome arrays and are sampled with MD simulations, whereas we use MC sampling.


## Summary and discussion
  The intricate relationship between chromatin structure and function is a fascinating area of research. Many efforts have been made by experimental and modeling groups to elucidate the regulation of chromatin architecture at the kb level.

  Here, we have analyzed atomic-resolution structures of nucleosome arrays solved experimentally (Fig. 2 and Table 1) and created an interactive web resource for their analysis by external users (https://www.biomath.nyu.edu/chromstruct/). We compared the experimental structures with chromatin fibers simulated with our nucleosome-resolution mesoscale model [40, 41] using average parameters, and used them as building blocks to explore the creation of larger chromatin arrays. We also analyzed these results in the context of more heterogeneous systems, as found in vivo.

  We found that interactions between first and second neighboring nucleosomes are affected by the linker DNA length; interdigitation with another chromatin fiber; and the type of stacking interactions (i.e., type I with tight stacks between H2A–H2B dimers, or type II with offset and more open stacks [38, 37]) (Fig. 4).

  In particular, short linker DNA creates an open structure in the tetranucleosome array 5OY7, but compact structures in the telomeric tetranucleosome arrays 7V9K and 7VA4 [35], similar to what is found in heterochromatin of immature retina cells [88].

  The effect of fiber interdigitation has been explored by molecular modeling [89], showing that in the presence of multiple fibers, nucleosomes establish multivalent interactions instead of i, i+2 interactions, deviating the structure from the typical 30-nm fiber. Here we see that effect in the dinucleosome array 6LA8 where the two nucleosomes orient with a small dyad–dyad angle.

  Type I and II interactions have been observed in Cryo-EM and Cryo-ET structures of reconstituted tetranucleosome arrays [38] and native chromatin fibers [90]. These structures have emphasized that native chromatin organizes with nucleosomes connected by a flexible and relaxed zigzag topology rather than straight linker DNA in a regular 30 nm fiber. The presence of epigenetic marks, auxiliary protein binding, and core histone protein variants generates deviations from average typical values.

  The relationship between linker DNA and other geometrical properties has been well studied (e.g., [17, 22, 42, 61, 70, 78]). Here we find that longer linkers favor the crossing of the two linker DNAs (Fig. 5), which is reflected by a small reduction of the α and β angles and the distance between linker DNAs. This agrees with previous findings showing that linker DNAs need to be long enough, around 40 bp, to allow crossover, usually induced by LH binding [15, 61, 62].

  The plot of neighboring internucleosome distance versus linker DNA length (Fig. 5) shows the expected trend of increase with linker DNA length, emphasizing a shared basic folding. The structures that deviate have distinct components that make them different from the typical 30 nm fiber, such as core histone protein variants or very short linker DNAs.

  The increase of plane–plane angles as linker DNA length decreases for the trinucleosome arrays 7PFT, 7PFA, 7PF0, and 7PEU has interesting implications. There is a reorientation of the central nucleosome (Fig. 2) as linker DNA length decreases, even if the DNA length is in the 10n bp family. Thus, changes in nucleosome rotational position are not only a product of changing the number of DNA helical repeats, but also produced by changes in linker DNA length per se. The longer linker reduces the stress in the DNA and in the twist energy, allowing the nucleosome to reorient [74].

  Our simulated fibers of 16 nucleosomes with increasing linker DNA length (Fig. 6) show overall consistent and similar results to the experimental values. For i, i+1 distance versus linker DNA, the 5 bp resolution model approximates better to the experimental trend, as expected [42, 70, 74]. The best agreement with experimental data is found for fibers with short and 10n bp linker DNA, like the 20 or 30 bp fibers. Once linkers become longer, the fibers adopt a more heterogeneous configuration with more long range interactions [62]; these are not captured in the experimental structures due to the short size of the arrays.

  The current view of chromatin organization in vivo highlights heterogeneity, irregularity, plasticity, and dynamics, challenging the regular 30-nm view [1, 8, 9, 91]. Although the experimentally solved nucleosome arrays studied here have uniform linkers and lack epigenetic marks, they allow us to understand how chromatin architecture is organized at a small sub-kb scale and regulated by linker DNA length, auxiliary protein binding, and core histone protein variants.

  Many groups have investigated how introducing heterogeneity and in vivo-like factors into chromatin models affect fiber folding compared to regular fibers (see, for example, [92–96]). As we show in Fig. 9 with studies from our group, variations in linker DNA length, LH binding, and epigenetic marks are essential for shaping chromatin architecture and contributing to many genomic and biological processes such gene folding and repression, cell differentiation, and disease development.

  In particular, we first showed how irregular linker DNAs, or “life-like” distributions, produce more polymorphic, flexible fibers, as well as encourage kb-range contacts via hierarchical looping, explaining the diversity of chromatin folding observed in experiments [62, 63] (Fig. 9A). Modeling LHs at various densities also revealed how its binding mode (symmetric or asymmetric), density, and dynamic binding features, as found in vivo, regulate fiber architecture and compaction, promote the formation of heteromorphic nucleosome clusters, and control fiber unfolding through hierarchical looping mechanism [79, 97–100]. Together these epigenetic factors (LH density, linker length, core histone tail modifications, and transcription factor binding) direct gene folding and looping in kb-range fibers [101–103]. We found that the threshold concentration of LH needed to trigger a transition into an ordered 30 nm fiber depends on the linker DNA length and that a critical value is around 0.5 LH/nucleosome [79, 101]. The interaction between tails and linker DNA also depends on both the DNA length and LH density [101]. The effect of LH on fiber folding and compaction is regulated by core histone tail acetylation; binding of LH to non acetylated regions produces hierarchical looping and segregation of acetylated from wild-type regions [102].

  These findings make clear that accounting for fiber heterogeneity is essential for structural and mechanistic capturing of gene folding found in vivo. This includes incorporating genomic contacts, nucleosome positions, LH and transcription factor binding locations, and core histone tail acetylation and methylation locations. Using such heterogeneous settings, the folding of several genes like GATA4 [104], HOXC [105] (Fig. 9B), Pou5f1 [11], Eed [103] (Fig. 9C), and HTT [106] (Fig. 9D), as well as the X-chromosome inactivation center [107] (Fig. 9E) were successfully studied.

  Our most recent study of in vivo chromatin used experimental Cryo-ET images to define linker DNA lengths in chromatin models of immature and mature retina cells (Fig. 9F) [88]. These simulations clearly show how heterogeneous linker DNA length is, spanning from ∼0 to 100 bp in a small kb genomic region, and how such variations regulate chromatin higher-order and dictate cell maturation. Although conversion of Cryo-ET images into 3D parameters needed for chromatin modeling requires specialized programs, such general utilities can be developed in the future. See references [108–110] for recent efforts.

  Thus, overall, using a parametrized nucleosome-resolution model of polynucleosome beads on atomic-level experimental data incorporated in a heterogeneous setting can provide insights into how epigenetic factors and specific nucleosome positioning regulate many processes such as cell differentiation, gene folding, gene repression, chromosome inactivation, and heterochromatin formation. These features can be obtained for nucleosome-resolution models parametrized based on these experimental systems.

  Overall, our work summarizes the most typical structural parameters adopted by small chromatin arrays (Figs 4 and 5) and how these are modulated by several factors like linker DNA length, core histone protein variants, auxiliary protein binding, and fiber interdigitation. This information serves as reference for coarse-grained modeling.

  The motifs we analyzed can also be used as modular building blocks (Figs 7 and 8) for nucleic acid design, with care in assembly to ensure geometric fit and avoidance of steric incompatibilities. These ideas could be relevant for the emerging field of chromatin engineering [111] to create more compact or open chromatin segments to regulate gene expression. Artificial intelligence tools should be able to construct reliable and physically meaningful computational systems more easily. Already, promising chromatin-based therapies employ “writer” and “eraser” proteins for directing epigenetic modifications to specific genomic regions [112]; in this way, local chromatin compaction can be regulated for exciting biomolecular applications.
